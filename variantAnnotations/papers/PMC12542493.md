## Abstract

**Introduction:** 
: Dabigatran is a direct oral anticoagulant associated with a high incidence of gastrointestinal bleeding, which presents a significant clinical concern. Genetic polymorphisms in the enzymes responsible for drug absorption (ABCB1) and activation (CES1) may influence dabigatran’s pharmacokinetics, potentially altering drug concentration and therapeutic response. The current systematic review and meta-analysis aim to identify genetic variants correlated with dabigatran exposure and evaluate their importance.

**Methods:** 
We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Embase to identify studies on dabigatran pharmacogenomics. The review included observational and clinical studies that met eligibility criteria. RevMan 5.4 was used to conduct the meta-analysis. Quality assessment was done using ROB 2.0 and NOS tools.

**Results:** 
Out of 1336 records retrieved, 1008 were screened, resulting in 16 studies included in the systematic review and 9 in the meta-analysis. Data from 3834 participants (61.8% males) were reviewed. For the ABCB1 polymorphism rs4148738, both CT and TT genotypes decreased C trough compared to CC genotype (MD = − 9.82, 95% CI [− 17.65, − 1.99], P = 0.01) and (MD= − 7.69, 95% CI [− 15.54, 0.16], P = 0.05), respectively. While CES1 rs8192935 GG increased C max compared to AA (MD = 22.66, 95% CI [5.04, 40.27], P = 0.01). For CES1 rs2244613 AA, both C max and C trough exhibited higher levels compared to GG (MD = 13.58, 95% CI [− 0.08,27.25], P = 0.05) and (MD = 13.41, 95% CI [8.05,18.77], P < 0.01), respectively. Also, compared to GG the heterozygote type GA increased the C max (MD = 32.02, 95%CI [16.54,47.5], P < 0.01). Bleeding risk did not significantly differ across ABCB1 rs1045642, ABCB1 rs4148738 and CES1 rs8192935 polymorphisms. Only CES1 rs2244613 T allele showed significant effect on bleeding (OR = 2.43, P = 0.002). Stroke incidence did not differ across ABCB1 rs4148738, CES1 rs2244613, and CES1 rs8192935 genotypes.

**Conclusion:** 
ABCB1 rs4148738 T allele reduced dabigatran trough levels, while CES1 rs8192935 GG increased peak levels. CES1 rs2244613 TT raised both peak and trough levels, and its T allele was linked to higher bleeding risk. No consistent associations were found for other variants. These findings highlight CES1 rs2244613 as a key contributor to variability in dabigatran response, warranting further large-scale studies to confirm its role in personalized anticoagulation therapy.

## Introduction

Oral anticoagulants are approved for prevention and treatment of thromboembolic events related to atrial fibrillation, venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, prosthetic heart valves, and stroke [[1](#CR1), [2](#CR2)]. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are non-vitamin K antagonists that directly inhibit specific coagulation factors, with dabigatran targeting factor IIa and rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, and betrixaban targeting factor Xa [[2](#CR2)]. These agents are favored over traditional vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) such as warfarin, phenprocoumon, and acenocoumarol due to their oral administration in fixed-dose regimens, eliminating the need for routine monitoring, availability of specific antidotes, and reduced interactions with food and drugs [[3](#CR3), [4](#CR4)]. Dabigatran is a prodrug, belonging to the DOACS class, whose safety has been investigated for use in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Dabigatran etexilate is absorbed in a small amount with the aid of P-glycoprotein (P-gp), which is an intestinal efflux transporter encoded by the ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 1 (*ABCB1*) gene [[5](#CR5)]. After absorption, CES1 converts dabigatran into its active metabolite in hepatocytes, which is mostly excreted by the kidneys in its inactive form [[6](#CR6)]. Studies reported that dabigatran had significant inter-individual variations in pharmacokinetics (PK), pharmacodynamics (PD), and the concentration of the active metabolite (dabigatran etexilate) [[6](#CR6)]. Compared to warfarin, dabigatran was found to be associated with a higher risk of major bleeding and gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB), but had a decreased risk of intracranial hemorrhage in patients with AF in a study by Hernandez et al. [[7](#CR7)]. However, in a study by Rosian et al., they had the same risk of bleeding [[8](#CR8)]. The reasons for this variability in AF patients receiving dabigatran were attributed to HAS-BLED score ≥ 3, and prior history of GIB within 1 year [[4](#CR4)], plasma concentration of dabigatran, and the concomitant use of aspirin in these patients [[5](#CR5)].

Other studies have linked the incidence of bleeding to genetic polymorphisms in *ABCB1* and *CES1* genes. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in *CES1* and *ABCB1* may contribute to inter-individual variability in dosing requirements, affecting the therapeutic efficacy and safety of dabigatran [[6](#CR6), [9](#CR9), [10](#CR10)]. The *CES1* gene is located on chromosome 16 and contains 14 exons; and more than 14,000 polymorphisms have been described for CES1 according to the dbSNP database [[11](#CR11)]. Many SNPs, such as rs2244613 (G >T), rs8192935 (G >A), and rs71647871 (G >A) have been associated with pharmacokinetic variations of dabigatran [[6](#CR6)]. For ABCB1, the most common polymorphisms are rs1128503 (1236 C >T), rs2032582 (2677 G >T), rs1045642 (3435 C >T), rs4148738 (intronic in the promoter, A >G), and the last two SNPs are associated with increased peak concentration of dabigatran [[11](#CR11)]. Understanding these genetic variations is crucial for tailoring individual treatment plans and ensuring effective and safer therapeutic regimens, especially in high-risk patients. The evidence supporting the genetic hypothesis is not consistent across literature [[5](#CR5)]. Additionally, the PharmGKB database had categorized the correlation between these SNPs; *ABCB1 rs1045642*,* ABCB1 rs4148738*,* ABCB1 rs2032582*,* CES1 rs2244613*, and *CES1 rs8192935* and dabigatran as level 3, indicating that there is a low level of evidence backing this association [[10](#CR10)]. If this genetic hypothesis is validated, routine genotyping for these polymorphisms may be considered before prescribing dabigatran. This will help in optimizing personalized dosing and reducing adverse events in high-risk patients, including those with renal or hepatic impairment, those undergoing surgery, and those at risk of drug-drug interactions [[12](#CR12)].

Due to the limited evidence exploring the incontrovertible influence of several sequence variants on pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes of dabigatran and the inconsistent findings, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to gather and evaluate the existing evidence about the effect of genetic polymorphisms on patients’ response to dabigatran, as well as their impact on dabigatran pharmacokinetics. This will help to consolidate evidence-based decisions related to pharmacogenetic tests and encourage the adoption of precision medicine.

## Methodology

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [[13](#CR13)] and the Cochrane Handbook for Conducting Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [[14](#CR14)], pre-specified steps were followed. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO under the following ID: CRD42025648675.

### Search strategy and study selection process

A comprehensive literature search was done on PubMed, Web of Science (WOS), Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Embase till 15th May 2025, using this search strategy: (”novel oral anticoagulant” OR “new oral anticoagulant” OR “direct oral anticoagulant” OR “target-specific oral anticoagulant” OR “Direct Factor Xa Inhibitors” OR “NOAC” OR “DOAC” OR “TSOAC” OR “dabigatran”) AND (“CES1” OR “carboxylesterase 1” OR “carboxylesterase-1” OR “ABCB1” OR “ATP Binding Cassette Transporter” OR “pharmacogenomics” OR “pharmacogenetics” OR “genotype” OR “polymorphism” OR “precision medicine” OR “variants”). No restrictions on publication year or language were applied during the search of databases. Search strategy is shown in supplementary table 1. ### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria:

Population: Patients treated with dabigatran.

Exposures: Homozygote and heterozygote ABCB1 and CES1 polymorphisms.

Comparator: The wild type of ABCB1 and CES1 polymorphisms.

Outcomes: Pharmacokinetic parameters, including C max and C trough. Pharmacodynamic parameters, including bleeding status, incidence of minor bleeding, major bleeding and thrombosis.

Study Design: Observational studies and randomized clinical studies were included. Review articles, editorials, conference proceedings, case reports, and case series were all excluded from this review. Duplicated records were identified and removed using EndNote software. The screening was performed in two phases. First, the title and abstract of every study were screened by multiple authors independently to ensure reliability, then the potentially included studies underwent full- text screening. The conflicts were resolved through discussion between authors. All studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria were included in the systematic review, but only those that provided sufficient quantitative data (e.g., odds ratios with confidence intervals, or raw data that allowed effect size calculation) were pooled in the meta-analysis.

### Data extraction

Data extraction sheets consisted of study characteristics, including study ID, study design, country, inclusion and exclusion criteria, study population, ethnicity, sample size, mean age, percentage of males, body mass index (BMI) (kg/m^2^), height (m), weight (kg), creatine kinase, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, dabigatran dose (mg), indication of dabigatran use, genotype detection method, minor allele frequency (MAF), SNP ID (gene), consistency with Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and concentration analysis method. We used two sheets for the outcomes; the first one for categorical outcomes was extracted as incidence rate and total number of participants. The second sheet includes continuous outcomes that were extracted as mean and standard deviation (SD) by two reviewers. To extract graphical data, we used plot digitizer tool.

### Allele harmonization and strand orientation strategy

In conducting this systematic review and meta-analysis, we observed inconsistencies in the reporting of alleles for several SNPs, particularly across studies involving diverse ethnic populations. These variances often stem from differences in strand orientation, reference genome usage, and population-specific allele frequencies. For example, the same SNP may be reported with different alleles (e.g., C vs. G or A vs. T) depending on whether the study refers to the coding or reverse strand, or uses population-specific data.

To ensure consistency and accuracy in allele interpretation across studies, we adopted a standardized approach: first, wild-type alleles were identified using the dbSNP database, which provides reference alleles based on the human genome assembly and serves as a consistent baseline across populations. Second, strand orientation was verified using SNPedia, which clearly indicates whether the SNP is located on the plus or minus strand. This allowed us to reconcile allele naming differences and avoid misclassification.

### Statistical analysis

RevMan 5.4. was used for the meta-analysis. Forest plots were generated to represent the pooled effect sizes using risk ratio (RR) for the dichotomous outcomes, and mean difference (MD) for the continuous outcomes, with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI). Random-effects model was used in case of significant heterogeneity. The I^2^ and chi-square (χ^2^) tests were used to detect and measure the heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was considered significant when the I^2^ exceeded 50%, and χ^2^ p-value was below 0.1.

Publication bias was explored by Egger’s formula and presented using funnel plots. Egger and colleagues stated that the threshold number of included studies should be more than or equal to 10 studies for their test to be accurate enough to be used [[15](#CR15), [16](#CR16)].

### Quality assessment

The Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (ROB 2.0) [[14](#CR14)] was used to assess the quality of clinical trials, whereas the Newcastle Ottawa scale (NOS) [[17](#CR17)] was used for observational studies.

The NOS tool consists of three components: selection, comparability, and outcome. The selection items, which had a maximum rating of 5 stars, assessed the representativeness of the sample group of the total population. The comparability items, which had two stars for grading, considered the confounders and matching of the groups’ characteristics for proper comparison between the study groups. The outcome component was evaluated using a maximum of three stars and determined the outcomes assessment (blinding, and measurement tools) and the statistical tests used to measure the association between exposure and outcomes. Studies with a NOS score of ≥ 5 stars out of 10 were considered of high quality. The rating of 3 to 4 stars was considered of moderate quality and 1 to 2 stars of low quality.

RoB2 tool includes five domains: randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The decision for each domain is low risk, some concerns, or high risk based on the answers to the leading questions in the RoB2 algorithms. Each included study was assessed by two authors and cross-checked their decisions. All discrepancies were resolved through discussion. RobVis visualization tool was used to generate the risk of bias figure and graph.

## Results

### Literature search

After a comprehensive literature search on five databases, 1336 records were retrieved. After removing 328 duplicated records, only 1008 underwent title and abstract screening. Twenty-nine records were found to be potentially eligible for full-text screening. All studies that met the predefined inclusion criteria were qualitatively reviewed. A total of 16 studies were eligible for the systematic review [[8](#CR8)–[12](#CR12), [18](#CR18)–[28](#CR28)]. However, only nine of these were included in the quantitative meta-analysis [[8](#CR8), [10](#CR10), [12](#CR12), [20](#CR20), [21](#CR21), [23](#CR23), [25](#CR25), [26](#CR26), [28](#CR28)]. The remaining seven studies, although meeting the general inclusion criteria, could not be quantitatively synthesized due to insufficient or non-extractable data (e.g., lack of effect estimates, incomplete statistical reporting, or data presented in a non-combinable format). Detailed reasons for exclusion from the meta-analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 3. The PRISMA flow chart is shown in Fig. [1](#Fig1).

### Baseline characteristics of patients in the included studies

Overall, 3834 participants were included in this systematic review, with males comprising 61.8% of the study population. Most studies were prospective observational (*n* = 8), while two were retrospective and four were randomized controlled trials. In addition, one study was a case-control and one was a genome-wide association study. The age of participants varied by study design: younger cohorts were observed in pharmacokinetic trials of healthy volunteers (Liu et al., Zubiaur et al., Gouin-Thibault; mean ages 20–40 years), whereas atrial fibrillation (AF) cohorts such as those by Paré, Ji, Zhu, Lähteenmäki, Cumitini, and Roșian had mean ages between 68 and 74 years. Body mass index (BMI) values, reported in most studies, ranged from 21 to 28 kg/m^2^, indicating that participants were generally in the normal-to-overweight range.

Ethnic composition also differed substantially: 10 studies were conducted in predominantly Caucasian populations from European countries, four studies were performed in Chinese populations, and one study was in Kazakh cohorts, while one Canadian trial included a mixed multi-ethnic population. The 16 included studies covered varied indications: eight on atrial fibrillation (including 1 with CKD stages), four on healthy participants, two on DOAC users, one on stroke, and one on knee replacement. This mix spans chronic and acute conditions, as well as controlled healthy-subject data, adding both breadth and heterogeneity.

The most commonly reported comorbidities in clinical cohorts were hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease. Dabigatran dosing also differed, with most clinical studies using 110–150 mg twice daily, while pharmacokinetic studies in healthy volunteers administered a single 150 mg dose. Such heterogeneity in study design, demographics, and ethnicity may have contributed to the variability in pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic outcomes observed in this review, as illustrated in Table [1](#Tab1).

### Quality assessment

All observational studies were of fair quality, except for Sychev et al. [[23](#CR23)], and Xie et al. [[18](#CR18)], which were of poor quality using the NOS tool. All four of the included RCTs had a low risk of bias, as assessed using the ROB2 tool. Liu et al. [[19](#CR19)], Zubiaur et al. [[22](#CR22)], and Thibault et al. [[11](#CR11)] exhibited some concerns in individual domains (notably in randomization and reporting), but their overall risk of bias was still categorized as low, indicating acceptable methodological quality across all studies. On the other hand, Paré et al. [[12](#CR12)] demonstrated low risk of bias across all domains, as shown in supplementary table S2 and Fig. [2](#Fig2).

### Meta-analysis of pharmacokinetic effects of polymorphisms on Dabigatran serum concentration

Nine records were included in this quantitative synthesis. Two approaches were used for the meta-analysis. Initially, we evaluated the effects of genetic variants on dabigatran C _max_ and C _trough_ concentration. In the second approach, we assessed the effects of each variant on ADRs (bleeding and thrombosis) associated with dabigatran.

## Discussion

Our meta-analysis evaluated the impact of genetic polymorphisms in ABCB1 (rs1045642, rs4148738, rs2032582) and CES1 (rs8192935, rs2244613) on dabigatran pharmacokinetics, bleeding risk, and stroke incidence.

Most polymorphisms in ABCB1 and CES1 did not significantly affect Dabigatran’s C _max_ or C _trough_ levels. However, three polymorphisms; ABCB1 rs4148738, CES1 rs8192935, and CES1 rs2244613 showed consistent associations with altered drug levels, highlighting their potential role in interindividual variability.

### ABCB1 polymorphisms

Among the ABCB1 variants, rs4148738 demonstrated the clearest effect, with CT and TT carriers showing reduced trough concentrations compared with CC homozygotes.

This association, not observed for rs1045642 and rs2032582, may reflect the unique functional role of rs4148738 as a deep intronic polymorphism potentially in linkage disequilibrium with regulatory variants affecting P-glycoprotein (P-gp) expression or splicing. Importantly, this reduction in trough concentration did not translate into significant differences in bleeding or stroke incidence, suggesting limited clinical impact at present [[22](#CR22), [26](#CR26), [27](#CR27)]. Our findings align with previous meta-analyses by Xie et al. [[30](#CR30)] showing inconsistent PK effects of ABCB1 variants across NOACs, while Xie et al. highlighted possible PK effects of ABCB1 variants across NOACs, our results did not confirm significant effects for rs1045642 or rs2032582 on dabigatran. Notably, both Xie’s results and our study agree that ABCB1 polymorphisms have little impact on bleeding outcomes.

### CES1 polymorphisms

In contrast, CES1 variants exerted more pronounced effects, rs2244613 emerged as the most clinically relevant polymorphism. The TT genotype was associated with increased peak and trough concentrations as well as higher bleeding risk, whereas the GT genotype increased trough concentrations only.

These findings confirm earlier evidence by Li et al. [[31](#CR31)] that identified TT carriers as a high-risk group, while our expanded analysis adds new insights by demonstrating its influence on both C max and C trough. Taken together, these results support the importance of rs2244613 in dabigatran pharmacogenetics, especially regarding bleeding complications, and underscore the need for monitoring trough concentrations in carriers of the TT genotype.

In addition, rs8192935 presented an interesting result. Our meta-analysis found that the homozygous GG genotype was associated with higher peak concentrations, suggesting a role in elevated dabigatran exposure. This contrasts with ClinPGx, which categorizes the association between rs8192935 and dabigatran as “Level 3” (low evidence), largely based on a single small cross-sectional study reporting an opposite trend. By synthesizing multiple independent cohorts, our analysis strengthens the evidence base and provides a more reliable estimate of effect. The discrepancy likely reflects differences in study design, sample size, and population characteristics [[32](#CR32), [33](#CR33)]. Additionally, although dabigatran etexilate is rapidly and completely converted into dabigatran by CES enzymes, reduced or altered CES1 activity due to polymorphisms does not appear to significantly affect plasma concentrations, possibly due to the drug’s metabolic flexibility: hepatic CES1 and intestinal CES2 initially convert the pro-drug into intermediate metabolites (M1 and M2), which are then transformed into dabigatran by CES2 and CES1, respectively. Thereby, this reciprocal pathway enables CES2 to compensate for any CES1 deficiency, helping to maintain stable drug exposure [[6](#CR6)].

Furthermore, we underscore the importance of population-specific approaches in pharmacogenomics. The universal widespread presence of *ABCB1 rs4148738* and *CES1 rs2244613* variants suggests a shared role and significance in drug metabolism across populations [[34](#CR34)]. Europeans and Caucasians (especially in Spain and Finland), and Asians (including Chinese, Han Chinese, and Kazakhs) show high SNP diversity, particularly in the *ABCB1* gene, with unique and rare variants indicating potential variability in drug response and population-specific genetic traits [[30](#CR30)]. Interestingly, Caucasians and White Europeans from Italy, the Czech Republic, Romania, and France had a narrower SNP range than other European cohorts, potentially due to small sample sizes or distinct genetic patterns [[35](#CR35), [36](#CR36)].

By systemically reviewing data from sixteen studies, the results aggregate and synthesize available evidence on the role of ABCB1 and CES1 SNPs on dabigatran PK/PD. This approach provides a broad overview of exciting knowledge and highlights key findings as they are the most mentioned in CPIC guidelines, and our results support and strengthen the guidelines regarding *ABCB1 rs4148738* and *CES1 rs8192935* and *rs2244613* [[37](#CR37)]. These insights suggest that routine genotyping is not currently warranted for all patients but highlight the potential value of targeted pharmacogenetic approaches in high-risk populations, particularly where trough monitoring is feasible.

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides an updated synthesis of 16 studies, including four recently published investigations, thereby expanding the available evidence base beyond earlier reviews. By integrating both PK and clinical outcomes, our analysis offers a comprehensive perspective on the role of pharmacogenetics in dabigatran therapy.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Only five studies were available for quantitative analysis, and some outcomes were supported by as few as two studies. Patient-level characteristics such as age, sex, comorbidities, and concomitant therapies may have influenced outcomes but were not uniformly reported. The number of Asian patients was limited compared with White cohorts, and other anticoagulation therapies were not assessed, potentially restricting generalizability. Finally, heterogeneity across studies remained high, reflecting differences in study design, sample size, and indications for dabigatran use.

## Conclusions

This study confirms that carriers of *ABCB1 rs4148738 CT* and *TT* genotypes have lower dabigatran trough concentrations compared with *CC* carriers, while *CES1 rs8192935 GG* is associated with increased peak concentrations. Moreover, *CES1 rs2244613 TT* was linked to higher peak and trough concentrations as well as an elevated bleeding risk, whereas the *GT* genotype was associated with increased trough levels only. These findings highlight the potential influence of pharmacogenetic variation on dabigatran exposure and safety. To validate and expand upon these results, future large-scale prospective studies across diverse ethnic populations are warranted.
