# Theratyping as a tool to guide clinical decision-making in patients with suspected cystic fibrosis and variants currently ineligible for CFTR modulator therapy

## Abstract

The combination of elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) is effective in people with cystic fibrosis (CF) with at least one F508del-CFTR allele; however, approximately 10% have variants without established ETI responsiveness and are ineligible for therapy. Additionally, diagnostic confirmation or exclusion of CF can be challenging in individuals with compatible symptoms, CFTR variants of unknown significance, and indeterminate sweat chloride or nasal potential difference (NPD) results. We implemented a minimally invasive method for “theratyping” in subjects with confirmed or clinically suspected CF and a CFTR variant currently ineligible for ETI therapy. We derived air-liquid interface epithelia from airway basal cells obtained via nasal brushings; we then assessed CFTR function at baseline and in response to ETI with Ussing chambers. We present six cases highlighting the value of our theratyping approach in guiding clinical decision-making. In two subjects, theratyping demonstrated substantial improvement in CFTR function and supported approval for ETI therapy, with subsequent clinical improvements in airway mechanics, weight maintenance, microbiology, and symptom burden. Conversely, theratyping identified two subjects with CF unlikely to respond to ETI, suggesting they may be candidates for future trials of new therapeutics. Finally, in two subjects with suspected CF, theratyping demonstrated normal-range CFTR function and decreased suspicion for CF. This work highlights our theratyping approach as a practical strategy for 1) predicting ETI response in people with CF and CFTR variant currently ineligible for ETI therapy and 2) aiding in the diagnostic confirmation or exclusion of CF in cases with inconclusive sweat chloride or NPD results.

## Introduction

The triple combination of elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) has been shown to be highly effective in patients 2 years and older with cystic fibrosis (CF) characterized genetically by at least one *F508del (c.1521_1523del; p.Phe508del)* allele or a variant that is responsive based on in vitro data ([1](#R1)–[4](#R4)). However, up to 10% of people with CF remain ineligible for CFTR modulating (including ETI) therapy because their variants have not yet been demonstrated to benefit from this treatment. Moreover, diagnostic confirmation of cystic fibrosis in people with compatible symptoms and *CFTR* variants of unknown significance can be challenging when assays such as sweat chloride or nasal potential difference (NPD) are borderline abnormal. We recently implemented a minimally invasive method for “theratyping” in subjects with confirmed or clinically suspected CF and a *CFTR* variant currently ineligible for ETI therapy. Our theratyping approach is based on obtaining airway epithelial basal cells from candidate subjects via nasal brushing, expansion of the basal cells in vitro, and differentiation into air-liquid interface epithelia. Epithelia are then exposed to ETI and *CFTR*-mediated anion transport is measured in Ussing Chambers. This approach can 1) help identify *CFTR* dysfunction as a cause for a patient’s disease and 2) predict whether patients will clinically benefit from ETI therapy. Here, we describe our methods and outcomes for 6 cases in which theratyping informed clinical decisions.

## Methods

This study was approved by the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board, IRB# 202102236. The subjects are patients in the adult or pediatric CF clinic at the University of Iowa CF Center; subjects were recruited at the request of their CF specialist clinician and included: patients with confirmed CF with CF-causing CFTR variants not approved for ETI treatment at the time of request, or for patients with clinically suspected CF and inconclusive confirmatory testing (sweat chloride or NPD assay and inconclusive genotype). Human nasal epithelial cells (HNEs) were obtained via nasal brushing, expanded in vitro, and cultured at the air-liquid interface as previously described ([5](#R5)–[9](#R9)). Epithelia were treated with basolateral vehicle (DMSO) or ETI (VX-445 3μM (MedChemExpress), VX-661 18μM, VX-770 1μM (SelleckChem)) for 24 hours. Epithelia were then mounted in Ussing chambers containing symmetric Krebs Ringers solution containing 118.9 mM NaCl, 25 mM NaHCO_3_, 2.4 mM K_2_HPO_4_, 0.6 mM KH_2_PO_4_, 1.2 mM MgCl_2_, 1.2 mM CaCl_2_, 5 mM dextrose, at 37°C and adjusted to pH 7.4 in the presence of 5% CO_2_. Transepithelial voltage clamped to 0. After recording baseline current, amiloride (100 uM) was added to block epithelial Na^+^ channels. Then, DIDS (10 uM) was added apically to block calcium activated Cl^−^ channels. To fully activate CFTR, forskolin (10uM) was added, followed by CFTR_inh_-172 (10 uM), an inhibitor of CFTR. Short-circuit current (I_SC_) produced by CFTR channel function was assessed by the change in short-circuit current (ΔI_SC_) in response to a CFTR inhibitor (CFTR_inh_-172) ([7](#R7), [8](#R8)). Subjective and objective data discussed below and displayed in [table 2](#T2) was collected by electronic medical record review and discussion with the patient and their care team.

Based upon data obtained from theratyping response, *insurance approval for ETI was attained for subjects A and B*.

## Discussion

In this study, we applied an ex vivo theratyping approach ([7](#R7)–[9](#R9)) to assess *CFTR* function in airway epithelial cells from individuals with *CFTR* variants ineligible for ETI therapy and used the results to guide clinical decisions. Our theratyping results demonstrate that two out of the six individuals (Subjects A and B), both of whom carry 1 copy of the *N1303K* allele, exhibited notable improvement in *CFTR*-mediated ion transport following ETI treatment. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that the *N1303K* variant, historically classified as a minimal function variant, may retain some residual *CFTR* activity and respond to *CFTR* modulators in specific contexts ([9](#R9)–[12](#R12)).

Theratyping in Subject A (*N1303K/G542X*) demonstrated a marked increase in *CFTR* function from 3.1% to 20.6% of healthy control levels following ETI treatment. Clinically, this patient demonstrated stable lung function, no pulmonary exacerbations, and most notable improvements in 1) ppFEV1 from 84.5% to 101.5% (averages from 4 separate visits) and 2) airway microbiology colonization, exhibiting no growth >1 year after initiation of ETI therapy.

Theratyping in Subject B (*N1303K/R553X*) exhibited a similar improvement in CFTR function, increasing from 2.8% to 13.9% of healthy control levels. Clinically, this individual experienced a significant reduction in pulmonary exacerbations, decreased sputum production, decreased cough frequency and sustained lung function, despite severe baseline disease. Sweat chloride levels decreased by approximately 22 mmol/L (from 109 and 110 mmol/L to 87 and 91 mmol/L, respectively) after treatment initiation, indicating improved CFTR function in vivo. Importantly, Subject B’s clinical improvements also facilitated a significant life event—two successful full-term pregnancies—highlighting the broader impact of ETI on quality of life. Previously, it was strongly recommended that she avoid pregnancy, as women with an ppFEV1 of <50% of predictive are recommended to avoid pregnancy due to health risks to both the mother and the fetus ([13](#R13), [14](#R14)).

Conversely, four individuals in our study (Subjects C–F) demonstrated either minimal or no response to ETI, highlighting the heterogeneous nature of *CFTR* variants and the necessity of a personalized approach to CF treatment. Subjects C and E exhibited low baseline *CFTR* function and failed to show meaningful improvement with ETI. However, whereas subject C’s variants — *(G542X/1717-1G>A)* are expected to be unresponsive to ETI, the variants in subject E (genotype *G542X; 9T;TG11/ 5T;TG12*) have a more complex effect on CFTR activity. While *G542X* is not expected to respond to modulators, *5T;TG12* has been added to the FDA-approved list of ETI-responsive variants. Of the 5T haplotypes, *5T;TG12* is of modest penetrance and classified as having varying clinical consequences. Children with *5T;TG12* in trans with a CF-causing variant display a gradient of presentations with moderate rates of CF and CFTR-related disorders compared to the non-CF causing *5T;TG11* variant and the more penetrant *5T;TG13* variant. Interestingly, many of the children with *5T;TG12* or *5T;TG13* who went on to develop CF had benign presentations and normal sweat chlorides upon initial testing further underscoring the need for alternative ways to evaluate *CFTR*-function in these patients ([15](#R15)).

Meanwhile, Subjects D and F displayed normal to high-range baseline *CFTR* activity and no further response to ETI, indicating that their symptoms likely stem from non-*CFTR*-related pathophysiology. Given their respective genotypes — Wild-Type and *F508del/5T;TG11* — these results are in concordance with current evidence that such genotypes are not expected to cause CFTR-related lung disease. While *5T;TG11* was previously grouped with more penetrant 5T alleles as a variant of varying clinical consequence, subsequent studies have shown it causes minimal splicing defects and is now classified in CFTR2 as non–CF-causing. In trans with a CF-causing variant, it does not cause CF and is only rarely associated with *CFTR*-related disorders. Overall, these subjects were therefore deemed unlikely to have cystic fibrosis; these results encouraged subsequent evaluation for other diagnoses.

Of the *CFTR* variants not initially approved for ETI therapy, the *N1303K* variant is among the most common and has been classified as a class 2 defect (processing/folding) similar to *F508del*. Notably, *N1303K* has additional features of a class 3 defect (gating) not seen in *F508del* and was historically classified as non-responsive to ETI. Recent emerging evidence suggests that patients with at least 1 *N1303K* allele may respond to ETI therapy ([9](#R9)–[12](#R12)).

A recent multicenter, open-label, single arm trial evaluating the effects of a 28-day ETI therapy in individuals with *N1303K* found significant improvements in ppFEV_1_, BMI, and weight retention though no change in mean sweat chloride ([10](#R10)). Similarly, there are two recent case reports using a theratyping approach to provide evidence for and initiate ETI therapy in individuals with at least 1 *N1303K* allele. In each report, patients saw improvements in lung function by ppFEV_1_, weight retention, symptom burden, yet no improvements in mean sweat chloride ([11](#R11), [12](#R12)); this variant was approved for ETI more recently.
