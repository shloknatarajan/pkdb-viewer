## Abstract

Pharmacogenetic dosing improves the accuracy of warfarin dosing, but current pharmacogenetic dosing algorithms are less accurate in populations of African ancestry. The cytochrome P450 2C9*5 (CYP2C9*5) allele is found almost exclusively in populations of African ancestry, and in vitro studies suggest CYP2C9*5 is associated with reduced clearance of warfarin. The clinical relevance of this single-nucleotide variation (SNV) (formerly SNP) is uncertain. In this multicentered study of 2,298 patients (49% female, 35% Black) taking warfarin, we quantified the association between the CYP2C9*5 allele and warfarin requirements. The CYP2C9*5 SNV was present in 2.3% of Black and 0.07% of White patients. Without taking CYP2C9*5 into account, pharmacogenetic algorithms that include other SNVs overestimated the warfarin dose by 30% (95% confidence interval (19–40%), P < 0.001), an average of 1.87 mg/day (SD 1.64) in heterozygotes (P < 0.001). Noncarriers required a slightly (0.23 mg/day, SD 2.09) higher than predicted dose. Genotyping for CYP2C9*5 corrected the potential overdose and halved overall dosing error in heterozygotes. Patients carrying CYP2C9*5 require a clinically relevant reduction in warfarin dose. Given the potential to improve the accuracy and safety of warfarin dosing in populations of African ancestry, we have incorporated this SNV into a nonprofit website to assist warfarin initiation (www.WarfarinDosing.org).

## METHODS

All patients provided written informed consent in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was approved by the Human Subjects’ Committee at the participating medical centers.

### Patients

The study population included patients of varying race (assessed via self-report) requiring warfarin for a variety of indications. Therapeutic dose was defined as the dose (mg/day) leading to INRs within therapeutic range on at least two consecutive visits. The most common target INR was 2.5 (range 2–3). Patients were recruited from Washington University,^13^ University of Florida,^21^ University of Alabama,^15^ University of Chicago,^22^ University of California at San Francisco (UCSF),^23^ Brigham and Women’s Hospital and all other PREVENT sites,^24^ and University of Illinois at Chicago (UIC)^25^ as previously detailed.

To reduce variability, we excluded patients who were known to carry rare variants that affect warfarin sensitivity: 2 with the vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1(*VKORC1*) D36Y,^26^ 3 with calumenin (CALU rs339097 A > G),^27^ and 11 who were homozygous for gamma-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCx rs11676382).^28^ We also excluded patients taking rifampin (*N* = 1) or carbamazepine (*N* = 3), because these drugs increase warfarin clearance.^18^

### Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the percent dose reduction attributable to the *CYP2C9*5* allele, based on the ratio of observed therapeutic dose to the pharmacogenetically predicted dose. The secondary outcome was prediction error (PE), defined as the difference (in mg/day) between the predicted and actual therapeutic warfarin dose in patients with a **5* variant. The predicted dose^13^ accounts for the *VKORC1* polymorphism, *CYP2C9*2* and **3* variants, age, body surface area, target INR, use of amiodarone, smoking status, Black vs. other race, and warfarin indication. PE = *ŷ* − *y*_i_, where *ŷ* = predicted dose (mg/day), and *y*_i_ = actual dose (mg/day). Positive values of PE correspond to overprediction by the algorithm. PE was used to calculate a correction factor for the pharmacogenetic algorithm, which accounts for the **5* variant effect.

### Genotyping methods

At University of Florida and UIC, mouthwash samples were obtained from each patient. At Washington University St. Louis and University of Chicago, whole blood samples or saliva samples were obtained. At the University of Alabama at Birmingham, DNA was harvested from whole blood samples using the PAXgene blood DNA extraction kits (Germantown, MD).^15^ At UCSF, DNA was extracted from whole blood using Qiagen (Hilden, Germany). Genomic DNA was isolated according to methods previously detailed.^13,22,25,29^

### Statistical analysis

The significance level was set as a type I error rate ≤ 0.05 for a two-sided test. To normalize the right skewed distribution of the therapeutic warfarin dose, we analyzed its natural logarithm. We compared PEs among carriers of the **5* variant and noncarriers using a two-tailed *t* test. Ordinary least squares regression was used to minimize the error of the *CYP2C9*5*-adjusted model ([Supplemental Material S1](#SD1)). When comparing categorical data (i.e., covariate distributions), we used a χ^2^ test except that we used the Fisher exact test to compare warfarin indications among those who had vs. those who lacked the **5* variant (because some expected cell counts were <5).

The predicted dosing algorithm incorporates clinical and genetic factors that are known to affect warfarin dose requirements. This pharmacogenetic dosing algorithm^13^ was adjusted to account for the *CYP4F2* V433M variant (rs2108622) by increasing the prediction by 8% (i.e., we multiplied the predicted dose by 1.08) in known carriers of the variant, after adjusting for the race-specific allele frequency and average dose. We then repeated our analyses using the International Warfarin Pharmacogenetics Consortium (IWPC) algorithm.^18^ To calculate **5* adjustments for these validated pharmacogenetic algorithms,^13,18^ we quantified a multiplier based on allele frequencies and effect size, determined by average dose ratio for carriers and noncarriers in the whole population and in the Black subgroup, as detailed in the [Supplemental Material](#SD1). Because **5* adjustments differed by <1% in White and Black patients, we averaged the adjustments.

## RESULTS

### Baseline characteristics

Of 2,298 genotyped patients, 810 (35.2%) were Black. Participants were elderly (mean age = 61 years), and 48.7% were female ([Table 1](#T1)). A total of 20 patients had the *CYP2C9*5* allele (of whom 19 (95%) were Black): 17 patients were heterozygous (i.e., *CYP2C9*1/*5* genotype). Three were compound heterozygotes: one each with **2/*5*, **5/*6*, and **5/*11* genotypes. Patients who carried the **5* allele were more likely to be Black (*P* < 0.001): The frequency of the **5* allele was 0.012 in Black and 0.0003 in White patients. The one White patient with the **5* allele was an immigrant from the former Yugoslavia.

### Warfarin dosing results

In **5* carriers (*n* = 20), the mean daily dose of warfarin was 4.46 mg (range 2.64–8.52 mg), compared with 5.50 mg (range 0.53–34.29 mg) in noncarriers. Without taking *CYP2C9*5* into account, the validated pharmacogenetic algorithm overpredicted the warfarin dose by 30% (95% confidence interval (19–40%), *P* < 0.001), or an average of 1.87 mg/day (SD 1.64) in heterozygotes (*P* < 0.001, [Table 3](#T3)). Noncarriers had a slightly (0.23 mg/day, SD 2.09) greater than predicted dose (*P* < 0.001, [Table 3](#T3)).

Each *CYP2C9*5* variant was associated with a 30% reduction in warfarin dose. Adjusting the predicted dose for this SNV in heterozygotes, as proposed ([Table 4](#T4)), reduced their mean PE by 51.9%, to 0.97 mg/day (median absolute error 0.93 mg/day). Using the method detailed in [Supplemental Material S1](#SD1), [Table 4](#T4) provides the multiplicative adjustment for patients who do and do not carry the *CYP2C9*5* variant allele. For example, patients predicted to have a daily dose of 5.0 mg/day (with any pharmacogenetic algorithm) would have a therapeutic dose of 5 mg × 0.70 = 3.5 mg/day if they were heterozygous. This adjustment was applied to the pharmacogenetic algorithm used by the IWPC,^18^ and results were identical (median absolute error = 0.93 mg/day).

## DISCUSSION

*In vitro*, the *CYP2C9*5* allele has reduced catalytic activity of *S*-warfarin compared with the **1* allele.^14,31^ Therefore, we hypothesized that patients with the **5* variant would have significantly lower warfarin dose requirements. Our results confirm this hypothesis: patients carrying the **5* variant required a 30% lower than predicted warfarin dose. This effect exceeds the 19% reduction from the **2* variant and is commensurate with the 33% reduction from the **3* variant. The **2* and **3* variants triple the risks of a supratherapeutic INR and adverse events in patients beginning warfarin,^2,8,32^ so the **5* variant also is likely to be clinically important.

Popular dosing algorithms^13,18,33,34^ have not quantified the *CYP2C9*5* variant effect, and have lower accuracy when predicting therapeutic warfarin requirements in Black patients.^35–38^ Failure to account for the **5* allele may contribute to the inaccuracy of other algorithms in Black patients.^23,39,40^ Like others,^35,36^ we found that Black patients required higher daily doses of warfarin when compared with other races, which is likely the result of a lower frequency of the *VKORC1–1639 A* allele in Black vs. White patients. However, we found that carriers of the **5* allele required significantly lower doses than predicted. When we used validated pharmacogenetic dosing algorithms to adjust for clinical and other genetic factors that affect warfarin dose, we found that they overpredict maintenance dose in **5* heterozygotes by 30%. Thus, there is potential for overdosing and adverse events in **5* carriers when initiating warfarin, similar to what has been observed among **2* and **3* carriers.^2,11,32,41^ With the incorporation of the **5* correction factor, the predicted error among **5* carriers decreased. Inability to account for other SNVs that are more common in Black patients also contributes to the lower accuracy of pharmacogenetic dosing algorithms in this population.^27,37,38,42,43^ The incorporation of the **5* correction factor resulted in a slightly higher predicted dose among noncarriers ([Table 4](#T4)).

This study had several strengths. First, we identified 20 patients with the **5* allele, which is as many patients as the prior studies combined.^34,38,44–47^ Second, we quantified the *in vivo* effect of the **5* variant on warfarin dose in a diverse multicentered collaboration, which provided study generalizability. Third, the predicted dose of the participants was first evaluated with validated pharmacogenetic dosing algorithms that took into account underlying clinical and pharmacogenetic covariates (*CYP2C9*2*, *CYP2C9*3* and *VKORC1* SNVs) that affect warfarin dose. This approach was helpful because of baseline differences between patients who did and did not carry *CYP2C9*5* ([Table 1](#T1)), reflecting the differing prevalence of this variant in White vs. Black populations.

A study limitation is that we were not able to directly characterize the *in vivo* pharmacokinetic properties of warfarin clearance with the **5* variant. However, we describe a warfarin dosing reduction in **5* heterozygotes that is comparable to that seen in **3* heterozygotes. These results are consistent with prior *in vitro* data^14^ suggesting that the **5* variant has reduced enzyme activity similar to that observed in patients with the *CYP2C9*3* variant. This effect is likely explained by the proximity of the *CYP2C9*3*^48^ and *CYP2C9*5*^14^ alleles, which lie within the substrate recognition site that seems to be a hot spot for clinically relevant mutations in *CYP2C9*. A minor weakness is that we did not have long-term outcomes, so we cannot determine whether the *CYP2C9*5* variant is associated with an elevated risk of adverse events, as has been observed with the *CYP2C9*2* and **3* variants.^2,11,32,41^ Another weakness is that patients of African ancestry are known to be more frequent carriers of other alleles associated with lower warfarin dose requirements, including *CYP2C9*6*, *CYP2C9*8* and *CYP2C9*11*, as well as a *CYP2C9* cluster variant and *FPGS* variant.^49,50^ This genotyping information was not available for most subjects, so these variants could not be included in the analysis. Finally, no patients were homozygous for the **5* allele, so the proposed correction factor for that genotype ([Table 4](#T4)) is uncertain.

To summarize, patients carrying the **5* allele had clinically and statistically significant reductions in their therapeutic warfarin dose. This effect may predispose them to bleeding complications during warfarin initiation. Refinement of pharmacogenetic algorithms by including the effect of the *CYP2C9*5* variant may improve the accuracy and safety of warfarin therapy, particularly among Black patients. Thus, we incorporated this SNV into a nonprofit website to assist warfarin initiation ([www.WarfarinDosing.org](http://www.WarfarinDosing.org)). Use of this website for a patient with the *CYP2C9*5* improved dosing accuracy.^40^
