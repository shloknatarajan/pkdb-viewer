# OPRM1 rs1799971 Polymorphism Predicts Differential Response to Extended-Release Naltrexone in Alcohol Use Disorder: The Interplay of Genetics and Motivation

## Abstract

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) remains a major contributor to the global burden of disease. Extended-release naltrexone (XR-NTX), a μ-opioid receptor antagonist, is a key pharmacological option for maintaining abstinence, though individual treatment response varies substantially. Genetic variability in the μ-opioid receptor gene (OPRM1), particularly rs1799971, may underpin this heterogeneity. Study objective was to evaluate the association between OPRM1 rs1799971 polymorphism and clinical outcomes of XR-NTX therapy in patients with AUD while examining interactions with motivational profiles. In this prospective study, 100 patients with AUD in remission received six monthly intramuscular injections of XR-NTX (380 mg). Genotyping for OPRM1 rs1799971 was performed using real-time allele-specific PCR. Primary outcomes included craving dynamics (PACS), relapse rates, abstinence duration, and treatment adherence over 180 days. Motivation was assessed through structured interviews. The A/A genotype was associated with longer abstinence (150.6 ± 36.2 days), lower relapse rates (29.2%), greater craving reduction, and higher treatment completion (86.2%) compared to A/G and G/G (A/A vs. A/G: p = 0.005; A/A vs. G/G: p = 0.021). The G allele significantly increased relapse risk (OR = 2.93; 95% CI: 1.48−5.79). Intrinsic motivation was associated with better outcomes. A significant genotype-motivation interaction was observed (p = 0.038). No genotype-related differences in adverse event frequency were observed. OPRM1 rs1799971 polymorphism significantly influences the efficacy of XR-NTX in AUD. Carriers of the A/A genotype derive greater therapeutic benefit, while G allele carriers may require intensified clinical support. Pre-therapeutic OPRM1 genotyping could optimize XR-NTX personalization, with G-allele carriers warranting intensified psychosocial support.

Keywords: AUD, extended-release naltrexone, pharmacogenetics, OPRM1 polymorphism, rs1799971, personalized medicine

## Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) remains one of the major contributors to the global burden of disease, ranking among the leading causes of morbidity, disability, and premature mortality worldwide. This underscores its significance as a pressing medical, social, and economic challenge on a global scale.^1^ Achieving—and, more importantly, sustaining—long-term remission in AUD constitutes a critical and clinically demanding objective. This challenge is exacerbated by the presence of pronounced pathological craving for alcohol (PCA) and the high rates of relapse commonly observed in affected individuals.^2^

Among current pharmacotherapeutic strategies for AUD, opioid receptor antagonists have emerged as a key class of agents, with extended-release formulations of naltrexone (XR-NTX) holding a particularly prominent position.^3^ The pharmacodynamic mechanism of naltrexone involves selective antagonism of μ-opioid receptors, a process that indirectly disrupts the function of the mesolimbic dopaminergic pathway. This pathway plays a central role in reward processing and the neurobiological underpinnings of pathological craving for ethanol.^4^ As a result, XR-NTX treatment attenuates PCA intensity, thereby enhancing the likelihood of sustained abstinence.

A notable advantage of the extended-release intramuscular formulation of naltrexone (commercially marketed as Vivitrol) lies in its ability to maintain stable therapeutic plasma concentrations of the active compound for up to 30 days following a single injection. This pharmacokinetic profile substantially improves treatment adherence compared to the daily dosing required with oral formulations.^5^ However, the marked interindividual variability in clinical response to XR-NTX highlights the need to identify predictors of its therapeutic efficacy.^6^

Genetic factors—particularly the functional polymorphism in the *OPRM1* gene (*rs1799971*, *118A*>*G*), which encodes the primary target of naltrexone—as well as clinical characteristics such as patient motivation, may play a pivotal role in shaping treatment outcomes. While prior studies of oral naltrexone and *OPRM1* yielded conflicting results, pharmacogenetic data for extended-release formulations remain scarce. We hypothesized that *A/A* homozygosity and intrinsic motivation would synergistically predict superior XR-NTX outcomes. The present study aimed to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the influence of *OPRM1* (*rs1799971*) polymorphism and clinical-psychopathological phenotypes on the efficacy and safety of XR-NTX therapy in patients with AUD.

## Material and Methods

### Participants and Setting

This prospective study enrolled 100 patients diagnosed with “Alcohol Dependence, remission” (ICD-10 codes F10.20/F10.21) who were undergoing treatment at the Moscow Research and Practical Center on Addictions between May, 2017 and May, 2018. The study protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Russian Medical Academy of Continuing Professional Education of the Ministry of Health of the Russian Federation, on November 28, 2016. All participants provided written informed consent for participation, which included both pharmacotherapy and genetic testing.

Inclusion criteria were: age 18–75 years; confirmed diagnosis of alcohol dependence in remission; completion of alcohol withdrawal management; absence of signs of acute intoxication based on clinical and laboratory assessment (negative alcohol tests, normal carbohydrate-deficient transferrin [CDT] levels); and capacity to provide informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: acute psychotic states, dementia or other severe cognitive impairment, decompensated somatic illnesses (hepatic, renal, or cardiovascular failure), pregnancy or lactation, confirmed opioid use (including a positive naloxone challenge), hypersensitivity to naltrexone, and refusal of injectable therapy.

### Study Procedures

The study design comprised several sequential phases: initial screening, initiation of extended-release naltrexone (XR-NTX) therapy, molecular genetic testing, and clinical follow-up over a 180-day treatment period.

At baseline, participants underwent eligibility screening, medical history taking, physical examination, and laboratory testing, including hepatic enzyme levels (ALT, AST), γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT), and CDT, as biochemical markers of alcohol use.

Upon confirmation of clinical remission and provision of informed consent, all participants received an initial intramuscular injection of XR-NTX (Vivitrol) at a dose of 380 mg (Day 0). Concurrently, venous blood samples were collected for genotyping of the *OPRM1* polymorphism (*rs1799971*). Genotyping was performed via allele-specific real-time PCR.

A total of six injections were planned at 30-day intervals (Days 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150), and all treatments were administered in an outpatient setting. Follow-up visits occurred at the same intervals, including a final assessment at Day 180.

At each visit, a comprehensive assessment was conducted, including: verification of abstinence through clinical interview, GGT and CDT levels; evaluation of pathological craving for alcohol using the Penn Alcohol Craving Scale (PACS);^7^ monitoring of treatment safety using the UKU Side Effect Rating Scale;^8^ assessment of adherence to treatment.

Relapse was defined as any confirmed alcohol consumption occurring within the pharmacologically active window of the last injection (i.e., within 28 days), based on clinical and biochemical data. Alcohol use following a missed injection or early study withdrawal was also classified as relapse. Treatment completion was defined as receipt of all six scheduled injections; discontinuation at any stage was classified as non-completion.

Craving severity was assessed via PACS on Days 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 150. Remission and relapse outcomes were evaluated over the full 180-day treatment period. Thus, the study design allowed for evaluation of the impact of *OPRM1* genotype on key treatment outcomes: intensity of craving, risk of relapse, duration of abstinence, and treatment completion.

### Psychopathological Phenotyping: Motivation

At baseline, all participants underwent comprehensive psychopathological assessment, including the assessment of motivational profile for treatment participation.

Motivational profiles were categorized based on interview findings into three levels:

Intrinsic motivation—voluntary participation driven by personal insight and commitment to sobriety;

Extrinsic motivation—treatment engagement driven by external pressure (e.g., family, employer, legal or social services);

Indifferent stance—lack of explicit motivation, with passive agreement to participate.

Associations between motivation level and *OPRM1* genotype (*rs1799971*), as well as their influence on treatment outcomes, were analyzed using chi-square tests and logistic regression. Interactions between motivational profile and *G*-allele carriage were also assessed in models predicting treatment completion and relapse risk.

### Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics v26.0 and Statistica v13.0. Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. In cases of non-normal distribution, non-parametric methods were applied.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons of continuous variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. Genotypic comparisons were conducted using both a three-group model (*A/A, A/G, G/G*) and a binary model comparing *G*-allele carriers (*A/G* + *G/G*) versus *A/A* homozygotes. Due to the small size of the *G/G* subgroup (n = 4), we prioritized a dominant model (*G*-allele carriers [*A/G* + *G/G*] vs. *A/A*). Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold was p < 0.017 for primary outcomes.

Relapse and treatment completion were analyzed as binary outcomes. Associations between genotype and these outcomes were assessed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Multivariate models included demographic (age, sex), clinical (duration of illness, age of onset), and motivational variables (motivation type, rehabilitation potential), if significantly associated with outcomes in preliminary analyses. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To control for Type I error in multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

## Results

### Genotype Distribution

Genotyping of the *OPRM1 rs1799971* polymorphism revealed the following distribution: 65% of participants (n = 65) were homozygous for the major allele (*A/A*), 31% (n = 31) were heterozygous (*A/G*), and 4% (n = 4) were homozygous for the minor allele (*G/G*). The genotype distribution conformed to Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (χ^2^ = 0.0158, p = 0.99), indicating that the sample was representative for evaluating the clinical relevance of this variant. Minor allele frequency (MAF) was 19.5%, consistent with European populations.

### Remission Duration

Duration of abstinence over the 180-day observation period varied according to genotype. Patients with the *A/A* genotype demonstrated the longest remission, with a mean abstinence duration of 150.6 ± 36.2 days. In comparison, individuals with the *A/G* genotype exhibited shorter remission (128.1 ± 47.3 days; p = 0.005 vs. *A/A*), and those with the *G/G* genotype had the shortest average duration (104.8 ± 64.9 days). Although the *G/G* subgroup was small, a clear trend toward shorter remission in carriers of the *G* allele was observed, particularly between the *A/A* and *A/G* groups.

### Relapse Rates

Relapse frequency was also significantly associated with genotype. During the 180-day follow-up, relapse occurred in 29.2% of *A/A* homozygotes, 51.6% of *A/G* heterozygotes, and 75% of *G/G* homozygotes (p < 0.01). Logistic regression analysis confirmed that the presence of the *G* allele significantly increased the risk of early relapse (OR = 2.93; 95% CI: 1.48−5.79; p = 0.002). Adjusting for potential confounders—including age, sex, and motivational profile—did not attenuate the statistical significance of this association.

### Treatment Adherence

Treatment adherence, defined as completion of all six scheduled injections of extended-release naltrexone, also varied by genotype. Among *A/A* homozygotes, 86.2% completed the full course, compared to 61.3% of *A/G* heterozygotes and only 25% of G/G homozygotes. These differences were statistically significant (χ^2^ = 12.47; p = 0.002), suggesting that *OPRM1* genotype may be an important determinant of treatment retention.

### Craving Dynamics

Craving severity, measured by the PACS, was monitored throughout the 180-day treatment period. A/A homozygotes showed a robust and sustained reduction in alcohol craving, from a baseline score of 12.6 ± 3.4 to 3.9 ± 2.3 at Day 180 (p < 0.001). In contrast, *A/G* carriers exhibited a more modest decline (from 12.1 ± 3.7 to 6.8 ± 3.2; p = 0.013), and the *G/G* group showed no statistically significant change. Between-group differences at Day 180 remained significant (p = 0.001).

### Motivational Profiles

Motivational profiles were determined through structured interviews and self-report. Participants with intrinsic (self-driven) motivation achieved better outcomes (treatment completion: 83%; relapse-free: 79%) than those with extrinsic motivation (completion: 52%; relapse-free: 38%) or indifferent attitudes (completion: 29%; relapse-free: 18%). Intrinsic motivation was more prevalent in *A/A* carriers (68%) than *G*-allele carriers (42%; χ^2^ = 4.21, p = 0.041). In multivariate analysis, intrinsic motivation reduced relapse risk in *G*-allele carriers (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.19−0.93, p = 0.032).

### Adverse Events

No significant differences in the frequency or severity of adverse drug reactions were observed across genotype groups. The most common side effects were transient fatigue, injection site discomfort, and reduced appetite—none of which required discontinuation of therapy. This suggests that tolerability of extended-release naltrexone is not influenced by *OPRM1 rs1799971* genotype.

### Summary of Findings

In summary, the presence of the *G*-allele at *rs1799971* in the *OPRM1* gene was associated with reduced remission duration, increased relapse rates, attenuated reduction in alcohol craving, and lower adherence to extended-release naltrexone therapy. These findings support the potential utility of *OPRM1* genotyping in the personalized management of alcohol use disorder.

### Discussion

The findings of this study provide robust evidence that the *OPRM1 rs1799971* polymorphism significantly influences clinical outcomes during extended-release naltrexone (XR-NTX) treatment in patients with AUD. Specifically, the presence of the minor *G*-allele (i.e., *A/G* or *G/G* genotypes) was associated with less favorable outcomes across multiple dimensions: reduced duration of abstinence, higher relapse rates, lower treatment adherence, and less pronounced reduction in pathological alcohol craving.

Previous literature has suggested that the *G*-allele of *rs1799971*, which results in an amino acid substitution (*Asn40Asp*) in the μ-opioid receptor, may reduce the receptor’s affinity for endogenous ligands such as β-endorphin. This alteration may in turn affect the neuropsychobiological mechanisms underlying reward and reinforcement. In the context of μ-opioid receptor antagonism—such as with naltrexone—these functional changes may attenuate the clinical efficacy of the drug. The *G*-allele (*Asn40Asp*) reduces β-endorphin binding affinity, potentially blunting naltrexone’s antagonism of alcohol-induced reward. While some studies, particularly those focusing on heroin or opioid dependence, have reported enhanced treatment response in *G*-allele carriers, findings in alcohol use disorder remain inconsistent.

In the present study, patients with the wild-type *A/A* genotype demonstrated more substantial reductions in alcohol craving, higher rates of treatment completion, and lower relapse incidence. These results align with several prior investigations that reported better clinical outcomes on naltrexone among *A/A* carriers with alcohol dependence.^9,10^ Conversely, other studies have failed to detect genotype-based differences,^11^ possibly due to methodological heterogeneity, differences in follow-up duration, or variation in outcome measures. Notably, this is among the first studies to specifically evaluate the pharmacogenetic effects of *OPRM1 rs1799971* in the context of the extended-release formulation of naltrexone, thereby expanding the pharmacogenetic evidence base for this therapeutic modality.

A further contribution of this study lies in its analysis of gene–phenotype interactions. Our data indicate that the patient’s motivational profile significantly modulate treatment outcomes. For example, individuals with an intrinsic motivation demonstrated favorable outcomes regardless of genotype, whereas *G*-allele carriers with extrinsic motivation were at particularly high risk of relapse. These findings underscore the importance of integrating clinical-phenotypic assessments when planning treatment and selecting candidates for pharmacogenetic testing.

Importantly, tolerability of XR-NTX did not vary by genotype, and adverse event profiles were comparable across all groups. This rules out the possibility that differences in treatment completion were attributable to poorer tolerability among *G*-allele carriers.

Finally, multivariate regression analysis confirmed that the *OPRM1* genotype was an independent predictor of relapse risk, even after adjusting for confounding variables. Nevertheless, findings pertaining to the *G/G* subgroup should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size (n = 4). For this reason, we applied a combined analysis of *A/G* and *G/G* carriers, in accordance with established pharmacogenetic guidelines for analyzing rare alleles and minimizing misinterpretation.

Taken together, these results highlight the clinical utility of combining genetic and clinical–psychological factors when developing individualized treatment strategies for alcohol dependence. Genotyping for *OPRM1* may serve as a valuable tool for predicting treatment response to naltrexone, particularly when used alongside assessments of patient motivation. Implementing a personalized medicine approach in addiction psychiatry could enhance treatment effectiveness, improve retention, and reduce the risk of early relapse.

Nonetheless, replication of these findings in larger samples with extended follow-up and the inclusion of additional molecular and neuropsychological biomarkers will be essential to further elucidate the mechanisms of pharmacogenetic response and optimize treatment protocols.

### Limitations

Despite yielding clinically significant findings, the present study has several methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the open-label design without a placebo or active comparator group limits the ability to isolate genotype-specific effects of XR-NTX and assess the specificity of observed associations.

Second, the sample size for the *G/G* genotype subgroup was extremely small (n = 4), reducing statistical power and increasing the risk of both Type I and Type II errors. While differences between *A/A* and *A/G* genotypes were statistically significant, the association between *G/G* genotype and poor treatment outcomes requires validation in larger cohorts. Nonetheless, this study incorporated 180-day follow-up data on all key outcomes—abstinence duration, relapse rate and timing, PACS-based craving dynamics, and treatment adherence—thereby enhancing its clinical relevance.

Third, only a single polymorphism (*OPRM1 rs1799971*) was examined. The therapeutic response to naltrexone likely reflects a polygenic architecture involving multiple loci within *OPRM1* and other genes involved in dopaminergic, glutamatergic, and serotonergic signaling. The limited genetic scope reduces the comprehensiveness of the pharmacogenetic analysis.

Fourth, phenotypic characteristics such as motivational profile were assessed primarily through clinical interviews and validated yet subjective instruments. This introduces a potential for rating variability, particularly in stratifying patients by non-molecular phenotypes.

Additionally, the study was conducted at a single clinical center, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Sociodemographic characteristics, motivational dynamics, and treatment histories may differ across other geographic or clinical populations.

Finally, without a placebo arm, we cannot exclude that genotype effects reflect general prognostic differences rather than XR-NTX-specific pharmacodynamics.

Taken together, while the associations observed between *OPRM1* genotype and clinical outcomes are promising, they should be regarded as preliminary and warrant confirmation through multicenter, prospective, randomized studies with expanded genetic and phenotypic profiling.

### Conclusion

The present study demonstrates a significant association between genetic variation at *OPRM1 rs1799971* and treatment response to extended-release naltrexone in patients with alcohol dependence. Individuals with the *A/A* genotype—the wild-type variant of the μ-opioid receptor—exhibited greater reductions in pathological craving, longer periods of abstinence, and higher adherence rates. In contrast, carriers of the *G* allele, particularly those homozygous for *G*, were more likely to relapse, showed weaker clinical response, and were more likely to discontinue treatment prematurely.

These genotype-dependent differences support the pathophysiological relevance of the μ-opioid receptor as a therapeutic target for naltrexone and highlight the potential of *OPRM1* genotyping as a clinical tool for stratifying risk of treatment failure. For *G*-allele carriers, more intensive therapeutic interventions—such as enhanced psychosocial support, closer clinical monitoring, or combination treatments—may be warranted.

Thus, the *OPRM1 rs1799971* polymorphism represents a promising pharmacogenetic marker for predicting individual response to naltrexone and for informing a personalized approach to the treatment of alcohol dependence. Incorporating pharmacogenetic testing into clinical decision-making algorithms could improve therapeutic precision, reduce relapse risk, and enhance remission stability in patients undergoing treatment with extended-release naltrexone.
