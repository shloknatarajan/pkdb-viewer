## Introduction

Hypertension remains one of the most significant public health challenges worldwide, serving as a leading risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, stroke, and chronic kidney disease^1^. In Pakistan, the burden of hypertension has reached alarming levels, affecting nearly one-third of the adult population^2^. Within the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province, particularly in Peshawar and its surrounding rural districts, prevalence estimates are even higher reaching up to 38%, reflecting the complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors that contribute to this condition^3^. Importantly, hypertension is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous disorder, characterized not only by elevated blood pressure values but also by variable degrees of target organ involvement, including hypertensive cardiac remodeling and functional impairment, which may influence therapeutic responsiveness^4^.

The renin–angiotensin system (RAS) is a central regulator of blood pressure and fluid homeostasis, and it represents one of the most important therapeutic targets in hypertension management. Pharmacological agents that interfere with this system, such as angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) like valsartan, and diuretics like hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), are among the most widely prescribed antihypertensive medications globally. Combination therapy with valsartan/HCTZ has been shown to enhance therapeutic efficacy and facilitate better blood pressure control compared to monotherapy^5^. Despite its clinical success, however, the response to such combination therapy varies markedly among individuals, and a substantial proportion of patients fail to reach target blood pressure levels^6^. This interindividual variability may partially reflect underlying differences in cardiovascular structure^7^, neurohormonal activation^8^, and salt sensitivity, all of which interact with pharmacologic mechanisms of action^7,8^.

This variability in treatment response underscores the importance of pharmacogenomics, the study of how genetic differences influence drug efficacy and safety. Among the most investigated pharmacogenetic contributors to antihypertensive response are single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genes encoding key components of the RAS^9^. The angiotensinogen (*AGT*) gene encodes the precursor of angiotensin II, while the angiotensin-converting enzyme (*ACE*) gene catalyzes the conversion of angiotensin I into its active form. Variations in these genes, including the *ACE* insertion/deletion (I/D) polymorphism and several *AGT* promoter variants such as rs5050, rs5051, and rs699, have been repeatedly examined for their influence on drug response^10^. The *ACE I/D* polymorphism, in particular, alters the circulating and tissue levels of *ACE* and has been associated with variable blood pressure–lowering effects in response to diuretics such as HCTZ^11^. Similarly, *AGT* variants are hypothesized to modify angiotensinogen expression and substrate availability, ultimately affecting downstream RAS activity and the efficacy of ARB or diuretic therapy^12^.

While numerous studies from Western and East Asian populations have explored these associations, there remains a significant gap in the understanding of pharmacogenomic determinants of antihypertensive response among South Asian populations, particularly those from Peshawar. Genetic structure and allele frequency distributions often differ across ethnic groups, and such diversity can influence the magnitude and even the direction of pharmacogenomic effects^13^. Furthermore, South Asian populations, including those in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, exhibit a higher prevalence of salt-sensitive and low-renin hypertension phenotypes, which may modify responsiveness to antihypertensive therapies^2^. Consequently, findings derived from non-South Asian cohorts cannot be directly extrapolated to Pakistani populations without local validation. Moreover, most prior research has focused on single-locus associations, overlooking the possibility that multiple variants within the same physiological pathway may interact to produce synergistic or antagonistic effects. Given the hierarchical nature of the RAS—where angiotensinogen acts as a substrate and *ACE* functions as a key enzymatic regulator—gene–gene interactions between *AGT* and *ACE* variants could exert non-linear and context-dependent influences on therapeutic outcomes^14^.

The present study addresses this knowledge gap by exploring both single-variant associations and multilocus interactions within the RAS pathway among hypertensive patients from Peshawar. Considering the high prevalence of hypertension in this region and the frequent clinical use of valsartan/HCTZ combination therapy, understanding genetic determinants of drug response carries substantial clinical relevance. This study specifically investigates the relationship of five RAS gene variants, *AGT* rs5051, rs5050, rs4762, *ATG* rs699, and *ACE I/D*, with the magnitude of systolic and diastolic blood pressure reduction following Valsartan/HCTZ treatment. Additionally, it examines the independent and interactive effects of these polymorphisms, employing multivariate regression and multilocus analyses to elucidate their combined influence on antihypertensive response. By integrating genetic and clinical data, this research seeks to contribute to the foundation for personalized medicine in hypertension management within the Pakistani population, paving the way toward genotype-guided therapeutic strategies and more precise blood pressure control in high-risk groups.

## Results

The overall cohort demonstrated significant antihypertensive responses to combination therapy with valsartan and hydrochlorothiazide (Valsartan/HCTZ). Mean SBP decreased by 23.2 ± 16.4 mmHg, and DBP decreased by 14.8 ± 10.9 mmHg, confirming the clinical efficacy of this regimen. However, the variability observed across individuals highlighted the influence of demographic, lifestyle, and genetic factors on treatment outcomes.

Overall, the cohort responded favorably to Valsartan/HCTZ therapy, but the magnitude of reduction varied across subgroups.

Age exerted a modest influence on response. Participants aged > 60 years demonstrated slightly greater mean reductions in both systolic (24.0 ± 22.4 mmHg) and diastolic BP (13.5 ± 14.4 mmHg) than younger individuals, a trend likely related to higher baseline vascular stiffness and increased capacity for improvement^5^. Sex had no significant effect, with males (22.7 ± 16.3 mmHg) and females (23.6 ± 16.6 mmHg) showing comparable SBP decreases.

Body mass index (BMI) remained an important determinant of efficacy. Normal-weight participants (18–25 kg/m²) experienced greater reductions in SBP and DBP (22.9 ± 15.9 mmHg and 15.5 ± 9.3 mmHg, respectively) than obese subjects (≥ 35 kg/m²; SBP 14.2 ± 17.3 mmHg, DBP 10.7 ± 11.4 mmHg). This inverse relationship reflects the impact of obesity-related sodium retention and sympathetic activation, which reduce RAS-blockade sensitivity^6^.

Lifestyle factors further modulated treatment outcomes. Current smokers exhibited numerically higher SBP reductions (28.3 ± 35.4 mmHg) than nonsmokers (23.2 ± 16.4 mmHg), though variability was wide. Snuff users showed the greatest overall BP decline (SBP 39.2 ± 20.0 mmHg; DBP 25.0 ± 16.5 mmHg), possibly reflecting unmeasured metabolic or behavioral influences^9^. Patients following hypertension-specific or low-sodium diets achieved larger SBP reductions (25.6 ± 17.6 mmHg) than those consuming unrestricted diets (22.9 ± 16.3 mmHg), emphasizing the additive benefit of dietary sodium restriction in conjunction with diuretic and RAS blockade^5^. Regular physical activity was associated with slightly greater SBP lowering (24.3 ± 17.9 mmHg) compared with inadequate exercise (23.0 ± 16.1 mmHg), suggesting a modest contribution of fitness to therapeutic outcome.

Socio-demographic indicators also influenced response patterns. Literate participants demonstrated marginally higher BP reductions (24.5 ± 15.7 mmHg) than illiterate individuals (22.8 ± 16.7 mmHg), while those reporting satisfactory financial status showed somewhat greater SBP and DBP decreases (25.8 ± 17.5 mmHg; 15.6 ± 14.1 mmHg) than economically constrained patients. These trends implied that treatment adherence and access to care may contribute to observed differences.

Clinical history variables yielded additional insight. A positive family history of hypertension corresponded with greater mean SBP and DBP decreases (24.6 ± 17.2 mmHg and 15.5 ± 10.8 mmHg, respectively), possibly reflecting heightened awareness and better adherence. Duration of disease and age at onset exerted minimal influence, and the presence of comorbidities did not substantially alter outcomes except in a few outliers.

Dose level exerted a modest effect: patients receiving 160/12.5 mg achieved slightly larger SBP and DBP reductions (23.6 ± 16.6 mmHg; 15.0 ± 11.0 mmHg) compared with the 80/12.5 mg group (22.5 ± 15.9 mmHg; 13.8 ± 10.7 mmHg), suggesting dose-dependent enhancement within therapeutic limits. Compliance was satisfactory overall; adherent participants showed BP reductions similar to non-adherent ones, indicating consistent medication effectiveness among those who completed the protocol.

Taken together, the univariate analysis demonstrated that age, BMI, diet, physical activity, and certain lifestyle and socioeconomic factors modestly affected the antihypertensive response. However, the magnitude and variability of BP reduction could not be fully explained by these non-genetic variables, underscoring the essential contribution of genetic determinants explored in subsequent analyses.

### Genotype and allele frequencies of the investigated polymorphisms

Genotype frequencies for all five investigated polymorphisms are presented in Supplementary Table [S1](#MOESM1). For *AGT* rs5050, genotype frequencies were AA (24.9%), AC (70.6%), and CC (4.5%), corresponding to allele frequencies of 60.2% for the A allele and 39.8% for the C allele. For the *ACE* I/D polymorphism, genotype frequencies were II (13.8%), ID (76.0%), and DD (10.2%), yielding allele frequencies of 51.8% for the I allele and 48.2% for the D allele. These data demonstrate substantial allelic representation of functionally relevant *AGT* and *ACE* variants within the study cohort, supporting the validity of downstream pharmacogenetic association analyses.

For *AGT* rs5051, the AG genotype was most frequent (75.7%), while *AGT* rs4762 variant allele carriage was observed in 15.8% of participants. For *AGT* rs699, 87.6% of individuals carried at least one variant allele.

### Association of gene variants with blood pressure response

Among the studied polymorphisms, *AGT* rs5050 exhibited the strongest and most consistent influence on treatment response. Carriers of the variant C allele (AC or CC) demonstrated significantly greater BP reductions compared with wild-type (AA) individuals. The mean SBP reduction was 13.37 ± 16.83 mmHg in AA carriers, 26.48 ± 15.17 mmHg in AC carriers, and 26.56 ± 11.62 mmHg in CC carriers (Table [2](#Tab2)). Similarly, DBP reductions were 6.91 ± 9.85 mmHg (AA), 17.74 ± 10.06 mmHg (AC), and 13.33 ± 8.45 mmHg (CC) (Table [3](#Tab3)).

Regression analysis confirmed these differences: for SBP, the AC genotype showed a B coefficient of 13.1 mmHg (95% CI: 9.34–16.9, *p* < 0.001) and the CC genotype 13.2 mmHg (95% CI: 4.92–21.5, *p* = 0.002), both relative to AA carriers (Table [2](#Tab2)). For DBP, AC carriers had 10.82 mmHg (95% CI: 8.40–13.25, *p* < 0.001), while CC carriers showed 6.42 mmHg (95% CI: 1.11–11.73, *p* = 0.018) (Table [3](#Tab3)).

The *ACE* I/D polymorphism also displayed a significant association with DBP change (Table [3](#Tab3)). DD homozygotes exhibited smaller DBP reductions (8.01 ± 10.8 mmHg) compared with II carriers (13.67 ± 11.6 mmHg), corresponding to a regression coefficient of − 5.66 mmHg (95% CI: −10.29, − 1.04, *p* = 0.017).

Multivariate regression incorporating clinical covariates (age, sex, BMI, obesity, age at onset of HTN, duration of HTN, family history, drug dose and adherence) confirmed the independent contribution of *AGT* rs5050 and *ACE* I/D polymorphisms to antihypertensive efficacy. The *AGT* rs5050 variant remained highly significant for both SBP (AC: B = 13.91, 95% CI: 9.63–18.19, *p* < 0.001; CC: B = 15.18, 95% CI: 6.40–23.95, *p* < 0.001) as shown in Table [2](#Tab2), and DBP (AC: B = 10.49, 95% CI: 7.75–13.22, *p* < 0.001; CC: B = 7.15, 95% CI: 1.55–12.76, *p* = 0.013). The *ACE* I/D polymorphism also retained an independent association with DBP (B = − 4.88, 95% CI: −9.24, − 0.52, *p* = 0.028). (Table [3](#Tab3))

These results indicated that the influence of these genetic variants was not mediated through confounding demographic or clinical variables but reflected direct modulation of the pharmacodynamic response to therapy.

In contrast, *AGT* rs5051, ATG rs699, and *AGT* rs4762 demonstrated no statistically significant associations with SBP or DBP changes after multivariate adjustment, despite some nominally significant findings observed in unadjusted (crude) analyses. Specifically, *AGT* rs5051 showed a nominal association with SBP in the unadjusted allele model (B = 2.8 mmHg, 95% CI: 0.7–4.9, *p* = 0.009), which was no longer significant after covariate adjustment (multivariate B = 1.1 mmHg, 95% CI: −1.1 to 3.3, *p* = 0.316) (Table [2](#Tab2)). Similarly, ATG rs699 demonstrated an unadjusted association with DBP (B = 5.15 mmHg, 95% CI: 1.71–8.58, *p* = 0.003), but this effect attenuated and lost significance in the multivariate model (B = 1.96 mmHg, 95% CI: −1.63 to 5.54, *p* = 0.284) (Table [3](#Tab3)). For *AGT* rs4762, a nominal unadjusted association with DBP, as shown in Table [3](#Tab3), was observed (B = − 3.73 mmHg, 95% CI: −6.85 to − 0.61, *p* = 0.019), which did not persist after adjustment (B = − 2.09 mmHg, 95% CI: −5.21 to 1.04, *p* = 0.189).

These findings indicate that the initial unadjusted significance of these variants was likely driven by confounding demographic or clinical factors rather than representing independent pharmacogenomic effects on Valsartan/HCTZ response.

### Multilocus and gene–gene interaction effects

The multilocus analysis based on Estimated Marginal Means (EMM) demonstrated a significant interaction between *AGT* rs5050 and *ACE* I/D genotypes in determining the antihypertensive response to Valsartan/HCTZ therapy. All pairwise genotype comparisons in this analysis were evaluated using Bonferroni-adjusted p-values to account for multiple testing. As shown in Tables [4](#Tab4) and [5](#Tab5), individuals carrying both the *AGT* rs5050 heterozygous (AC) and *ACE* II genotypes exhibited the greatest mean BP reductions (20.61 ± 2.27 mmHg), followed by the *AGT* rs5050 heterozygous (AC) combined with *ACE* ID genotype (17.90 ± 0.67 mmHg). In contrast, patients with the AA/DD combination demonstrated the lowest reduction (3.80 ± 2.33 mmHg), indicating a marked gene–gene influence on BP response (Bonferroni-adjusted *p* < 0.001).

These patterns indicated that the coexistence of favorable alleles in both *AGT* and *ACE* genes produced additive or synergistic antihypertensive benefits, while the *ACE* DD genotype appeared to diminish the pharmacologic response even in the presence of *AGT* variant alleles.

### Estimated marginal means (EMM) analysis of AGT rs5050 and ACE I/D × dose interaction

The gene–dose interaction analysis evaluated the influence of *AGT* rs5050 and *ACE* I/D genotypes across two Valsartan/HCTZ dose levels (80/12.5 mg and 160/12.5 mg) (Fig. [1](#Fig1)). For *AGT* rs5050, at 80/12.5 mg, mean SBP reductions were 17.5 ± 2.4 mmHg (AA), 29.4 ± 1.7 mmHg (AC), and 27.3 ± 6.9 mmHg (CC), while at 160/12.5 mg, corresponding reductions were 9.9 ± 2.2 mmHg, 25.0 ± 1.2 mmHg, and 26.2 ± 4.6 mmHg, respectively. DBP changes followed the same pattern, with the largest reductions observed in AC and CC carriers at both doses. These findings indicated that variant carriers achieved maximal antihypertensive effects even at the lower dose, suggesting dose-independent sensitivity and early pharmacodynamic saturation.

## Discussion

The findings of this study provided compelling evidence that both clinical and genetic factors contributed to the interindividual variability in blood pressure (BP) response to Valsartan/HCTZ therapy. The investigation demonstrated that polymorphisms in *AGT* and *ACE*, two essential genes in the RAS, significantly modulated systolic and diastolic BP responses, both independently and through gene–gene and gene–dose interactions. An important strength of the present study is the integration of genotype and allele frequency data, which allows the pharmacogenetic findings to be interpreted within a population-specific genetic framework. In this Pakistani hypertensive cohort, the *AGT* rs5050 C allele frequency was 39.8%, while the *ACE* D allele frequency was 48.2%, indicating that nearly half of the population carried alleles previously linked to altered RAS activity.

These observations support the concept that antihypertensive drug response reflects not only pharmacologic exposure but also underlying cardiovascular and neurohormonal heterogeneity^4^, Collectively, these results highlight the complexity of pharmacogenomic determinants governing antihypertensive drug efficacy and underscore the importance of distinguishing robust genetic effects from nominal associations.The regression analyses identified *AGT* rs5050 as the strongest genetic determinant of BP reduction. Even after adjustment for demographic and lifestyle covariates, the variant’s association with SBP and DBP response remained statistically robust. This supported the hypothesis that *AGT* variant played a critical role in regulating the pharmacodynamic effectiveness of ARBs. The relatively high prevalence of the rs5050 C allele suggests that a substantial proportion of hypertensive patients in this population may be genetically predisposed to enhanced responsiveness to ARB-based therapy, particularly when combined with a thiazide diuretic. Similar associations between *AGT* promoter variants and antihypertensive response have been reported in Asian populations, suggesting population-specific relevance of *AGT*-mediated RAS regulation^15^. In contrast, other *AGT* variants examined in this study (rs5051, rs699, and rs4762) demonstrated only nominal significance in unadjusted analyses and did not retain statistical significance after multivariate adjustment, indicating that their effects were likely influenced by confounding clinical or demographic factors rather than reflecting independent pharmacogenomic contributions.

The biological rationale was grounded in the function of *AGT* as the gene encoding angiotensinogen, the precursor substrate for the RAS cascade. Promoter region polymorphisms such as rs5050 were known to upregulate *AGT* transcription, leading to elevated plasma angiotensinogen levels^12^. This increase enhanced substrate availability for renin and augmented Ang I and Ang II synthesis. Consequently, individuals with variant alleles experienced heightened RAS activity, predisposing them to higher baseline BP. When treated with valsartan, a selective AT₁ receptor antagonist, these patients exhibited a more pronounced hemodynamic response because the drug blocked an overactive pathway. In other words, the greater the basal RAS drive, the greater the relative impact of pharmacologic blockade^5^. This mechanism aligns with pharmacogenomic models described in East and South Asian cohorts, where ARB efficacy appears closely linked to baseline RAS activation rather than absolute BP alone^10^.

The *ACE* I/D polymorphism also exerted a significant independent effect, particularly on DBP reduction. The D allele was associated with higher plasma and tissue *ACE* activity, resulting in enhanced Ang II formation and reduced responsiveness to RAS blockade^11^. This mechanism may be especially relevant in patients with hypertension-mediated cardiac remodeling, where elevated tissue *ACE* activity contributes to persistent vascular tone and diastolic dysfunction^4^. DD homozygotes displayed attenuated responses, supporting this mechanistic model. Although DD homozygotes represented only 10.2% of the cohort, the nearly equal distribution of I and D alleles (51.8% vs. 48.2%) underscores the relevance of *ACE* genotype in shaping interindividual variability in antihypertensive response within this population. Importantly, the persistence of this association after multivariate adjustment suggested that the observed genetic effects are not simply confounded by clinical or lifestyle factors, in agreement with previous pharmacogenomic studies of RAS polymorphisms^10^. Notably, the attenuation of unadjusted associations observed for rs5051, rs699, and rs4762 after covariate adjustment contrasts with the stability of the *ACE* I/D and *AGT* rs5050 effects, reinforcing the specificity and robustness of these variants as pharmacogenetic predictors. This finding highlights that variant prevalence alone does not imply functional pharmacogenomic significance and reinforces the importance of adjusted models when interpreting genetic associations.

The multilocus interaction analysis (Tables [4](#Tab4) and [5](#Tab5)) provided a deeper understanding of how RAS gene combinations influenced BP response. The most favorable outcomes were observed among individuals carrying the *AGT* rs5050 AC genotype in conjunction with the *ACE* II or ID genotypes, while those with AA/DD exhibited diminished BP reduction. This pattern reflected a functional interplay between substrate availability (*AGT*) and enzymatic activity (*ACE*). Importantly, the *AGT* rs5050 × *ACE* I/D interaction effects were statistically significant based on Bonferroni-adjusted p-values derived from post hoc pairwise comparisons, indicating that these multilocus associations are unlikely to be explained by chance or inflation of type I error arising from multiple testing.

Mechanistically, the *AGT*–*ACE* interaction reflects complementary control points within the RAS, where *AGT* variants increase angiotensinogen substrate availability and *ACE* genotypes modulate enzymatic conversion to angiotensin II. In individuals carrying *AGT* variant alleles combined with the low-activity *ACE* II genotype, elevated substrate levels are met with reduced conversion capacity, resulting in minimal Ang II formation during ARB therapy and maximal suppression of Ang II–mediated vasoconstriction, producing greater BP reduction. Conversely, when *AGT* variant alleles coexist with the high-activity *ACE* DD genotype, enhanced *ACE* activity sustains residual Ang II production—likely through both *ACE* and chymase pathways thereby diminishing the pharmacologic effect of AT₁ receptor blockade and attenuating the overall BP response^6,10,14^.

These results emphasized the importance of viewing pharmacogenetic effects as network phenomena rather than isolated single-gene influences. RAS regulation was inherently compensatory, involving feedback between renin release, angiotensinogen synthesis, and aldosterone secretion. Multilocus combinations therefore provided a more comprehensive and physiologically relevant predictor of treatment response^10^.

The estimated marginal means (EMM) analyses (Fig. [1](#Fig1)) also revealed profound influences of both *AGT* rs5050 and *ACE* I/D genotypes on the dose–response relationship to Valsartan/HCTZ therapy. Variant *AGT* carriers (AC and CC) exhibited markedly greater BP reductions than wild-type individuals even at lower doses, suggesting that maximal pharmacologic effect was achieved earlier. Similarly, the *ACE* I/D × Dose interaction demonstrated that heterozygous ID carriers exhibited the strongest SBP reduction at standard doses, whereas DD homozygotes required higher exposure for a comparable response. This convergence at higher doses indicates that increased ARB availability can partially overcome excessive *ACE*-mediated angiotensin II production^5^. These findings are particularly relevant in South Asian populations, where mixed RAS- and volume-mediated hypertension mechanisms often necessitate combination therapy^16^.

Collectively, these multilocus and gene–dose findings demonstrated that *AGT* and *ACE* polymorphisms jointly influenced both the magnitude and kinetics of antihypertensive response. The gene–gene and dose–response analyses reinforced the concept that genetic determinants modulated the pharmacodynamic ceiling, slope, and sensitivity of Valsartan/HCTZ therapy.

These observations carried important translational implications. Patients harboring *AGT* rs5050 AC or CC and *ACE* II/ID genotypes could achieve optimal BP reduction at standard doses, while *ACE* DD or *AGT* AA carriers might have required higher initial doses or early combination therapy. Such genotype-informed strategies could improve BP control while minimizing unnecessary dose escalation, offering particular benefit to South Asian populations who f*ACE* high hypertension prevalence and early cardiovascular risk^17^.

The consistency of associations across regression, multilocus, and dose–response analyses strengthened the evidence that RAS polymorphisms meaningfully altered antihypertensive response. Mechanistically, these variants influenced both the degree of receptor blockade efficacy and the sensitivity of BP response to dose increments. In *AGT* and *ACE* variant carriers, receptor blockade effectively inhibited an overactive hormonal axis, leading to substantial BP reduction even at moderate drug exposure. Conversely, wild-type *AGT* or *ACE* DD genotypes exhibited partial blockade and required dose intensification or adjunctive pharmacologic mechanisms to achieve equivalent results^6^.

The present findings extended earlier pharmacogenomic studies by integrating gene–gene and gene–dose analyses, demonstrating how multilocus interactions shaped clinical efficacy. These results were consistent with previous observations by Brugts et al.^14^ and Oliveira-Paula et al.^10^, which indicated that *AGT* and *ACE* variants predicted interindividual variability in response to RAS blockade.

The observed genotype-associated BP differences exceeded 10–15 mmHg for SBP, a clinically meaningful variation comparable to the effect of adding an additional drug class in combination therapy^5^. Such differences carried substantial implications for cardiovascular risk reduction, as each 10 mmHg decrease in SBP has been associated with a 20% reduction in major cardiovascular events.

Population-specific factors in the studied cohort, including dietary salt intake and regional genetic architecture characteristic of Pakistani hypertensive patients, might have contributed to the observed genotype effects^13^. Hence, incorporating population-specific pharmacogenomic data could further refine therapeutic algorithms for hypertension management within Pakistani and closely related South Asian populations. South Asian populations have been reported to exhibit a mixed hypertension phenotype, combining elements of salt sensitivity and variable renin activity, which may enhance the clinical relevance of RAS-related genetic variation when ARBs are used in combination with diuretics^16^. The allele frequencies observed for *AGT* and *ACE* variants in this Pakistani hypertensive cohort align with this intermediate phenotype and support the biological plausibility of the reported pharmacogenetic associations. Differences in allele frequencies of *AGT* and *ACE* variants across South Asian, European, and African populations further emphasize the importance of region-specific pharmacogenomic validation rather than direct extrapolation from Western cohorts^18,19^. In contrast, African ancestry populations more frequently demonstrate low-renin, volume-dependent hypertension with attenuated BP response to RAS blockade, underscoring how ancestry-related pathophysiology can modify the clinical impact of identical pharmacogenomic variants^20,21^.

This study has several methodological and contextual constraints that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The evaluation of antihypertensive response was limited to a four-week treatment period, which restricts the ability to assess long-term blood pressure control or clinically meaningful outcomes such as hypertension-mediated organ damage, and may underestimate delayed or cumulative pharmacogenomic effects that emerge with prolonged therapy. Blood pressure measurements were obtained exclusively in the clinic; therefore, variations that may have been captured through home or ambulatory monitoring were not assessed. Although clinic blood pressure measurement remains the cornerstone of routine hypertension management and pharmacogenomic trials, reliance on this approach may be susceptible to white coat effects and may not fully reflect 24-hour blood pressure variability. Such non-differential measurement error would be more likely to attenuate true genotype–response associations rather than generate spurious findings. Lifestyle data, including smoking, diet, and physical activity, were self-reported and susceptible to recall bias. Misclassification of these variables may have limited their ability to fully account for behavioral confounding; however, this bias is unlikely to be genotype-dependent and therefore unlikely to explain the observed genetic associations. The absence of a washout period may have allowed residual effects of previous antihypertensive medications to influence baseline measurements, potentially affecting the magnitude, but not the direction, of blood pressure change estimates. The study population was recruited from a single geographic region, which may limit generalizability of the genetic distribution and treatment-response patterns, particularly given known ethnic differences in renin–angiotensin system activity and allele frequencies. Furthermore, although several RAAS-related variants were examined, the sample size may have constrained the detection of subtle gene–gene or gene–dose interactions. The focus on valsartan-based therapy also restricts the applicability of results to other antihypertensive drug classes, and extrapolation to alternative RAS inhibitors or non–RAS-based therapies should be undertaken with caution.

In conclusion, these findings demonstrated that *AGT* rs5050 and *ACE* I/D polymorphisms were major determinants of BP response to Valsartan/HCTZ therapy. Their combined multilocus and dose-dependent effects revealed that therapeutic response in hypertension was governed by an intricate interplay of genetic, biochemical, and pharmacologic factors. Recognition of these genotype–dose relationships could facilitate the implementation of personalized antihypertensive strategies, where genetic profiling informs drug selection, initial dosing, and combination therapy to optimize efficacy and minimize resistance.

## Materials and methods

### Study design and population

A prospective pharmacogenomic association study was conducted involving 354 adult patients (≥ 30 years) diagnosed with essential hypertension. Participants were prescribed Valsartan/Hydrochlorothiazide (Valsartan/HCTZ) at doses of either 80/12.5 mg or 160/12.5 mg. Patients were recruited from cardiology department hypertension clinics at Lady Reading Hospital, Peshawar, beginning in May 2021 until December 2022. Individuals with secondary hypertension, significant renal or hepatic impairment, pregnancy, or using other renin–angiotensin system–modifying drugs were excluded. Detailed demographic, clinical, and lifestyle data (including age, sex, BMI, smoking, diet, exercise status) were obtained via structured questionnaires. The study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of Lady Reading Hospital, Medical Teaching Institution (Ref: 92/LRH/MTI, dated 22.03.2021), all participants provided written informed consent, and all study procedures were performed in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations, as well as the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

### Blood pressure measurement

Blood pressure (BP) was measured in the clinic using a validated oscillometric device by trained personnel. After the patient rested for five minutes in a seated position, three consecutive BP measurements were taken at one-minute intervals, and the average of these was used as the recorded BP, consistent with guidelines recommended by the European Society of Hypertension^22^. Baseline BP was recorded before initiating Valsartan/HCTZ, and follow-up BP was assessed after a treatment duration of four weeks. The change in BP was calculated as the difference between baseline and post-treatment values (ΔSBP, ΔDBP).

### Clinical and adherence data collection

Clinical covariates collected included comorbidities, duration of hypertension, family history of hypertension, and treatment dose. Lifestyle factors (smoking, diet, exercise) were self-reported using structured questionnaires. Medication adherence was assessed using the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), a validated tool in hypertensive populations^23^. The MMAS-8 yields scores from 0 to 8, with lower scores indicating poorer adherence^24^.

### DNA extraction and genotyping

Peripheral blood (3 mL) was collected in EDTA tubes, and genomic DNA was extracted from leukocytes using a standard commercial kit. DNA quality and concentration were verified using spectrophotometry, and samples were normalized to a working concentration for genotyping.

We genotyped the following polymorphisms: **AGT** **rs5050 (− 20 A/C)**, **AGT** **rs5051 (− 6 A/G)**, **AGT** **M235T (rs699)**, **AGT** **T174M (rs4762)**,** and** **ACE** **I/D**. These variants were selected based on prior strong evidence for their roles in the RAS, blood pressure regulation, and response to antihypertensive therapy, in line with STREGA recommendations^25^.

### AGT rs5050 and rs5051 genotyping

The promoter polymorphisms rs5050 and rs5051 were genotyped via PCR–RFLP using a previosely reported method^26^. PCR reactions (20 µL) included 10× buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.1 mM dNTPs, 0.5 µM of each primer, 1 U Taq polymerase, and approximately 100 ng of genomic DNA. For **rs5051**, we used forward primer 5′-ACATCTGAGAGAGACAAGACCG-3′ and reverse primer 5′-CTCAGGCTGTCACACACCTAG-3′, with annealing at 59 °C, followed by digestion with **BsiE I**. For **rs5050**, the primers were 5′-T*AGT*ACCCAGAACAACGGCAG-3′ (forward) and 5′-GTCGCTTCTGGCATCTGTC-3′ (reverse), with annealing at 62 °C, followed by digestion with **Eco0109 I**. Digestion products were resolved on 3% agarose gels and visualized under UV after ethidium bromide staining.

### AGT M235T (rs699) and T174M (rs4762) genotyping

Genotyping of **rs699** (M235T) and **rs4762** (T174M) was also performed via PCR–RFLP using a previously reported method^27^. A common primer set was used: forward 5′-GATGCGCACAAGGTCCTG-3′ and reverse 5′-CAGGGTGCTGTCCACACTGGCTCGC-3′. PCR cycling included an annealing temperature of 67 °C. The amplified product for rs699 was digested with **SfaNI** to discriminate the M (ATG) and T (ACG) alleles, whereas for rs4762, **NcoI** was used to distinguish the T and M alleles. Restriction fragments were separated by electrophoresis on agarose gel, and genotypes assigned based on fragment size.

### ACEInsertion/deletion (I/D) genotyping

The *ACE* I/D polymorphism in intron 16 was genotyped using PCR amplification with forward primer 5′-GCCCTGCAGGTGTCTGCAGCATGT-3′ and reverse primer 5′-GGATGGCTCTCCCCGCCTTGTCTC-3′. PCR products yielded 319 bp (D allele) and 597 bp (I allele), visualized on 1.5% agarose gel. To avoid misclassification due to preferential amplification of the D allele, samples initially classified as DD underwent a second confirmatory PCR using insertion-specific primers (annealing at 67 °C) that amplify only the I allele (Forward primer: 5′-TGGGACCACAGCGCCCGCCACTAC-3′; Reverse primer: 5′-TCGCCAGCCCTCCCATGCCCATAA-3′). This reaction produces a 335 bp fragment if the I allele is present. All genotyping was performed in duplicate, and results were independently verified by two blinded investigators; ambiguous samples were re-genotyped^28^.

### Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using *jamovi* software version 2.7 [[https://www.jamovi.org](https://www.jamovi.org)] continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation; categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences in baseline characteristics were tested using independent t-tests or one-way ANOVA for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

The primary outcomes, ΔSBP and ΔDBP, were used in linear regression models. First, unadjusted (crude) linear regressions examined the association between each genetic variant and BP change. Then, multivariate linear regression models adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, BMI, duration of hypertension, family history, treatment dose, and self-reported adherence (MMAS-8). This stepwise analytical approach allowed differentiation between nominal genotype–phenotype associations and those that remained robust after accounting for potential clinical and lifestyle confounding, thereby strengthening causal inference.

We also tested for interaction effects: **genotype × dose** (to see whether genetic effects differed by Valsartan/HCTZ dose) and **multilocus genotype interactions** (e.g., *AGT*–*ACE*) using general linear models with estimated marginal means. For multilocus interaction analyses, Bonferroni correction was applied to post hoc pairwise comparisons to control for inflation of type I error arising from multiple testing. Accordingly, p-values reported for Tables [4](#Tab4) and [5](#Tab5) represent Bonferroni-adjusted values rather than nominal significance levels. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This analytical framework was designed to mitigate false-positive findings while preserving sensitivity to detect biologically meaningful pharmacogenomic effects, particularly in the context of candidate-gene analyses and interaction modeling. Although no formal a priori power analysis was conducted, a post hoc power calculation was performed to confirm that our sample size provided at least 80% power to detect moderate effect sizes in genotype–BP associations under the observed allele frequencies and phenotype variance. Nevertheless, limited statistical power for rare genotype combinations may have reduced sensitivity to detect subtle gene–gene or gene–dose interactions, and such findings should be interpreted cautiously.
