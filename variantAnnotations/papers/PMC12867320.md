# Frequency of thiopurine methyltransferase variants and exploratory predictors of azathioprine-induced leukopenia in Saudi patients with inflammatory bowel disease


## Background:

Azathioprine, a common inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) treatment, carries a myelosuppression risk, particularly leukopenia. Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) genotyping can predict azathioprine-induced leukopenia (AIL), but data on TPMT polymorphisms in Saudi IBD patients are scarce. We aimed to determine the frequency of TPMT genotype variations in adult Saudi IBD patients and its association with AIL.


## Methods:

We conducted a retrospective analysis including 90 adult IBD patients treated with azathioprine. TPMT genotyping focused on *2, *3A, 3B, and 3C alleles. Patient demographics, clinical data, and azathioprine-related adverse events were extracted. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression identified leukopenia predictors.


## Results:

The TPMT wild-type genotype (*1/*1) was predominant (98%), with only two patients (2.2%) having the heterozygous *1/*3C genotype. Leukopenia occurred in 13% of the cohort, with the higher, though not statistically significant (P = 0.054), incidence in heterozygous patients (50%) versus wild-type (12.5%). Lower hemoglobin (OR = 0.70, P = 0.039) and platelet counts (OR = 0.982, P = 0.005) were significant predictors of leukopenia in wild-type patients. No significant associations were found with age, gender, IBD type, or concomitant therapies.


## Conclusions:

TPMT variants were rare in this Saudi IBD cohort, consistent with other Asian populations, which limited the ability to assess their clinical impact. The observed leukopenia in variant carriers is descriptive only and should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, lower hemoglobin and platelet counts emerged as important predictors of AIL among wild-type patients. These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive hematological monitoring and support the need for future research incorporating additional pharmacogenetic markers such as NUDT15.


## INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting the gastrointestinal tract. Its estimated global prevalence is rising, with significant healthcare burdens associated with disease management. Azathioprine, a thiopurine medication, first introduced in 1969 for the treatment of IBD, continues to be a cornerstone in managing the disease, even with the advent of biologics and small-molecule therapies. Established as an effective monotherapy for maintaining remission, the role of Azathioprine has evolved to include combination therapy with biologics, enhancing outcomes in both induction and maintenance of remission. Its utility extends to special scenarios such as reducing post-operative recurrence and managing fistulizing disease, where its immunomodulatory effects remain indispensable. Despite being an older drug, AZA use is still recommended in clinical guidelines, underscoring its proven efficacy. In addition, its convenient oral route of administration and cost-effectiveness are attractive, offering major advantages in both resource-limited and modern healthcare settings.

Despite its efficacy, a significant limitation of azathioprine treatment is the potential for myelosuppression, a decrease in white blood cell count, particularly leucopenia (low white blood cell count) and neutropenia (low neutrophil count). This complication can increase susceptibility to infections, necessitating dose reduction or treatment discontinuation in some patients.

Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) is a key enzyme involved in the metabolism of azathioprine. Genetic variations within the TPMT gene lead to altered enzyme activity, impacting the rate at which azathioprine is broken down. Individuals with specific TPMT polymorphisms, such as those with two copies of a variant allele (homozygous) or one copy (heterozygous), have reduced TPMT activity. This can result in higher levels of active azathioprine metabolites, increasing the risk of azathioprine induced leucopenia (AIL). Personalized approach allows for tailoring azathioprine dosing to individual TPMT status, potentially minimizing the risk of AIL and optimizing treatment efficacy. The frequency of TPMT deficiency varies with ethnicity, with the highest prevalence in Caucasians (~10%) to lowest in Asians and Africans (2% to 5%). In addition, the common mutant loci and the association of TPMT polymorphism with AIL varies between ethnicities.

In addition to pharmacogenetic testing, therapeutic drug monitoring of thiopurine metabolites—6-thioguanine nucleotides (6-TGN) and 6-methylmercaptopurine (6-MMP)—provides a functional measure of thiopurine metabolism and TPMT activity. Elevated 6-TGN levels are associated with therapeutic efficacy but also an increased risk of myelotoxicity, whereas disproportionate accumulation of 6-MMP is linked to hepatotoxicity and skewed metabolism. International guidelines, including the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC), recommend that integrating genotyping (TPMT, and increasingly NUDT15) with metabolite monitoring may improve the safety and efficacy of thiopurine therapy. However, this combined approach is not routinely implemented in clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings.

While the importance of TPMT genotyping for optimizing thiopurine therapy is increasingly recognized, most data from Asia originates from Far East. Unfortunately, there is lack of information on the prevalence and impact of TPMT polymorphisms in Saudi IBD patients. This knowledge gap poses challenges for implementing a personalized medicine approache in IBD management within the Saudi Arabian population.

Hence, we aimed to determine the frequency of TPMT genotype variation in adult Saudi patients with IBD and the risk of thiopurine-induced myelosuppression.


## PATIENTS AND METHODS

This retrospective study investigated the frequency of TPMT genotypes and their association with azathioprine-induced leucopenia in adult patients with IBD.


### Study design and setting

This was a single center study performed at a tertiary referral hospital in Saudi Arabia. A retrospective analysis was conducted utilizing the electronic medical records (EMR) and included patients from January 2019 until January 2024.


### Patients

Adult patients (>18 years old) with a confirmed diagnosis of IBD, who received care KAMC were reviewed. All patients who received azathioprine treatment and underwent TPMT genotyping were included. Patients with incomplete documentation, including follow-up of white blood cell (WBC) counts or azathioprine exposure less than 7 days, were excluded.


### Data collection

Demographic and clinical details were retrieved from EMRs, including: age, gender, type of IBD using the Montreal classification, azathioprine dosage, full blood count data, specifically WBC and neutrophil counts, were collected. Leukopenia was defined objectively as WBC ≤2.5 × 109/L or neutrophil count ≤1.0 × 109/L, applied uniformly across all patients, with treating physicians determining attribution to the azathioprine without influencing this definition.


### Genotyping

Bioscientia international lab, Ingelheim, Germany, performed molecular genetic analysis of the TPMT gene. The analysis focused on three coding exons covering mutations known to cause variants associated with reduced TPMT activity: c.238G>C (rs1800462), c.460G>A (rs1800460), and c.719A>G (rs1142345) resulting in TPMT *2, *3A, *3B, or *3C alleles.


### Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 25 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. For variables with small sample sizes, minimum and maximum values are also reported. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using independent t-tests for continuous parametric data and Chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Multivariable analysis was performed using logistic regression to identify independent predictors. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.


### Ethical considerations

The study was approved by our center’s Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 23-1203). All patient data was anonymized to ensure confidentiality.


## RESULTS


### Patient characteristics

A total of 90 patients were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 31.5 years [standard deviation (SD) ±11.4]. The cohort consisted of 81% of patients diagnosed with Crohn’s disease (CD) and 56% males with a mean IBD disease duration of 6.32 (SD years ± 3.78). Most patients were receiving anti-TNF (78%), apart from Ustekinumab (8.9%), vedolizumab (5.6%) and steroids (5.6%). With regards to thiopurine dosing, most patients were maintained on 100 mg of azathioprine (72.2%), while smaller proportions received 50 mg (15.6%), 75 mg (5.6%), or 150 mg (6.7%). Azathioprine-related adverse events developed in 19% of the patients in the form of leukopenia (13%), gastrointestinal (GI) upset (2.2%), skin rash (2.2%), and abnormal liver enzymes (1.1%). ### Risk of leukopenia in wild-type TPMT patients

In 88 patients with wild-type TPMT 11 (12.5%) developed leukopenia. There were no statistically significant differences in age (mean 29.5 vs. 31.7 years, P = 0.99), BMI (23.2 vs. 24.2 kg/m², P = 0.45), or disease duration (5 vs. 6 years, P = 0.17) between those who developed leukopenia and those who did not. Patients who developed leukopenia had significantly lower platelet counts (233.6 × 109/L vs. 348.6 × 109/L, P < 0.01) compared to those without leukopenia. All leukopenia cases occurred in CD patients (100%), whereas no cases were recorded in UC or IC patients. However, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.39). Azathioprine dosage was not significantly different between patients with and without leukopenia (P = 0.789). No significant differences were found in the use of anti-TNF agents (P = 0.24), steroids (P = 1.00), or vedolizumab (P = 0.5). However, a non-significant trend toward increased Ustekinumab use was observed in patients with leukopenia (27% vs. 6.5%, P = 0.058) [Table 3]. The results were similar when the two patients with heterozygote TPMT genotypes were included.

## DISCUSSION

This study investigated the frequency of TPMT genotypes in adult Saudi patients with IBD and their impact on thiopurine-induced myelosuppression, specifically leukopenia. Our findings reveal a predominantly wild-type TPMT genotype (98%) in this Saudi cohort, with only two patients exhibiting a heterozygous *1/*3C genotype. This overall low frequency of TPMT variants is consistent with previous studies from Asian populations. While generally low, TPMT polymorphism rates do show some regional variation within Asia. Studies from China, Japan, and Korea have consistently reported TPMT polymorphism rates around 1%–3%, while studies in India have reported slightly higher frequencies ranging from 4.2% to 6.2%. This contrasts sharply with the higher prevalence (~10%) observed in Caucasian populations.

Despite the low prevalence of TPMT variants, 19% of our patients experienced azathioprine-related adverse events, with leukopenia being the most common (13%). Although the incidence of leukopenia appeared higher in the two patients with heterozygous TPMT genotypes (50%) compared to wild type (12.5%), this finding is descriptive only and cannot be interpreted statistically due to the extremely small number of variant carriers. Similar limitations have been reported in other Asian cohorts, where the rarity of TPMT variants restricts the ability to establish robust associations. This contrasts with Western populations, where larger numbers of variant carriers have enabled stronger conclusions regarding the predictive role of TPMT genotyping for AIL. In addition, TPMT variant carriers in our study were prescribed lower azathioprine doses (50–75 mg) compared with wild-type patients, who predominantly received 100 mg; however, this observation should be interpreted with caution given the very limited number of variant cases and is likely descriptive only. In our Saudi cohort, therefore, the key contribution was to document the low frequency of TPMT polymorphisms, consistent with other Asian populations.

Multivariate analysis identified lower hemoglobin levels (OR = 0.70, P = 0.039) and platelet counts (OR = 0.98, P = 0.005) as independent predictors of leukopenia in patients with wild-type TPMT. A retrospective study from China similarly reported an association between lower hemoglobin and an increased risk of leukopenia (HR = 0.34; 95% CI: 0.18–0.67). However, there is a paucity of studies evaluating the relationship between baseline platelet count and leukopenia. Our finding that lower platelet counts may predict leukopenia is noteworthy and warrants further investigation. It is possible that these lower hematological parameters reflect the general myelosuppressive effect of azathioprine, rather than an independent predisposition to myelosuppression unrelated to the drug’s known pharmacological profile.

This study also explored other potential risk factors for leukopenia. While all cases of leukopenia in our cohort occurred in patients with Crohn’s disease, the difference in leukopenia rates between Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis was not statistically significant. This lack of a significant association between IBD type and leukopenia is in line with prior studies. In our cohort, commonly used IBD medications, including anti-TNF agents, corticosteroids, and the biologics Ustekinumab and vedolizumab, did not significantly affect the risk of leukopenia. Although we observed a non-significant trend towards increased leukopenia with Ustekinumab (P = 0.058), this finding requires further investigation in larger studies. Previous research on the influence of anti-TNF agents on AIL has yielded mixed results, with European studies reporting a negative association, while Asian studies, mirroring our findings, have generally shown the lack of association. Matching the results of most studies, our results did not find a significant association between age or gender and the development of leukopenia. Finally, the lack of association between vedolizumab and leukopenia in our study is in accordance with its gut-specific mechanism of action, which is not expected to directly impact bone marrow function.

Our study has several important limitations. First, the small number of TPMT variant carriers significantly limited statistical power and prevented meaningful conclusions regarding their association with leukopenia. This small sample size also restricted the ability to perform more complex analyses, such as exploring interactions between different variables. Second, NUDT15 genotyping was not performed. This is a key weakness, as NUDT15 variants are more frequent than TPMT in Asian and Middle Eastern populations and are increasingly recognized as stronger predictors of thiopurine-induced leukopenia. Third, we were unable to measure thiopurine metabolites (6-TGN and 6-MMP). These pharmacokinetic markers provide essential insight into drug exposure and toxicity, and their absence prevented us from distinguishing between genetic and non-genetic causes of leukopenia. Furthermore, the study’s single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to other Saudi IBD populations, and its retrospective nature introduces the possibility of bias in data collection and interpretation. These omissions reflect the reality of current practice in our region, where such assays are not routinely available, but they nonetheless reduce the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should integrate both pharmacogenetic (TPMT and NUDT15) and pharmacokinetic (6-TGN and 6-MMP) data, ideally in larger multicenter cohorts, to provide a more complete and clinically meaningful risk assessment in Saudi IBD patients.

In conclusion, our study confirms the low prevalence of TPMT variants in Saudi IBD patients, consistent with other Asian populations. Although leukopenia appeared more frequent in the two heterozygous carriers, this finding is descriptive only and cannot be interpreted statistically due to the very small number of variant cases. Importantly, our exploratory analysis suggests that baseline hemoglobin and platelet counts may provide additional risk signals for leukopenia in wild-type patients. Taken together, the main contribution of our study is to document the rarity of TPMT polymorphisms in this population and to highlight other potential clinical predictors. Future research with larger cohorts and inclusion of additional pharmacogenetic markers, such as NUDT15, will be essential to better define genetic risk factors for thiopurine-induced leukopenia and to advance personalized medicine approaches in Saudi IBD patients.
