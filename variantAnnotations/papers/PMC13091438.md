# Pharmacological boosting of azacitidine/venetoclax in acute myeloid leukemia


## Key Points

Cobicistat allowed an eightfold reduction in venetoclax while optimizing exposure, demonstrating a cost-effective strategy for AML treatment.

Cobicistat potentiated antileukemic activity of azacitidine/venetoclax, positioning it as a promising complementary agent in AML therapy.


## Visual Abstract


## Abstract

Azacitidine/venetoclax is the standard treatment for patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) unfit for intensive chemotherapy. Cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) is the major metabolizing enzyme for venetoclax, and its inhibition can boost venetoclax. In the HOVON 171 phase 2 trial, patients with AML were treated with azacitidine/venetoclax/cobicistat. This 2-stage, open-label, multicenter phase 2 trial included a crossover run-in phase followed by an ongoing extension phase. In cycle 1, patients received standard-dose azacitidine/venetoclax. In cycle 2, cobicistat was added, and venetoclax was reduced to 50 mg. The primary end point was pharmacokinetic equivalence, defined as 90% confidence interval (CI) of geometric mean ratios (GMRs) >0.8 for area under the curve over 24 hours (AUC0-24h) and maximum plasma concentration (Cmax). Polymorphisms in genes encoding for CYP enzymes were determined. In vitro assays were performed to assess cobicistat’s impact on the antileukemic effect of azacitidine/venetoclax in AML cell lines. In 13 evaluable patients, cobicistat-boosted venetoclax at 50 mg achieved higher exposure than standard 400-mg dosing. GMRs were 2.0 (90% CI, 1.4-2.8) for AUC0-24h and 1.4 (90% CI, 1.0-2.0) for Cmax. In the intention-to-treat population, 65% achieved complete remission (CR) or CR with incomplete hematologic recovery. Interpatient variability in venetoclax exposure because of CYP3A4 polymorphisms was reduced by cobicistat. No unexpected toxicities were observed. In in vitro, cobicistat enhanced azacitidine/venetoclax antileukemic effects. In conclusion, cobicistat enhances and optimizes venetoclax exposure, enabling an eightfold dose reduction while maintaining efficacy. The potentiated antileukemic activity positions cobicistat as a promising complementary agent in AML therapy. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06014489.


## Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common acute leukemia in adults. It is a clonal disorder of hematopoietic stem cells, characterized by uncontrolled growth of leukemic blasts, suppressing normal hematopoiesis. Venetoclax is a BCL-2 inhibitor that has efficacy in patients with AML when combined with a hypomethylating agent. The pivotal VIALE-A study established azacitidine/venetoclax as standard care for patients with newly diagnosed AML who are unfit for intensive chemotherapy. Azacitidine/venetoclax resulted in a median overall survival of 14.7 months compared with that of 9.6 months for azacitidine monotherapy.

Despite its clinical benefits, azacitidine/venetoclax comes with substantial treatment-associated costs. Strategies to reduce the costs of venetoclax could improve its affordability and accessibility. Venetoclax is extensively metabolized by cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) to multiple metabolites. The coadministration of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as ritonavir, ketoconazole, and posaconazole significantly enhances venetoclax exposure, increasing the reported area under the curve (AUC) up to 8.8-fold. Consequently, a venetoclax dose reduction of at least 75% is recommended by the manufacturer when coadministered with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. In the VIALE-A study, venetoclax dose was reduced from 400 mg to 50 mg when combined with voriconazole or posaconazole.

Pharmacokinetic boosting of venetoclax by CYP3A4 inhibition presents a strategy to allow significant dose reduction and eliminate the impact of CYP3A4 genetic polymorphisms on venetoclax exposure variation. Cobicistat is a potent and selective irreversible CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) inhibitor and is dosed at 150 mg once daily. Cobicistat’s potential as a pharmacokinetic booster has been demonstrated in combination with other drugs, including axitinib, tofacitinib, olaparib, and osimertinib. Posaconazole, also a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, is used in AML when antifungal prophylaxis is indicated. However, if no antifungal therapy is needed, cobicistat is a suitable alternative CYP3A4 inhibitor for venetoclax boosting, minimizing off-target effects and potential side effects.

CYP3A4 has been reported to be involved in chemoprotection across various leukemias. CYP3A4 mediates resistance to FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) inhibitors in vitro, and the inhibition of CYP3A4 was proposed to enhance the efficacy of FLT3-targeted drugs. In addition, CYP3A4 is involved in the protective effects of bone marrow (BM) stromal cells against bortezomib and etoposide in both multiple myeloma and leukemia models. The coadministration of cobicistat augmented the antileukemic activity of ibrutinib. These findings support the rationale to investigate the pharmacological impact of cobicistat in combination with azacitidine/venetoclax.

In this study, we explored the pharmacokinetic effects of coadministration of cobicistat in patients with AML treated with azacitidine/venetoclax. Furthermore, the potential of cobicistat to enhance the antileukemic activity of azacitidine/venetoclax was tested in vitro.


## Methods


### Study design and participants

This 2-stage, open-label, multicenter, phase 2 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06014489) consisted of a run-in phase with a crossover design followed by an extension phase. The run-in phase evaluated the pharmacokinetic equivalence of cobicistat-boosted venetoclax vs standard-dose venetoclax. The ongoing extension phase assesses the clinical feasibility, efficacy, and safety of cobicistat addition to azacitidine/venetoclax in adults with newly diagnosed AML, according to the International Consensus Classification (ICC) 2022 classification, who are ineligible or declined for intensive chemotherapy.

Patients requiring prophylaxis or treatment for invasive fungal disease were excluded from the run-in phase. Patients in the crossover run-in phase who completed cycles 1 and 2 were included in the pharmacokinetic evaluation. For the outcome evaluation, an intention-to-treat approach was used.

This study was conducted by the Dutch HOVON AML group and sponsored by HOVON. Ethics approval was obtained from the Medical Research Ethics Committee Foundation Beoordeling Ethiek Biomedisch Onderzoek (BEBO) (Assen, Netherlands) on 25 October 2023 (European Union Clinical Trial number 2023-505379-61-00). The trial adhered to the International Conference on Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. Independent monitoring ensured trial integrity and patient safety. Patients were enrolled at 7 investigational sites in the Netherlands. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This article describes the analysis of the run-in phase of the study.


### Procedures and treatment

During the crossover run-in phase, azacitidine was administered at a dose of 75 mg/m2 on days 1 to 7 or days 1 to 5 and 8 to 9 of each cycle. In the first 28-day cycle, venetoclax was initiated using a 3-day ramp-up schedule (100 mg on day 1, 200 mg on day 2, and 400 mg from day 3 onward). Venetoclax was continued for either 21 or 28 days, depending on the BM blast percentage assessed at day 21: patients with <5% blasts received 21 days of venetoclax, whereas those with ≥5% blasts received 28 days of venetoclax.

In cycle 2, cobicistat was introduced at a dose of 150 mg once daily, and the venetoclax dose was reduced to 50 mg once daily to account for CYP3A4 inhibition. In case of blast clearance, cycle 2 was initiated after blood count recovery, with a thrombocyte count of ≥50 × 109/L and neutrophil count of ≥0.5 × 109/L. Venetoclax + cobicistat was administered for 21 days if BM blasts were <5% after cycle 1, and cycle 2 was initiated before day 42. If cycle 2 began on day 42 or later, venetoclax + cobicistat was administered for 14 days. For patients with ≥5% BM blasts after cycle 1, cycle 2 was initiated from day 29 of cycle 1, and the duration of venetoclax + cobicistat in cycle 2 was determined by the BM blast percentage on day 21 of cycle 2. If blasts were <5% at that time, treatment was discontinued for the remainder of the cycle. Pharmacokinetic sampling was performed on day 14 (or alternatively on day 15 or 16) of cycles 1 and 2 to ensure steady-state conditions. All patients received the same crossover treatment sequence. Blood samples were collected predose and at 2, 4, 6, and 8 hours after the dose. Venetoclax concentrations at 24 hours after the dose were assumed equivalent to before the dose (0 hour) levels because sampling was performed at steady state. Venetoclax and cobicistat plasma concentrations were quantified using a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) assay. Pharmacogenetic testing was performed for each participant, analyzing polymorphisms in a standard gene panel consisting of ABCG2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, CYP3A4, CYP3A5, MTHFR, NUDT15, SLCO1B1, and VKORC1.


### End points

The primary end point of the crossover run-in phase was pharmacokinetic equivalence between cobicistat-boosted venetoclax and unboosted venetoclax, assessed by AUC over 24 hours (AUC0-24h) and maximum plasma concentration (Cmax). Equivalence was defined as geometric mean ratios (GMRs) >0.8 for AUC0-24h and Cmax and was based on the European Medicines Agency (EMA) bioequivalence guideline. Secondary end points included the complete remission (CR) rate and CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) according to European LeukemiaNet 2022, additional pharmacokinetic parameters, and toxicity. Adverse events (AEs) were graded using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.


### Statistics

GMRs for AUC0-24h, Cmax, and venetoclax trough concentration (Cmin) were log transformed and analyzed using the TOSTER package to assess bioequivalence. For pharmacogenetic comparisons, 1-way analysis of variance was performed to assess differences in venetoclax exposure across diplotype groups in either cycle 1 or 2. Group differences in pharmacogenetic box plots and in vitro assays were evaluated using unpaired 2-tailed t tests. Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between cobicistat AUC0-24h and Cmax with dose-normalized venetoclax AUC0-24h cycle 2/cycle 1 ratio. Pearson correlation was also applied to evaluate associations between venetoclax concentrations at different time points and venetoclax AUC0-24h. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2).


### Pharmacokinetic analysis and simulations

Noncompartmental pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated with the R packages NonCompart and ncar. Pharmacokinetic data were analyzed using nonlinear mixed effects modeling (NONMEM) (version 7.4.4), in conjunction with Perl-speaks-NONMEM (version 5.4.0), R (version 4.4.2), and RStudio (version 2024.09.0). A venetoclax population pharmacokinetic (popPK) model served as the basis for the analysis and was repurposed. The covariate effect of coadministration with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor was reestimated using data from this study. A reduction in the objective function value of 3.84, corresponding to a significance level of P < .05, was considered indicative of model improvement.

To evaluate venetoclax exposure under various dosing conditions, simulations were conducted using the final model under steady-state conditions. A total of 1000 virtual individuals per sex were simulated for each dosing regimen. Four clinically relevant dosing scenarios were selected for simulation: unboosted venetoclax at 400 mg once daily, venetoclax at 50 mg once daily with cobicistat at 150 mg once daily, venetoclax at 30 mg once daily with cobicistat at 150 mg once daily, and venetoclax at 20 mg once daily with cobicistat at 150 mg once daily. For each dosing regimen, venetoclax concentrations were simulated at individual time points, and the median, 5th percentile, and 95th percentile values were calculated and visualized. These distributions were subsequently visualized in concentration-time curves to enable comparative evaluation across regimens.


### In vitro methods


### Cell culture

OCI-AML2 and OCI-AML3 cell lines were cultured in minimum essential medium α supplemented with 20% fetal calf serum (FCS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). HepG2 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium with 10% FCS and 1% P/S. KG-1 cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco medium with 20% FCS and 1% P/S. All cultures were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.


### Patient specimens

Primary AML samples were obtained from the OncoLifeS data-biobank for Oncology (Dutch trial register: NL7839). In the OncoLifeS data-biobank, all patients were included after obtaining written informed consent, and the study protocol has been approved by the local institutional review board (University Medical Center Groningen Medical Research Ethics Committee 2010/109). Cells were cultured in minimum essential medium α supplemented with 25% FCS and 1% P/S.


### Drug screening and viability assays

OCI-AML2 cells were seeded at 20 000 cells per well in 96-well flat-bottom plates and treated with venetoclax (catalog no. C5160; Alsachim), 5-azacitidine (catalog no. A2385; Sigma Aldrich), cobicistat (catalog no. 23433; Cayman), posaconazole (catalog no. C3461; Alsachim), tariquidar (catalog no. SML1790; Sigma Aldrich), and voriconazole (catalog no. C2674; Alsachim), alone or in combination. After 24 hours, cell viability was assessed by flow cytometry using PE Annexin V (catalog no. 640908; BioLegend) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (catalog no. D9542; MilliporeSigma) staining. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (TreeStar Inc), and cell viability was calculated.

Drug synergy was assessed using the synergyfinder R package. Synergy scores were calculated using the Loewe additivity model. Positive synergy scores indicated synergistic interactions, whereas negative scores suggested antagonism.


### CYP3A4 expression and phenotype

To assess CYP3A4 expression, 300 000 cells were lysed in 1× Laemmli buffer (10% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 50% glycerol, 10% 2-mercaptoethanol, and 300mM Tris [pH 6.8]), heated at 100°C for 10 minutes, and loaded onto 4% to 20% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels. Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 0.45 μm) and probed overnight at 4°C with mouse monoclonal anti-CYP3A4 antibody (Invitrogen, MA5-17064, 1:1000) or rabbit monoclonal anti–glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Abcam, EPR16891, 1:1000). Secondary horseradish peroxidase–conjugated rabbit anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibody (1:10 000) was applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Detection was performed using SuperSignal West Dura substrate (catalog no. 34076; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc system.

To assess CYP3A4 activity, 1 million cells were treated with midazolam (Aurobindo, 5 mg/L ampoule), cobicistat, alone or in combination. Samples were collected after 24 and 48 hours. The formation of 1-hydroxy-midazolam was quantified via LC-MS/MS.

The predefined bioequivalence threshold (GMR >0.8) was reached for AUC0-24h and the Cmax. The coadministration of cobicistat with 50 mg of venetoclax resulted in a GMR of 2.0 (90% confidence interval, 1.4-2.8) for AUC0-24h and 1.4 (1.0-2.0) for Cmax, indicating a twofold increase in overall exposure and a 40% increase in peak concentration relative to 400 mg of unboosted venetoclax. Cobicistat reduced the apparent venetoclax clearance (CL/F) from 18.8 to 1.2 L/h, consistent with the potent inhibition of CYP3A-mediated metabolism (Table 2). The coefficients of variation (CV) for all pharmacokinetic parameters were lower in the cobicistat-boosted regimen, except for CL/F, which exhibited a higher CV than that in the unboosted condition.


### Pharmacokinetic analysis, modeling, and simulation

Cobicistat AUC0-24h and Cmin were not significantly correlated with dose-normalized venetoclax AUC0-24h ratio cycle 2/cycle 1, suggesting no dose-response relationship in the CYP3A4 inhibition effect of cobicistat (supplemental Figure 1). Venetoclax plasma concentrations at all measured time points were significantly correlated with venetoclax AUC0-24h in both treatment cycles (supplemental Figure 2). Notably, the strength of these correlations increased following cobicistat coadministration. For example, the trough concentration exhibited a Pearson correlation of r = 0.98 in cycle 2 compared with that of r = 0.91 in cycle 1, indicating that cobicistat-boosted venetoclax results in more predictable systemic exposure when sparse sampling is used (supplemental Figure 2).

By reestimating the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor covariate effect, the popPK model did improve significantly (P < .01). The reestimated covariate effect of cobicistat on the venetoclax clearance was 0.062 compared with that of 0.149 in the initial popPK model, meaning that cobicistat had a stronger effect on venetoclax clearance than the initial popPK model. Using cobicistat AUC0-24h or Cmax as a covariate on venetoclax clearance did not improve the popPK model, meaning that a higher cobicistat exposure does not have a higher CYP3A4-inhibiting effect. The goodness-of-fit plots are shown in supplemental Figure 3.

Among the tested polymorphisms, CYP3A4 ∗1/∗22 was significantly associated (P = .0013) with increased venetoclax AUC in the unboosted cycle (Figure 4A) but not in the boosted cycle (Figure 4B), indicating that cobicistat mitigates CYP3A4 genotype-dependent variability in venetoclax metabolism. Individual treatment responses and duration of response and treatment are depicted in Figure 5A with a median follow-up of 8.6 months. In the intention-to-treat population, 65% achieved a CR/CRi in the first 3 cycles. The responses per cycle are shown in Figure 5B. Among the 13 patients evaluable for pharmacokinetic analysis, 12 achieved CR, and 1 morphologic leukemia-free state, indicating a high response rate of 92% CR following the introduction of cobicistat in cycle 2. No unexpected toxicities were observed during the run-in phase. There were 38 AEs (grade ≥3) recorded for 17 patients; the rate of AEs was 85%. Most (39%) of the AEs were reported as infections. AEs per cycle are shown in supplemental Table 1. Median cycle duration was 35 days in cycle 1 and 32 days in cycle 2. Of the 6 deaths, 2 occurred while on protocol treatment. One patient died during cycle 1 because of sudden death not related to cytopenia, and the second patient died after 7.9 months on protocol from bacteremia, considered cytopenia related. The remaining 4 deaths occurred after treatment discontinuation during follow-up. Their subsequent clinical courses were heterogeneous and not consistent with cytopenia-related mortality: 1 patient received off-protocol chemotherapy and later died after relapse; 1 discontinued because of treatment refusal and received no further therapy; 1 required initiation of an unavoidable concomitant medication, resumed venetoclax-azacitidine during follow-up, relapsed, and died; and 1 started other antileukemic therapy, underwent off-protocol chemotherapy followed by allogeneic stem-cell transplantation, and died in CR from infection during neutrophil recovery.

Compared with azacitidine alone, azacitidine in combination with venetoclax significantly reduced the viability of OCI-AML2 cells (Figure 6A). Adding cobicistat to azacitidine/venetoclax showed at least a similar antileukemic effect as azacitidine/venetoclax. The numeric lower viability of the azacitidine/venetoclax/cobicistat combination did not reach statistical significance (Figure 6A). In contrast, Loewe synergy scores calculated for the azacitidine/venetoclax/cobicistat combination in various concentrations showed synergy of adding cobicistat already at the 0.5μM concentration level (Figure 6B). Similar results were observed with the addition of posaconazole (supplemental Figure 5).

In further experiments, the impact of various CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors was assessed. Cobicistat, posaconazole, tariquidar (a selective P-gp inhibitor), and voriconazole (a selective CYP3A4 inhibitor) did not exhibit any relevant antileukemic activity when used as monotherapy (supplemental Figure 5). The coadministration of both cobicistat, a CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitor, and tariquidar, a selective P-gp inhibitor, combined with azacitidine significantly reduced cell viability compared with that of azacitidine administration alone, whereas voriconazole and posaconazole had no observable effect (Figure 6C). These data suggest that the efficacy of azacitidine is boosted by P-gp inhibition. When focusing on venetoclax treatment without azacitidine in OCI-AML2 cells, cobicistat and posaconazole significantly reduced the viability compared with that of venetoclax monotherapy, whereas voriconazole and tariquidar had no effect (Figure 6D), suggesting that combined CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibition is important for in vitro boosting of venetoclax.

Western blot analysis showed that HepG2, OCI-AML2, OCI-AML3, and patient-derived leukemic cells all expressed CYP3A4 (Figure 6E). The midazolam metabolite 1-hydroxy-midazolam was formed in the HepG2 cells over time but to a much lesser extent by OCI-AML2, OCI-AML3, and patient-derived AML cells, suggesting that CYP3A4 activity in AML cells is low (Figure 6F). The formation of 1-hydroxy-midazolam was inhibited by cobicistat in HepG2 cells but not in the tested AML cell lines (Figure 6G), further suggesting that CYP3A4 inhibition is not the major mechanism of efficacy boosting of azacitidine/venetoclax.


## Discussion

In this run-in phase of the ongoing HOVON-171 phase 2 study, cobicistat coadministration with an eightfold reduced venetoclax dose resulted in increased and more consistent venetoclax exposure compared with standard dosing. Responses and AEs were consistent with those described in the VIALE-A trial. In vitro findings indicated enhanced antileukemic activity when cobicistat was combined with azacitidine/venetoclax. These findings indicate that pharmacokinetic enhancement with cobicistat may enable a dose reduction from 400 mg of venetoclax to 50 mg of venetoclax while preserving at least similar clinical efficacy.

Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that the cobicistat-boosted 50-mg venetoclax regimen resulted in higher Cmax and AUC0-24h than the standard 400-mg dose. These findings confirm the potency of cobicistat as a CYP3A4 inhibitor and its ability to enhance venetoclax exposure. The venetoclax exposure achieved with 50 mg of venetoclax + cobicistat was comparable with that reported for 50 mg of venetoclax coadministered with posaconazole. In clinical practice, venetoclax is often dosed at 70 or 100 mg when combined with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, which may result in higher venetoclax exposure and the risk of increased toxicity. High venetoclax exposure has been associated with severe neutropenia, prolonged duration of neutropenia, and a higher probability of infections, whereas real-world evidence does not demonstrate a consistent association with hematologic toxicity. The 50-mg venetoclax dose in combination with cobicistat aligns with the label recommendation to reduce venetoclax by at least 75% when coadministered with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. Real-world outcome data demonstrated that 50 mg of venetoclax combined with either voriconazole or posaconazole yields response rates comparable with those observed in the VIALE-A trial.

Cobicistat exposure was not associated with the CYP3A4-inhibiting effect on venetoclax exposure, suggesting no potential dose-response relationship in the CYP3A4 inhibition effect of cobicistat. The lack of improvement in the popPK model indicates that higher cobicistat exposure does not necessarily result in a stronger boosting effect on venetoclax clearance, supporting the hypothesis that 150 mg of cobicistat is sufficient for maximum CYP3A4 inhibition and that this cobicistat dose has a predictable pharmacokinetic boosting effect.

In addition to enhancing exposure, the cobicistat-boosted regimen demonstrated reduced interpatient variability, as reflected by lower CV in the key pharmacokinetic parameters AUC0-24h, Cmax, and Cmin. Although this suggests more consistent venetoclax exposure across patients, the clinical significance of reduced variability in AUC, Cmax, and Cmin remains unknown. Pharmacogenetic analysis showed that patients carrying the CYP3A4 ∗1/∗22 genotype, which is associated with reduced CYP3A4 enzymatic activity, exhibited the smallest difference in venetoclax exposure between boosted and unboosted conditions. This suggests that cobicistat coadministration reduces the venetoclax exposure variation between CYP3A4 ∗1/∗1 and ∗1/∗22 carriers and consequently optimizes venetoclax treatment for those with these polymorphisms.

The response rate of 65% observed in the intention-to-treat population of this study aligns with the 66.4% CR/CRi rate reported in the VIALE-A trial. The response rate of 92% in the patients treated with cobicistat is notably high. However, this subgroup consisted of patients who successfully completed the first 2 treatment cycles, thereby introducing potential selection bias that warrants cautious interpretation. In addition, patients in need of antifungal therapy were not eligible for inclusion in the pharmacokinetic analysis of the run-in phase. The safety profile observed in this initial cohort appears consistent with that reported in VIALE-A, suggesting that the azacitidine/venetoclax/cobicistat combination is safe. However, given the limited sample size in the run-in phase, definitive conclusions on efficacy and safety require confirmation in the ongoing expansion phase of the study.

Unlike other CYP3A4 inhibitors such as posaconazole and voriconazole, cobicistat lacks antifungal activity, thereby minimizing off-target effects. Cobicistat functions as an irreversible inhibitor of CYP3A4, distinguishing it from the reversible inhibition observed with azole antifungals. Venetoclax clearance decreases with increasing concentrations of posaconazole, reflecting the variable nature of reversible inhibition and thereby unpredictable effect on venetoclax exposure. Theoretically, the irreversible mechanism of cobicistat provides a more predictable and sustained suppression of CYP3A4 activity, leading to more consistent venetoclax exposure. The pharmacological specificity of cobicistat thus renders it a targeted and predictable enhancer of venetoclax exposure.

When treating AML cells in vitro, synergy was observed when cobicistat was combined with azacitidine/venetoclax. The coadministration of cobicistat to either azacitidine or venetoclax alone significantly reduced cell viability. Although CYP3A4 expression was detectable in leukemic cell lines, CYP3A4 enzymatic activity was low. Nevertheless, posaconazole and cobicistat were able to enhance the cytotoxic effect of venetoclax in leukemia cells. Tariquidar, a selective P-gp inhibitor, did not enhance venetoclax efficacy, indicating that the enhancing effect of cobicistat on venetoclax cytotoxicity is at least partly mediated by CYP3A4 inhibition.

Importantly, cobicistat also potentiated the antileukemic activity of azacitidine, despite azacitidine not being a known CYP3A4 substrate. This observation suggests that cobicistat exerts additional effects beyond CYP3A4 inhibition. Cobicistat is known to inhibit several ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, including P-gp (ABCB1) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP/ABCG2). These efflux transporters actively extrude drugs from cells and contribute to multidrug resistance. Efflux transporter inhibition by cobicistat may enhance intracellular azacitidine retention and potency, potentially explaining the observed synergistic effects with both venetoclax and azacitidine in vitro. This hypothesis is further supported by the finding that tariquidar, a potent P-gp inhibitor, significantly enhanced azacitidine-induced cytotoxicity. Overall, the in vitro data suggest that the potentiation of azacitidine by cobicistat is not mediated by CYP3A4 inhibition. Given that azacitidine is not considered a substrate for P-gp, BCRP, or MRP1, other efflux transporters may be involved. Previous studies have implicated additional ABC transporters, such as MRP4 (ABCC4), MRP5 (ABCC5), MRP7 (ABCC10), and MRP8 (ABCC11), in resistance to azacitidine and decitabine, suggesting that inhibition of these transporters by cobicistat or tariquidar explains the observed effects.

Given the observed increase in venetoclax exposure in combination with cobicistat, supported by our simulations indicating that venetoclax doses as low as 30 mg or even 20 mg may achieve adequate systemic exposure, and the potentiated antileukemic activity, it is conceivable that lower venetoclax doses could theoretically maintain efficacy while reducing toxicity. This hypothesis will be further explored in the ongoing extension phase, which evaluates the efficacy and tolerability of the azacitidine/venetoclax/cobicistat combination.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the pharmacokinetic crossover study was not randomized; all participants received the standard venetoclax dose of 400 mg before the reduced 50-mg dose combined with cobicistat, introducing potential sequence bias. Furthermore, the sample size was relatively small for the pharmacogenetic analysis, with only 13 patients included in the pharmacokinetic analysis, of whom 2 carried the CYP3A4 ∗1/∗22 genotype. This genotype has been associated with reduced CYP3A4 enzymatic activity and, in this study, appeared to correlate with higher venetoclax exposure and potential toxicity. A larger, genetically diverse cohort would be necessary to validate these observations and assess their clinical relevance.

Several considerations are relevant for implementing a pharmacokinetic boosting strategy in AML. In this patient population, antifungal prophylaxis or treatment can be required, and triazoles such as posaconazole, voriconazole, or isavuconazole may be administered. On days when posaconazole or voriconazole is given, cobicistat should be withheld, and venetoclax should be maintained at the 50-mg dose. When antifungal therapy is discontinued, cobicistat can be (re)introduced, with venetoclax continued at 50 mg (or reduced to 30 mg or 20 mg in cases of excessive toxicity). Isavuconazole requires a higher venetoclax dose of 200 mg (50% of the standard nonboosted dose). A broader challenge inherent to pharmacokinetic boosting strategies is the increased risk of drug-drug interactions (DDIs). Many patients with AML are on concomitant medications, and the introduction of a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor such as cobicistat may elevate the risk of clinically significant DDIs. However, the use of strong CYP3A4 inhibitors is already common practice in this population, particularly through the administration of triazole antifungals such as posaconazole or voriconazole. These agents share a similar interaction profile with cobicistat, suggesting that clinicians are generally familiar with managing such risks. To mitigate the risk of increased DDIs, careful medication review and monitoring are essential when implementing cobicistat-based regimens. A final limitation for broader implementation is that cobicistat tablets are not available in all countries. From other comparative studies, it is expected that ritonavir at 100 mg once daily can replace cobicistat for azacitidine/venetoclax boosting in settings where cobicistat is not available.


### Conclusions

All our findings support cobicistat as a feasible and effective pharmacological enhancer of azacitidine/venetoclax in AML. The eightfold venetoclax dose reduction, mitigated pharmacogenetic variability in exposure, and potentiated antileukemic activity position cobicistat as a promising complementary agent in AML therapy. Importantly, the use of cobicistat may also contribute to health care cost savings. The HOVON 171 study is still ongoing and is exploring cobicistat-boosted azacitidine/venetoclax in a larger cohort of patients with AML.
