# PPARA variant rs1800234 had a dose dependent pharmacogenetics impact on the therapeutic response to chiglitazar

## Abstract

**Background:** 
Our objective was to explore the pharmacogenetic impact of three known functional variants in drug target genes and determine whether they can explain the inter-individual variation in therapeutic response.

**Methods:** 
In a post hoc analysis of data from randomized controlled clinical trials of chiglitazar, we genotyped 481 Chinese patients with T2DM and investigated the association of variants in PPAR genes with the therapeutic outcome separated by dose using linear regression.

**Results:** 
rs1800234, a gain-of-function variant of PPARA, had a dose-dependent pharmacogenetic impact on the therapeutic response to chiglitazar. The C allele was significantly associated with reduced therapeutic response in the 48 mg group, while no significant association was observed in the 32 mg group. In addition, in patients without the C allele, patients treated with 48 mg chiglitazar had a better therapeutic response than those treated with 32 mg chiglitazar. To the contrary, in patients with the C allele, patients treated with 48 mg chiglitazar had a worse therapeutic response than those treated with 32 mg of chiglitazar.

**Conclusion:** 
The PPARA variant rs1800234 had a dose-dependent pharmacogenetic impact on the therapeutic response to chiglitazar. It could help explain the absence of a dose effect of chiglitazar and serve as a potential biomarker for the chosen dose of chiglitazar in the future. In addition, our study provided important reference for the design and clinical application of multi-target drugs.

## 1. Introduction

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are ligand-dependent transcription factors known for their crucial roles in glucose and lipid metabolism [[1](#cit0001)]. Thiazolidinediones (TZDs), one PPARG agonist, are potent insulin sensitizers with side effects such as weight gain, edema, bone fractures, and congestive heart failure [[2–4](#cit0002)]. Chiglitazar, a novel PPAR pan-agonist, exibits moderate but balanced activity for all three PPAR subtypes [[5](#cit0005),[6](#cit0006)]. Phase III clinical trials have demonstrated that chiglitazar significantly improved glycemic control and insulin sensitivity in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), while it also reduced triglyceride and free fatty acid levels [[7](#cit0007),[8](#cit0008)]. Moreover, chiglitazar users experienced fewer PPAR agonist-associated side effects than TZDs, with similar frequencies to the placebo group [[7](#cit0007),[8](#cit0008)].

As with other antidiabetic agents, considerable inter-individual variation exists in glycemic response to chiglitazar [[7](#cit0007),[8](#cit0008)]. Previous studies have shown that a loss-of-function variant (rs1801282) in the drug target gene PPARG was associated with enhanced glycemic response to TZDs [[9](#cit0009)]. In addition, rs1800234, a gain-of function SNP of PPARA, was associated with lower triglycerides [[10](#cit0010)]. rs6902123, a intron variant of PPARD, was associated with higher glycemic levels [[11](#cit0011)]. These suggest that functional genetic variants in the three drug target genes of chiglitazar might also contribute to variable responses among individuals.

Although phase III clinical trials have established the effectiveness and safety of chiglitazar, they did not observe a significant correlation between the drug dose and glycemic response [[7](#cit0007),[8](#cit0008)]. Surprisingly, patients treated with a higher dose of 48 mg chiglitazar did not exhibit a greater glycemic response compared to those in the 32 mg group, despite pharmacokinetic studies revealing significantly higher plasma levels of chiglitazar in the 48 mg group [[12](#cit0012)].

In this study, we conducted a post hoc analysis of the phase III clinical trials of chiglitazar. Our objective was to explore the pharmacogenetic impact of three known functional variants in drug target genes and determine whether they can explain the inter-individual variation in therapeutic response, as well as the lack of a significant dose–response correlation.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Sample ascertainment

Patients were ascertained from the chiglitazar phase III clinical trials, which has previously been described in detail [[7](#cit0007),[8](#cit0008)]. In brief, eligible T2DM patients with an glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level of 7.5%–10.0% at screening who met the study criteria after the placebo run-in were randomly allocated (1:1) to receive oral chiglitazar (32 mg) and chiglitazar (48 mg) for 24 weeks. The general conditions and biochemical indicators of the patients were measured before and after treatment. Biochemical indicators included body mass index (BMI), HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), postprandial blood glucose (PBG), triglycerides (TG), and so on. Whole blood samples were collected at baseline for DNA extraction. Patients who met the following conditions were included: previous untreated T2DM (no oral or injected anti-diabetes treatment for 12 weeks before randomization), aged 18–70 years, and BMI 18.5–35.0 kg/m^2^. On the basis of the study, samples were excluded for any other of the following reasons: no genotype data, incomplete study, baseline, or 24-week FBG missing. Finally, 481 patients were included. All participants provided informed written consent.

### 2.2. Drug response definitions

Therapeutic response is measured by baseline and 24-week characteristics. It should be noted that HbA1c was measured before the placebo run-in. This might result in HbA1c reduction being affected by confounding factors in the placebo run-in. Based on this, individuals’ glycemic response was modeled as baseline FBG minus 24-week FBG. Individuals’ lipid response was modeled as baseline TG minus 24-week TG. The multivariate linear model equation for these two outcomes is as follows: FBG (TG) reduction ~ baseline FBG (TG) + age + sex + baseline BMI + genotype. Reductions of other characteristics were also calculated as baseline values minus 24-week values.

### 2.3. Genotyping

In our study, we overall genotyped three functional variants in the PPAR genes. Whole genomic DNA was extracted by WuXi Apptec Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) according to the standard protocols. PCR primers and primer extension probes were designed using AssayDesigner3.1 software (CA, USA). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were genotyped using the Taqman-based allelic discrimination (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Fluorescence detection and genotype calling were performed using an ABI 7900FastHT sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems).

### 2.4. Statistical analysis

Baseline data were presented as the number (percentage) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was carried out with PLINK [[13](#cit0013)]. Multiple linear regression analyses of FBG and TG reduction were performed with PLINK under an additive genetic model with all the covariates included. Differences in therapeutic response by genotypes were detected by one-way analysis of variance. Statistical analyses were performed with *R*-4.2.3 software.

In the 481 patients studied, the allele frequencies of PPAR variants were showed in [Table 1](#t0001). All of the SNPs were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in the sample (*p* value > 0.05) and were not associated with baseline FBG or TG (*p* value > 0.05).

### 3.3. Gene-dose grouping

During above screening process, we found that rs1800234 had a dose-dependent pharmacogenetic impact on the therapeutic response to chiglitazar. The C allele of rs1800234 was significantly associated with reduced FBG reduction and TG reduction in the 48 mg group, while no significant association was observed in the 32 mg group (p for interacion < 0.001). To better assess the impact of rs1800234 in chiglitazar response, we divided the patients into four groups based on the dose and genotype ([Figure 1](#f0001)). We found that 48 mg had better FBG reduction than 32 mg in patients without the C allele at rs1800234, with a mean of 1.84 ± 1.96 mmol/L versus 1.36 ± 1.92 mmol/L (*p* value = 0.012). This was the dose effect we expected. In addition, in patients without the C allele, 48 mg had better PBG reduction, TG reduction, and insulin sensitivity improvement than 32 mg (Supplementary Table S3). However, among the 27 patients with the C allele in the 48 mg treatment group, the mean of FBG reduction was only 0.47 ± 1.62 mmol/L, and the difference from the mean of patients without the C allele was 1.37 mmol/L (*p* value <0.001). More interestingly, 48 mg had worse FBG reduction than 32 mg in patients carrying the C allele (*p* value = 0.038), which was the opposite of what we would expect from a dose effect. In overall patients, the FBG reduction in the 48 mg treatment group was not significantly higher than that in the 32 mg treatment group, with mean 1.69 ± 1.97 mmol/L versus 1.37 ± 1.91 mmol/L (*p* value = 0.078, Supplementary Figure S1). Our result indicated that the absence of significant dose effects of chiglitazar might be due to the worst FBG reduction in the 27 patients with the C allele in the 48 mg treatment group.

## 4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study on the pharmacogenomics of chiglitazar. We observed better glycemic response in older age women and those with increasing obesity. Surprisingly, we found that rs1800234 in PPARA was associated with therapeutic response to chiglitazar in the 48 mg treatment group, while no significant association was observed in the 32 mg treatment group. Among the Chinese individuals with T2DM, the rs1800234 variant was significantly associated with lower FBG and TG reduction after 24-week chiglitazar monotherapy in the 48 mg group. After dividing the patients into four groups based on the dose and genotype, we found that 48 mg had better therapeutic response than 32 mg in patients without the C allele at rs1800234, which was the dose effect we expected. However, 48 mg had worse therapeutic response in patients carrying the C allele, which was the opposite of what we expected from a dose effect. These results indicated that the absence of significant dose effects of chiglitazar might be due to the worst FBG reduction in the 27 patients with the C allele in the 48 mg treatment group.

The biological mechanisms underlying the documented why patients with the C allele in the 48 mg treatment group had the worst FBG reduction might be the overactivation of PPARA. The activation capacity of chiglitazar to targets was different, with EC_50_ values of 1.1 μmol/L for PPARA and 1.7 μmol/L for PPARD [[5](#cit0005)]. In the previous pharmacokinetic study, the plasma level of chiglitazar was approximately 1.1 μmol/L at a dose of 32 mg, while it was 1.7 μmol/L at a dose of 48 mg in humans [[14](#cit0014)]. rs1800234 was a gain-of-function variant of PPARA [[15](#cit0015),[16](#cit0016)]. These evidences indicated that PPARA might be hyperactivated in patients carrying the C allele of rs1800234 treated with 48 mg chiglitazar. Previous studies have found that overexpression of PPARA might suppress insulin secretion [[17](#cit0017)]. In our study, we did find that patients with the C allele in the 48 mg group had a worse HOMAB index after treatment. This might lead to a decrease in the glycemic response to insulin sensitizers. In addition, other studies have reported that high doses of fibrates, a PPARA agonist, could lead to less drug effect [[18–21](#cit0018)]. In our study, we found that patients carrying the C allele of rs1800234 received less TG reduction and had a higher TG level after taking 48 mg chiglitazar. Accumulation of triglycerides could result in more insulin resistance, leading to higher glycemic levels [[22](#cit0022)].

Our study provided important reference for the design of dual-target and multi-target drugs. Due to the synergistic or additional effects of different targets, dual-target and multi-target drugs have higher efficacy and safety [[23–25](#cit0023)]. Studies have reported that one challenge of the rational design of multitarget ligands with predefined biological profiles was that researchers must deal with the crucial issue of activation capacity balance toward different target proteins [[23–25](#cit0023)]. To activate targets that were more difficult to be activated, a higher dose is usually required, which might affect the action of other targets. However, few studies have reported the outcomes when the activation capacity was unbalanced. Our study provided evidence that an imbalance activation capacity could result in a poor therapeutic response. Chiglitazar has a low activation capacity for PPARD [[5](#cit0005)], and only 48 mg chiglitazar could activate PPARD [[14](#cit0014)]. This resulted in an overactivation of PPARA in the patients with the C allele in the 48 mg treatment group, leading to a decreased overall therapeutic response, while this effect was not compensated by the PPARG and PPARD pathways. This result suggested that scientists should pay more attention to balancing the activation capacity toward different targets when designing dual-target and multi-target drugs, avoiding the overactivation of one target that might lead to a poor therapeutic response. In addition, our study provided reference for the clinical application of dual-target and multi-target drugs. Patients carrying the C allele of rs1800234 had a higher basal activity of PPARA [[16](#cit0016)]. Our study found that when these patients received 48 mg of chiglitazar, the therapeutic response might be reduced due to the overactivation of PPARA. This result suggested that in the clinical application of dual-target or multi-target drugs, the genetic background of patients should be considered, focusing on patients carrying the gain of function variant of target genes, which were easier to be activated to avoid decreased overall therapeutic response due to the overactivation of the one target.

Our study provided evidence that gene polymorphisms had a dose-dependent pharmacogenetic impact on the therapeutic response to drugs. In this way, the optimal dose can be selected through the genotype as a biomarker to achieve the best drug efficacy. In our study, we found 48 mg had better therapeutic response than 32 mg in patients without the C allele at rs1800234, while 48 mg had worse therapeutic response in patients carrying the C allele. These results suggested that rs1800234 might be a candidate biomarker for the chosen dose of chiglitazar. In addtion, our study provided a future direction for the pharmacogenomics of hypoglycemic agents. Through the dose-dependent pharmacogenetics impact, appropriate dose could be selected according to the patients’ genotype to achieve a better therapeutic response.

It should be noted that the frequency of the rs1800234 C allele is 0.1% in European [[26](#cit0026)] while 4.3% in East Asian [[27](#cit0027)] and 6.1% in our study. This difference in allele frequency suggested that more attention should be paid to the effect of the rs1800234 variant in the East Asian population. In our study, 27 participants (10.98%) received worse therapeutic response after 24-week chiglitazar monotherapy in the 48 mg group, indicating that the rs1800234 variant could serve as a potential biomarker for personalized treatment of chiglitazar especially for the East Asian population.

Our study had some limitations. Firstly, our study used a small sample size, which might result in insufficient statistical power. In addition, chiglitazar would be used in combination with first-line drugs like metformin in the real world. More evidences or in vitro experiments are needed for the conclusion of this study. However, our results were derived from chiglitazar monotherapy, eliminating the potential interference of other drugs. Furthermore, our data came from clinical trials, with stricter inclusion and exclusion standards, more standardized trial procedures, and accurate measurement values. All of these enable us to have higher quality and clean data, which ensure the accuracy of our results.

## 5. Conclusion

In conclusion, we observed that the PPARA variant rs1800234 had a dose-dependent pharmacogenetic impact on the therapeutic response to chiglitazar in T2DM patients from the Chinese population. This variant might be a candidate biomarker for the chosen dose of chiglitazar. In addition, our study provided important reference for the design and clinical application of dual-target and multi-target drugs.
