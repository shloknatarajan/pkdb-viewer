## Abstract

Clopidogrel therapy is recommended to prevent recurrent ischemic stroke. However, clopidogrel resistance exists. This study examines which enzymes are involved in clopidogrel resistance. The study group includes 402 neurology patients who had experienced a recurrent ischemic stroke while receiving clopidogrel monotherapy. Patients were genotyped for CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2B6, CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP1A2, ABCB1 and CES1A1. Phenotype frequencies were compared with those of a Global(G), European(E) and a local patient control(C) group. Only for ABCB1 a significant difference in phenotype distribution was seen (pG = 0.003, pE = <0.001, pC = 0.019). In patient with ABCB1 rapid efflux activity (3435TT), no other enzyme was significantly associated. In patients with ABCB1 normal efflux activity (3435CT/CC), CYP2C19 intermediate and poor metabolism were significantly associated (pE = <0.001, pG = <0.001, pC = 0.002). In patients with normal CYP2C19 metabolism, CYP2C9 intermediate and poor metabolism were significantly associated (pG = <0.001, pE = <0.001, pC = <0.001). Besides CYP2C19 clinical guidelines, also for ABCB1 and CYP2C9 guidelines are needed to prevent clopidogrel resistance.

Subject terms: Genetics research, Neurological disorders

## Introduction

For neurology patients who have experienced a minor ischemic stroke or a transient ischemic attack (TIA), the risk of a recurrent ischemic stroke ranges from 3.7% to 5.1% within 90 days or one year after the event, respectively [[1](#CR1)]. For many years now, especially clopidogrel has been the preferred antiplatelet drug for preventing ischemic stroke recurrence, worldwide [[2](#CR2), [3](#CR3)]. However, despite its wide use, clopidogrel resistance is common: 16–50% of patients undergoing vascular or coronary interventions do not respond adequately to clopidogrel [[4](#CR4), [5](#CR5)].

Clopidogrel is a non-functional prodrug that needs to be converted into its active metabolite (Fig. [1](#Fig1)) [[6](#CR6)–[9](#CR9)]. Genetic variations in CYP2C19 affecting the enzyme activity, result in a reduced or increased effectiveness of clopidogrel. Although meta-analyses were required to support this, evidence suggests that tailoring antiplatelet therapy based on pharmacogenetic testing for CYP2C19 loss-of-function (LOF) polymorphisms can help to optimize patient outcomes, especially in high-risk cardio- and neurovascular patients [[10](#CR10)–[17](#CR17)]. Currently, various clinical guidelines recommend adjusting antiplatelet therapy based on CYP2C19 genotyping to ensure optimal treatment outcomes and reduce adverse effects (Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC), Dutch Pharmacogenomics Working Group (DPWG), French National Network of Pharmacogenetics (RNPGx), [www.ClinPGx.org](http://www.ClinPGx.org)).

Clopidogrel is rapidly absorbed by the intestines, where the ATP binding cassette (ABC) transporter ABCB1 is involved in effluxing part of it back into the lumen. In the liver, clopidogrel is mainly (~85%) metabolized by carboxylesterase 1 (CES1A1) into the inactive clopidogrel carboxylic acid. The remaining 15% is first metabolized into an intermediate metabolite, 2-oxo-clopidogrel, followed by metabolism into various thiol stereoisomers, of which only one, the H4, retains biological activity [6–8]. Both metabolic steps to form H4 are predominantly dependent on cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2C19 activity [8] but CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP2C9 and CYP1A2 could also play a role [9]. Cytochrome P450 (CYP), ATP binding cassette (ABC) transporter, carboxylesterase (CES), uridine diphosphate glucuronyltransferase (UGT), paraoxonase 1 (PON), purinergic receptor (P2Y), glutathione (GSH).

However, the most common CYP2C19*2 LOF allele only explains part (~12%) of the reduced platelet response to clopidogrel [[18](#CR18)] suggesting that other enzymes involved in the clopidogrel metabolism need to be considered in the clinical outcome of clopidogrel therapy. Some studies suggested a significant role for CYP2B6, as carriers of a reduced function CYP2B6 allele tended to have a lower plasma exposure to the active metabolite of clopidogrel, but no significant association with the primary efficacy outcome was observed [[19](#CR19)–[21](#CR21)]. Other studies showed a significantly decreased conversion of clopidogrel into the active metabolite for CYP2C9 LOF alleles [[22](#CR22), [23](#CR23)] but for LOF carriers no increased risk for thrombotic events was seen, compared to noncarriers [[24](#CR24)]. Also, the ABCB1 3435 TT variant was shown to be associated with a reduced platelet aggregation inhibition and higher event rates in patients using clopidogrel [[25](#CR25), [26](#CR26)] but a significant involvement in the clinical outcome of cardiovascular patients was not always seen [[27](#CR27)]. To date, for CYP1A2, no convincing evidence was found for an important role in the clinical outcome of patients using clopidogrel. Although for CES1A1, the LOF variant, G143E, was shown to result in an increased formation of the active metabolite of clopidogrel in vitro, and increased response to clopidogrel, its impact on the clinical outcome is still unclear [[28](#CR28), [29](#CR29)].

This study investigates the association between polymorphic enzymes involved in clopidogrel metabolism, and recurrent ischemic stroke in neurological patients. To investigate this, phenotype frequencies of different enzymes of the study (sub)group are compared with those of three reference groups.

## Methods

### Study population

From November 2020 through November 2023, 402 consecutive neurology patients from Catharina Hospital Eindhoven, who had experienced a recurrent TIA or ischemic stroke while receiving clopidogrel monotherapy (75 mg/day) were included on suspicion of clopidogrel resistance. There were no exclusion criteria. The study was approved as ‘non-WMO research’ by the medical research ethics committees united (MEC-U, W25.209) in the Netherlands. All patients were genotyped for CYP2C19 as part of routine diagnostics within a week after the event. In addition, genotyping was performed for CYP2C9, CYP2B6, CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP1A2, ABCB1 and CES1A1. To limit the number of groups to be compared, phenotype frequencies were determined instead of allele frequencies. Phenotypes were assigned, based on the genotypes according to the assignment described by ClinPGx ([https://www.clinpgx.org/](https://www.clinpgx.org/)). Patient data has been anonymized. No patient records were consulted.

### Reference groups

Allele frequencies accompanying the rs-number of the variants for which genotyping was performed, were retrieved from the NIH reference SNP report (National Institute of Health (NIH), National Library of Medicine, National Center for Biotechnology Information) for both the global and European populations. The average group size per variant was approximately 150000 (Global:18890-353904, median 181168, Europe: 14286-303112, median=159002). For the Global and Europe reference groups, phenotype frequencies were calculated based on the allele frequencies of the variants tested.

A third reference group (Control group), was formed based on pharmacogenetics profiles from local patients. From January 2024 to May 2025, 1056 pharmacogenetics profiles from all patients, except neurology and cardiology patients, were listed for CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2B6, CYP3A4, CYP1A2, ABCB1 and CES1A1 genotype, and phenotypes were determined in a comparable way as performed for the study population. This Control group did not differ significantly from the Europe and Global reference group (pE = 0.33, pG = 0.82) reflecting a normal population. Data has been anonymized. No patient records were consulted.

### Genotyping

Genotyping was performed using the Quantstudio 12 K flex (Thermo Fisher Scientific©, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) and OpenArray technology using custom-made panels (Thermo Fisher Scientific©) including CYP2C19*2, *3, *4, *8 (rs4244285, rs4986893, rs28399504, rs41291556, LOF) and CYP2C19*17 (rs12248560, gain of function (GOF)), CYP2C9*2, *3 (rs1799853, rs1057910, LOF), CYP2B6*4 (rs2279343, GOF) and CYP2B6*6 (rs3745274, LOF), CYP3A4*22 (rs35599367, LOF), CYP3A5*3 (rs776746, LOF), CYP1A2*1 C, *1 K (rs2069514, rs12720461, LOF), CES1A1 Gly143Glu (rs71647871, LOF), ABCB1 C3435T (rs1045642, GOF).

### Statistics

Phenotype frequencies were compared and Chi-square based p-values were obtained by using SPSS, (version 29, IBM©, Armonk, New York, United States). P-value below 0.05 was considered to be significant. For ‘overall significantly different’ all three p-values (p-value Europe group (pE), p-value Global group (pG), p-value Control group (pC)) had to be below 0.05. All p-values were checked using R (version 4.5.1) for p-Chi square and p-Fisher, using the exact group sizes for all groups.

The study population includes 54% men and 46% women with an average age of 74 years, with common characteristics for this type of patients (Table [1](#Tab1)) [[30](#CR30)]. Genotyping was performed for the most interesting enzymes (Fig. [1](#Fig1)) and accompanying variants involved in the metabolism of clopidogrel, and phenotype frequencies were determined (Table [2](#Tab2)).

### Total-study group

For all enzymes tested, phenotype frequencies from the study group were compared with those of the Global-, Europe- and local Control reference group (Table [3](#Tab3) and supplement Table [1](#MOESM1), column 1). The CYP2C19 phenotype distribution in the study group was significantly different from that of the ethnically close to similar Europe reference group (pE = 0.018). This is in agreement with the described important role of CYP2C19 in clopidogrel metabolism. However, the CYP2C19 phenotype distribution of the study group was only near significantly different from that of the ethnically more distinct Global group (pG = 0.063), and not significantly different from that of the Control group based on local people (pC = 0.139), despite a clear increase in CYP2C19 poor metabolizers (PM) seen in the study group compared to all three reference groups. This could be due to the smaller size of the Control group.

Interestingly, the phenotype distribution of the ABCB1 3435C > T variant of the total-study group was significantly different from that of the Europe group (pE = <0.001) and Global group (pG=0.012) and was near significantly different from the Control group (pC = 0.055). The most prevalent genotype for ABCB1 3435 is CT, which is linked to a normal efflux activity (NA). The 3435TT genotype is linked to a rapid efflux activity (RA) and the 3435CC genotype might have a normal or intermediate (decreased) efflux activity (IA). For the total-study group, a clear increase in RA, and a decrease of NA and IA was seen compared to all reference groups. Combining the NA and IA group (NA + IA), accepting both as normal efflux activity, resulted in an overall significantly different phenotype distribution in the total-study group compared to all three reference groups (pG = 0.003, pE = <0.001, pC = 0.019). This suggests that ABCB1 RA could play an important role in clopidogrel resistance.

Surprisingly, by analysing the other enzymes involved in the metabolism of clopidogrel (CYP2C9, CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP1A2, CES1A1) (Table [3](#Tab3), supplement Table [1](#MOESM1)), a difference in phenotype distribution was only seen for CYP2C9, with a clear increase of CYP2C9 IM and PM patients in the study group compared to all three reference groups. This difference in CYP2C9 phenotype distribution was significant compared to the Global and Control groups (pG = 0.025, pC = 0.010), but not compared to the Europe group (pE = 0.325). Yet, this could indicate a role for CYP2C9 in the clinical outcome of the study population.

### ABCB1 normal and intermediate activity-study subgroup

Because of the important role for ABCB1 shown in this study, CYP2C19 phenotype distribution in a study subgroup including ABCB1 NA + IA only (n = 277, Table [3](#Tab3), column 2) was analysed, and was shown to be significantly different from that of all three reference groups (pE = 0.002, pG = 0.006, pC = 0.015). Limiting the selection to ABCB1 NA patients only (n = 184, Table [3](#Tab3), column 3), this significance became even stronger: a clear increase of CYP2C19 intermediate metabolizers (IM) and PM, and a decrease of rapid metabolizers (RM) is seen (pE = <0.001, pG = <0.001, pC = 0.002). This shows that, at normal ABCB1 activity, reduced CYP2C19 activity is indeed strongly associated with a recurrent ischemic stroke for neurology patients using clopidogrel.

Next, CYP2C9 phenotype distribution in the ABCB1 NA + IA study subgroup (n = 277, Table [3](#Tab3), column 2) was compared with that of all three reference groups, and was shown to be significantly different from that of the Global and Control group (pG = 0.018, pC = 0.015), but again not significantly different from that of the Europe group (pE = 0.188), which is comparable to the results of the total-study group. However, by further limiting the study subgroup to ABCB1 NA patients only (n = 184, Table [3](#Tab3), column 3), for CYP2C9 significance was lost compared to all three reference groups (pG = 0.110, pE = 0.458, pC = 0.067). This shows that, at normal ABCB1 activity, CYP2C9 activity is a less important factor than CYP2C19 activity for preventing recurrent ischemic stroke.

For the other enzymes analysed, a significant difference in phenotype distribution compared to the three reference groups could not be established for the ABCB1 NA + IA and ABCB1 NA subgroups (supplement Table [1](#MOESM1)).

### ABCB1 rapid activity-study subgroup

Interestingly, by selection of ABCB1 RA patients only (n = 125, Table [3](#Tab3) and supplement Table [1](#MOESM1), column 4), the phenotype distribution of none of the enzymes studied showed any significant difference compared to all three reference groups. This strongly suggests that ABCB1 RA itself is an important factor associated with clinical outcome in neurology patients using clopidogrel to prevent thrombotic events.

### CYP2C19 normal, rapid and ultrarapid metabolizer-study subgroup

To rule out any influence of decreased CYP2C19 activity, known to be involved in clopidogrel resistance, a study subgroup, including only CYP2C19 normal (NM), rapid (RM) and ultrarapid metabolizers (UM) only (n = 279, Table [3](#Tab3) and supplement Table [1](#MOESM1), column 5), associated with normal to high clopidogrel efficiency, was analysed. For both ABCB1 phenotype distributions described above, results of the CYP2C19 NM + RM + UM subgroup were overall significantly different from those of the reference groups (pE = <0.001, pG = 0.001, pC = 0.008 for ‘NA, IA and RA’, and pE = <0.001, pG = <0.001, pC = 0.002 for ‘NA + IA and RA’), with a clear increase in ABCB1 RA patients. This supports that, for CYP2C19 NM, RM and UM patients, ABCB1 RA is an important factor in clopidogrel resistance. However, by selection of only CYP2C19 NM patients (n = 169, Table [3](#Tab3), column 6), significant difference was still seen compared to the Global and Europe group (pG = 0.029, pE = 0.006), but was lost compared to the Control group (pC = 0.075). As the percentage of RA (31.95%) in this subgroup was even slightly higher than that of the total-study group (31.09%), this was probably due of the smaller group size of the subgroup.

Interestingly, also, for CYP2C9, phenotype distribution in the CYP2C19 NM + RM + UM subgroup (n = 279, Table [3](#Tab3), column 5) was overall significantly different from that of all three reference groups (pG = <0.001, pE = 0.003, pC = <0.001), with a clear increase of CYP2C9 IM and PM patients. Interestingly, for the subgroup including only CYP2C19 NM patients (n = 169, Table [3](#Tab3), column 6), significance became even stronger (pG = <0.001, pE = <0.001, pC = <0.001) based on a further increase of the frequency of CYP2C9 IM and PM patients. This shows that especially for CYP2C19 NM patients, the CYP2C9 activity is of significant importance for clinical outcome for patients using clopidogrel to prevent recurrence of an ischemic stroke, while for CYP2C19 RM and UM patients this might be less.

In line with the above, by selecting CYP2C19 NM + ABCB1 NA patients from the study group (n = 76, Table [3](#Tab3), column 7), a significantly different phenotype distribution for CYP2C9 was still seen compared to all reference groups (pG = <0.001, pE = <0.001, pC = <0.001), again caused by a further increase of CYP2C9 IM and PM patients. This shows that also for CYP2C19 NM patients with normal ABCB1 activity, reduced CYP2C9 activity has a negative effect on clinical outcome.

For the other enzymes analysed, no significant difference in phenotype distribution compared to the three reference groups was seen in the CYP2C19 UM + RM + NM subgroup (supplement Table [1](#MOESM1)).

### CYP2C9 normal metabolizer-study subgroup

To further investigate the role of CYP2C9 in clopidogrel resistance, phenotype distribution of a subgroup of only CYP2C9 NM patients (n = 255, Table [3](#Tab3) and supplement Table [1](#MOESM1), column 8) was investigated. Significant differences in CYP2C19 phenotype distribution were observed for this CYP2C9 NM selection compared with all reference groups (pG = 0.011, pE = 0.008, pC = 0.005), with a clear increase of CYP2C19 IM and PM patients. So, also for CYP2C9 NM patients, reduced CYP2C19 activity has a negative effect on clinical outcome.

In addition, the ABCB1 phenotype distribution in CYP2C9 NM patients was significantly different compared to that of the three reference groups (pG = 0.009, pE = <0.001, pC = 0.034), due to a clear increase of ABCB1 RA patients in this subgroup. This again supports the important role of ABCB1 activity in the clinical outcome of the patients in the study subgroup.

## Discussion

Over the years, numerous studies have explored the clinical relevance of CYP2C19 genotyping in optimizing clopidogrel therapy [[19](#CR19), [25](#CR25), [26](#CR26), [31](#CR31), [32](#CR32)]. Despite these studies, a multicentre study and meta-analysis including high number of patients were needed to find proof that CYP2C19 genotype guided therapy could improve patient outcomes [[10](#CR10), [11](#CR11)]. However, till today, clinical guidelines on its widespread implementation remain inconsistent [[3](#CR3)].

Recently, the results of platelet activity testing during clopidogrel treatment of patients undergoing vascular or coronary interventions, showed that heightened platelet reactivity (HPR) is more common (more than 50%) than initially thought [[5](#CR5)]. Since CYP2C19 LOF may only account for approximately 12% of clopidogrel resistance, it is assumed that multiple factors contribute to clopidogrel resistance. Very recently, a polygenetic risk score (PRS) was presented, based on a post hoc analysis of 16 single nucleotide polymorphisms of 7 genes derived from patient of the CHANCE (Clopidogrel in High-Risk Patients with Acute Nondisabling Cerebrovascular Events) trial, showing that a high PRS was associated with reduced efficacy of clopidogrel-aspirin dual therapy compared to aspirin alone [[33](#CR33)].

In the current cohort study, a significant increase of ABCB1 3435TT carriers, associated with a rapid efflux activity, was observed, compared to three reference groups. Furthermore, it is shown that in ABCB1 3435TT carriers, the impaired activity of no other enzyme studied, including CYP2C19, can explain a recurrent ischemic stroke while using clopidogrel. Previously, for coronary patients, Simon and colleagues [[25](#CR25)] presented a higher rate of cardiovascular events within 1 year after PCI while treated with clopidogrel, for patients with the ABCB1 3435 TT genotype compared to those with the ABCB1 3435 CC genotype. Also, Mega and colleagues [[26](#CR26)] found that ABCB1TT carriers had an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and lower platelet aggregation inhibition compared to CT/CC individuals. Although these findings have not always been replicated, more recent studies have shown significant associations of the ABCB1 3435 TT variant with poor clopidogrel response and significantly lower serum concentrations of the active metabolite compared with 3435 CT and CC patients [[34](#CR34)–[36](#CR36)]. As the results presented in this paper are in agreement with those for coronary patients, it is suggested that also for neurology patients, the ABCB1 3435TT variant plays a major role in clopidogrel resistance.

For the total-study group, the evidence for a significant role for CYP2C19 appears limited compared to our Control group, possibly because the size of the Control group is too small for direct proof. However, for the subgroup of ABCB1 3435CT patients, with normal efflux activity, the CYP2C19 LOF allele is highly significantly associated with a recurrent ischemic stroke during clopidogrel use. This is in agreement with previous data from meta-analyses [[11](#CR11)], proving that CYP2C19 is the main enzyme responsible for metabolizing clopidogrel into its active metabolite.

For CYP2C9, the significance in the total-study group is also weak. However, particularly in patients with CYP2C19 NM, a highly significant increase in CYP2C9 LOF carriers is observed, showing that CYP2C9 activity is strongly associated with the clinical outcome of CYP2C19 NM patients taking clopidogrel for the prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke. Previously, CYP2C9 LOF has been associated with lower levels of clopidogrel active metabolite, reduced pharmacodynamic response and increased risk for stent thrombosis [[22](#CR22), [23](#CR23)]. Remarkably, in more recent studies, no increased risk of thrombotic events was observed in CYP2C9 LOF carriers compared with noncarriers [[24](#CR24), [33](#CR33)].

In the current study, in addition to the total-study group, subgroups based on the activity of one specific enzyme (e.g. ABCB1, CYP2C19 and CYP2C9) were analysed to exclude the influence of these enzymes on the analysis. Only in these subgroups the impact of the individual enzymes was evident. This could explain why some previous studies failed to find a significant association with clinical outcomes.

Based on our results only ABCB1, CYP2C19 and CYP2C9 were shown to play an important role in the development of a recurrent ischemic stroke in neurology patients using clopidogrel. Although CYP2C19 is involved in both metabolic steps of clopidogrel, CYP2C9 was shown to be only involved in the second metabolic step, which involves the formation of the active metabolite [[9](#CR9)], (Fig. [1](#Fig1)). This suggests that CYP2C9 has a less important role than CYP2C19 in the metabolism of clopidogrel, and could explain why a highly significant role for CYP2C9 is mainly seen in CYP2C19 NM patients.

As the ABCB1 transporter is the first enzyme involved in clopidogrel pharmacokinetics, and our study shows that ABCB1 RA itself is an important factor in patient outcome, clopidogrel resistance of 31% of the patients in the total-study group (Table [3](#Tab3), column 1, and Fig. [2](#Fig2)) could be explained by inadequate clopidogrel uptake. In addition, 36.4% of patients with ABCB1 NA (Table [3](#Tab3), column 3 and Fig. [2](#Fig2)) had a CYP2C19 LOF (IM + PM), associated with clopidogrel resistance and patient outcome, representing 16.7% of the total-study population. Finally, 48.7% of the ABCB1 NA plus CYP2C19 NM subgroup (Table [3](#Tab3), column 7, and Fig. [2](#Fig2)) had a CYP2C9 LOF (IM + PM), which in this study was shown to be highly significant in relation to patient outcome, representing another 9.2% of the total study group. Together, this could explain clopidogrel resistance in more than 50% of patients in our study population. Interestingly, this closely matches the previously presented clopidogrel resistance frequencies for vascular and coronary patients, 53% and 54% respectively [[5](#CR5)].

The other enzymes, CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP1A2, and CES1A1 played no significant role in this study, despite the fact that they can metabolise clopidogrel in vitro to some extent [[9](#CR9)]. This could partly be due to the low allele frequencies of variants resulting in reduced enzyme activity, as for CYP1A2 and CES1A1, in combination with the small total-study group. Another explanation could be the relatively high frequency of CYP2C19 RM and UM individuals in Global population, ~30% (Table [3](#Tab3)), which has previously been shown to be associated with increased formation of the active metabolite of clopidogrel and a lower risk of cardiovascular events [[37](#CR37)]. Therefore, it is possible that CYP2C19 RM and UM can compensate for reduced activity of the other metabolizing enzymes. In the same way, ABCB1 CC, possibly linked to reduced efflux activity with increased clopidogrel uptake, could compensate for reduced activity of metabolizing enzymes (Fig. [2](#Fig2)). In line with this, it has previously been shown that a significant reduction in the risk of new stroke was observed in patients with ABCB1 3435 CC, compared with patients with ABCB1 3435 CT and TT [[38](#CR38)].

A limitation of this study is the size of the total-study group. The impact of CYP2C19 LOF could potentially have been demonstrated directly in a larger patient population. In our hospital, the introduction of preventive testing, before starting clopidogrel treatment, was initiated in 2024, in addition to reactive testing which was initiated in 2020. This limited further patient enrolment as distinguishing between reactive and preventive testing without consulting the patient record, became impossible. Another limitation is the size of the subgroups. While this has led to clearly significant results for the most important enzymes, these subgroups may be too small for reliable results for less important enzymes. Finally, this study did not address the possible risk of bleeding associated with clopidogrel treatment.

Based on our results, it is recommended to develop clinical guidelines for the ABCB1 and CYP2C9 phenotypes, in addition to the existing guidelines for the CYP2C19 phenotype, and the combination of these three phenotypes, to improve clinical outcomes for neurology patients treated with clopidogrel for the prevention of recurrent TIA or ischemic stroke.
