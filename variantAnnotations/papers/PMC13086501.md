## Abstract

**Rationale:** 
Cystic fibrosis is caused by variants in the CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR), leading to defective chloride ion transport and multi-organ dysfunction. CFTR modulators substantially improve chloride ion transport and disease severity, but responses vary, which may in part be due to variation in drug concentrations.

**Objectives:** 
This study aimed to evaluate modulator concentrations among people with CF and the potential impact of cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A5 genotypes on sweat chloride.

**Methods:** 
This multicenter study enrolled 97 children and adult participants established on elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) therapy. ETI drug concentrations were quantified and CYP3A5 genotypes were determined. Relationship between drug concentrations, genotype, and sweat chloride response were investigated using correlation and multivariable regression models to examine associations between drug levels and sweat chloride.

**Measurements and Main Results:** 
Plasma concentrations of elexacaftor, tezacaftor and ivacaftor were highly variable. Analyses revealed that CYP3A5 genotype status had no significant effect on drug concentrations. Association analysis demonstrated an association of sweat chloride with drug concentrations including after adjusting for pre-modulator sweat chloride, age, race, BMI, and sex, showing that lower drug concentrations are associated with worse outcomes in sweat chloride.

**Conclusion:** 
This study provides evidence that lower drug concentration are associated with worse sweat chloride levels and may be a potential indicator of therapeutic effectiveness, especially for people with high sweat chloride despite treatment. The influence of drug variability underscores the need for personalized dosing strategies to improve CF treatment outcome, although well-known CYP3A5 genotypes are unlikely to be helpful.

Keywords: Elexacaftor, Tezacaftor, Ivacaftor, cystic fibrosis, sweat chloride

## Introduction.

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is caused by genetic variants in the CF transmembrane conductance regulator (*CFTR*), leading to impaired chloride ion transport across epithelial membranes.([1](#R1)) Defective protein expression and activity causes multi-organ system dysfunction, leading to pancreatic insufficiency, chronic pulmonary infection and inflammation among other complications. Elevated sweat chloride levels are a biomarker for CFTR function and are the standard diagnostic feature of cystic fibrosis.([1](#R1), [2](#R2))

The management of CF has changed substantially with the introduction of CFTR modulator compounds elexacaftor, tezacaftor, ivacaftor and lumacaftor.([3](#R3), [4](#R4)) These small molecule compounds regulate CFTR function and chloride transport, and have been shown to substantially improve sweat chloride in those with modulator responsive genetic variants. Sweat chloride has long been linked to improvements in lung function and associated with altering the risk of exacerbations and infections, thereby improving overall disease stability.([5](#R5))

Variability in response to CFTR modulators persists. While the etiology of altered response is likely multifactorial, one possibility is that these differences may be due to sensitivity to small alterations in drug exposure related to altered absorption, variable metabolism, or both, which are known to contribute to variations in drug exposure among people with CF.([6](#R6)–[10](#R10)) In real-world use, such as in the Characterizing CFTR Modulated Changes in Sweat Chloride and their Association with Clinical Outcomes ([NCT03350828](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03350828), CHEC-SC) study, variation in drug exposure is expected to be larger than in carefully controlled studies, due to alterations in dosing, diet, or other factors.([11](#R11)–[18](#R18)) CHEC-SC previously showed that variability in post-modulator sweat chloride was not substantially explained by individual characteristics, and that the majority of sweat chloride variation was as yet unexplained.([19](#R19))

In this study, we hypothesized that variations in modulator concentrations may contribute to variable sweat chloride outcomes. Further, genetic variants in cytochrome P450 enzymes such as CYP3A4, 5, and 7 or transporters may alter concentrations or clinical outcome.([6](#R6), [20](#R20)–[26](#R26)) While genetic variants in any of these pharmacogenes may contribute to modulator concentration variability, genetic variants in the CYP3A5 enzyme are known to impact concentrations and clinical response of certain drugs ([21](#R21), [22](#R22), [24](#R24), [26](#R26)), and is associated with a highly evidenced guideline for clinical implementation.([21](#R21)) These *CYP3A5* genetic variants confer defect that results in non-expression, which may influence any drug metabolized by CYP3A5.([27](#R27)) In addition to the CYP3A enzymes, several other pharmacogenes are involved in the metabolism or transport of one or more modulator compounds, including OATP1B1, OATP1B3, and P-glycoprotein.([20](#R20), [28](#R28)) Variants in any of these enzymes or transporters could influence drug concentrations or response. For this study, a targeted approach for well-characterized genetic variants in *CYP3A5* was selected due to biologic plausibility and sample size.

The pharmacokinetics sub-study of CHEC-SC (CHEC-pharmacokinetics) represents a significant opportunity to understand modulator concentration variability by people with CF in a real-world scenario, and to assess concentrations with sweat chloride testing. The results from this study will help assess whether low drug concentrations might help explain poor response in routine care.

## Methods.

### Study Participants.

This project was a sub-study of a large prospective, single-visit, multicenter observational study of sweat chloride changes after modulator therapy.([19](#R19), [29](#R29)–[31](#R31)) The focus of the study was the natural history of sweat chloride after modulator use, and one visit was planned to evaluate sweat chloride and all other clinical variables. For this study we recruited participants across six of the 51 clinical sites who were receiving triple-combination CFTR modulators (elexacaftor, tezacaftor, and ivacaftor) for at least 90 days prior to the study visit (see participant inclusion flow diagram in [Supplement](#SD1)). The study was approved by the IRB at all sites. All participants provided informed consent. Participants attended a single study visit and provided blood samples for pharmacokinetic and genetic analyses. Samples were collected at 0 hours (prior to ingestion of medication) and 4 hours later. Concentrations between these two time points were collinear, and thus for the remainder of analysis, only 0h concentrations were used. Sweat chloride at the time of study visit was assessed by local clinical laboratories as described previously, after the 4h blood sample was collected.([19](#R19)) Demographic and clinical variables, including sex, age, race, body mass index (BMI), and lung function (Forced Expiratory Volume in one second percentage predicted, ppFEV1) at the time of the visit were collected as covariates. The primary endpoint of the study was to determine relationships between concentration of each compound and impact on sweat chloride. A secondary endpoint of this study was to explore the impact of known genetic variants in CYP3A5 on modulator concentrations and sweat chloride at the time of the study visit or change in sweat chloride between the study visit and pre-modulator testing

### Quantitation of drug compounds.

The concentration of elexacaftor, tezacaftor, and ivacaftor were determined as described previously([11](#R11)), as well as the major human metabolites: elexacaftor-M23, tezacaftor-M1, ivacaftor-M1, and ivacaftor-M6. For each, we utilized liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Both assays were validated to standards set by the Food and Drug Administration for clinical trials.

### Genotyping for known CYP3A5 single nucleotide polymorphisms.

Buffy coat was collected from each whole blood sample. DNA extraction was completed using the Qiagen Puregene blood DNA purification system (Hilden, Germany). DNA samples were sent to CD Genomics (Shirley, New York, U.S.A.) for genotyping of three highly evidenced variants in CYP3A5: rs10264272, rs776746, rs41303343. These three variants define poor, intermediate, or normal metabolism as per the Clinical Pharmacogenomics Implementation Consortium ([CPIC.org](http://CPIC.org)) and were chosen as a likely target for influencing metabolism of modulators.

### Statistical analyses.

Descriptive measures including means, standard deviations, and percentages, were computed. Pearson correlation examined collinearity of the three compounds and initial association with sweat chloride. Results were separated into categories by concentration of each compound: all three concentrations high, all three low, and variable. We quantified high and low as dichotomous variables (~50% of the sample in highest or lowest concentrations) and in tertiles (~33% in high, low or average), and categorized individuals as always high, always low, and the remainder categorized as “variable.” In both cases, variable refers to a lack of collinearity between the three compounds. Subsequent analyses were found to be similar for either categorization, so we opted to present dichotomous data to examine absorption group. To examine the relationship between drug concentrations and CYP3A5, we used the Student’s t-test to examine differences in variables between poor metabolizers and intermediate metabolizers. To account for the non-linear change in the sweat chloride and the non-linear association between drug concentration and the sweat chloride, we took the natural log of the sweat chloride and the drug concentration. Differences are adjusted by age, sex, race/ethnicity, and BMI, all of which could influence either concentration or sweat chloride and were available in the CHEC-SC study. Metabolizer status was added to assess contribution to the association between sweat chloride measures and concentrations. Tables present the coefficients from the regression models as well as the percent change in the outcome given a doubling of the drug compound. Associations between drug concentrations and sweat chloride by high, variable, or low categorical groups are examined using linear regression. For this analysis we presented linear regression rather than log-log regression as the associations between the drug concentrations and sweat chloride are relatively linear within these groups and the percent change in sweat chloride associated with a percent change in the drug concentration is not comparable across the different absorption groups. Doubling of the drug concentration in the always low group could plausibly happen, while doubling of drug concentration in the always high absorption group is highly unlikely. Further exploratory analysis including interactions between the compounds used log-transformation. We present both the regression coefficient and the expected mmol/L change in the outcome given a 100 ng/mL increase in the drug concentration. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS institute, North Carolina) and R version 4.3.1.

### Effect of genotype of CYP3A5.

We performed targeted genotyping for the single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that are included in the existing implementation guideline([21](#R21)) and classified all participants based on their metabolizer status as defined in this guideline for CYP3A5 activity. Although three metabolizer statuses exist in humans, in the present study, we found only participants with “intermediate” (28.5%) and “poor” (71.4%) metabolizer status. In comparing these two metabolizer statuses, we found no significant differences in the concentrations of any compound quantified ([Figure E1](#SD1)). In addition, we found no differences between these groups for sweat chloride change, or sweat chloride at study visit ([Figure E1](#SD1)). Lung function (ppFEV1) and weight were also collected at the time of the visit, and no differences in these variables were seen by metabolizer status.

### Association of trough concentrations with sweat chloride.

Initial correlation analysis was completed between the trough and the sweat chloride at study visit and sweat chloride change. No correlation was seen between any concentration and sweat chloride change, but a significant correlation was seen with sweat chloride at study visit ([Figure 2](#F2)). [Figure 2](#F2) includes many data points above the estimated effective concentration of 90% maximal response (EC_90_),([34](#R34)) at which point there is no expected relationship between concentration and response. This is a ceiling effect, so data were re-analyzed with concentrations below estimated EC_90_, which revealed a stronger relationship between concentration and sweat chloride at study visit. The discrepancy between the two sweat chloride measurements is most likely attributable to the influence of residual function mutations on unadjusted associations, as well as the exclusion of participants without pre-modulator values from the analysis (see flow diagram, [Figure E2](#SD1)). This discrepancy is further explored in additional analyses below.

### Elexacaftor, tezacaftor, and ivacaftor concentrations are collinear.

Because these three drugs are used in combination, we evaluated collinearity (e.g., higher values in one equate to higher values in another). We evaluated associations between each pair of compounds and found that the R^2^ between the three compounds were: ivacaftor/tezacaftor 0.83, elexacaftor/tezacaftor 0.81, elexacaftor/ivacaftor 0.84 (all significant, p<0.001). Despite the drugs all being taken together and collinear in plasma concentration, the relationship is imperfect. We further assessed collinearity by examining the proportion of concentrations where all three compound concentrations were all high, all low, or variable (variable meaning low collinearity between compounds) ([Table 2](#T2)). For this categorization, 37.5% (n=36) fall into the all high, 38.5% (n=37) fall into the all low and 24% (n=23) fall into the variable category. Among the variable group, there was no relationship between the three concentrations (elexacaftor/tezacaftor R^2^=0.02, tezacaftor/ivacaftor R^2^=0.01, ivacaftor/elexacaftor R^2^=0.01).

### Association between unadjusted and adjusted (for age, sex, race, and BMI) sweat chloride and individual drug concentrations.

Initial correlation revealed a relationship between concentration and sweat chloride, but did not provide detailed information regarding the contribution of concentration to the measure of sweat chloride. Because the relationship between drug concentration and clinical response is non-linear, we took the natural log of the variables as described in methods to perform log-log regression. The log-log regression results for sweat chloride are presented in [Table 3](#T3). We developed four nested models to present the unadjusted association (Model 1); the influence of covariates which may affect sweat chloride, concentration, or both (Model 2, adding age, sex, race, and BMI); adjustment for pre-modulator sweat chloride which affects the magnitude of change in that measure (Model 3), and metabolizer status to further explore potential contributions from *CYP3A5* (Model 4). Including the covariates did not largely change the relationship between the drug compounds and the outcomes ([Table E1](#SD1)). Findings related to female sex and pre-modulator sweat chloride’s impact on sweat chloride change have been reported in the larger CHEC-SC study.([19](#R19)) The adjusted association between the drug compounds and sweat chloride at study visit and sweat chloride change are presented graphically in [Figures 3](#F3) and [E3](#SD1). In unadjusted analysis, each individual drug concentration only explains about 5% of the variation in sweat chloride ([Table 3](#T3)) when analyzing the entire population.

### Exploratory analysis including all three compounds of ETI in the model.

The above model analyses are limited because each compound is not used independently, and instead are ingested together, with the combined effect of all three compounds contributing to the sweat chloride concentration. To assess this combined effect, analysis including all three compounds in the same model is needed, but complicated by the significant collinearity between the compounds. Therefore, we performed exploratory regression analyses with all three drugs in the model by categories of concentrations (all compounds low, all compounds high, and variable as defined above). Analyses explaining these are shown in [Table 5](#T5). Due to high collinearity between the compounds, we do not interpret the coefficients, but focus on the R^2^ and determine significance from the model F test, which indicates whether our predictors significantly predict sweat chloride. Including all three compounds in the model without an interaction, we see a significant association with sweat chloride at study visit in the low group, suggesting that concentrations contribute around 28% of the variation in sweat chloride in this group. We can assess for interaction between the three compounds in the variable group (because the concentrations are not collinear), which shows a significant association for sweat chloride at study visit. In this interaction, the contribution of concentration remains uncertain, but may contribute as much as 62% of the observed variability in this analysis. Overall, these data support a strong association between concentrations of ETI with the sweat chloride at the study visit for low and variable ETI concentrations. Among individuals with the highest concentrations, concentrations are not associated with variation in sweat chloride, consistent with a ceiling effect. These analytic approaches do not show association of concentrations with sweat chloride change, a discrepancy which is consistent throughout our analysis and is explained above. A larger sample size with sufficient data will be needed to further explore these relationships.

## Discussion

In this study we found that people with lower drug levels were more likely to have higher sweat chloride values at a single clinical visit. In contrast, among people with high drug levels, further increases in concentration did not meaningfully change sweat chloride. These findings suggest that for patients with poor sweat chloride response, low drug levels may be a clinically relevant contributor. While of significant interest, important longitudinal evaluations such as lung function and growth were not collected in the parent study (CHEC-SC), thus limiting this study to sweat chloride measures, which are nevertheless associated with other clinical outcomes across populations.([35](#R35), [36](#R36)) In our analysis, each drug contributes to the variation in sweat chloride, suggesting that each is important and adds to the overall sweat chloride value. When evaluating the group with the lowest concentrations, we see a larger contribution to sweat chloride at study visit, which is a test that can be used clinically to monitor response. Attempting to include all three compounds in a single model yields mixed results. Therefore, while our data support a relationship between drug concentrations and sweat chloride, there are still unanswered questions. Regardless, the strong association with sweat chloride at lower concentrations suggests a concern for individuals who may have lower concentrations due to adherence, differences in drug absorption, metabolism, or clearance, or a reduction in dose due to concerns for side effects. In contrast, for individuals in the high concentration range, they are likely to be able to tolerate a dose reduction without substantial impact on their sweat chloride concentration. For either group of individuals, monitoring concentrations of ETI, sweat chloride, or both, may be helpful.([37](#R37)) Similar to other studies([11](#R11), [13](#R13), [14](#R14), [16](#R16)–[18](#R18), [32](#R32), [38](#R38)–[42](#R42)), we found a broad range of concentrations in both children and adults; many studies find a majority of individuals with concentrations exceeding those which would result in maximum responses. In one small study evaluating the real-world exposure-response relationship([39](#R39)), the authors found no association between exposure and response, which is expected with most concentrations in the study being in the highest range. Our study was sufficiently large and diverse to identify people with CF who may be at risk for poorer response due to low concentrations.

There has been a strong interest in the relationship of pharmacogenetic variants with ETI concentrations. Our study included a targeted pharmacogenetic study looking at CYP3A5, one of the major P450 enzymes for modulator metabolism and the only one in the CYP3A family of enzymes with a highly evidence guideline.([21](#R21)) In addition, the specific variants we tested result in non-expression, which would be expected to influence modulator concentrations if the CYP3A5 enzyme had a substantial role in their metabolism. We found nearly a third of our study population were intermediate metabolizers, which is interesting considering the racial and ethnic distribution of our study population, which is expected to possess poor metabolizer status.([23](#R23)) We found no evidence for a clinically relevant association of the CYP3A5 variants studied, which is in contrast with studies for other drugs.([21](#R21), [22](#R22), [24](#R24)–[26](#R26)) Focusing solely on these three SNPs is a limitation that does not preclude the possibility that other variants in *CYP3A5* or other pharmacogenes may influence ETI clinical outcomes or adverse events.([19](#R19), [28](#R28), [43](#R43), [44](#R44)) The contribution of any genetic variants to these drug concentrations or sweat chloride is likely to be complex and worthy of further study. At this time, the inclusion of genotype in drug metabolism or transport protein genes in clinical care decisions regarding modulator therapy has no basis in evidence and should be avoided. Additional explorations such as genome-wide association studies are necessary to identify clinically relevant genetic variants.

This data set was sufficiently powered to detect associations using Pearson correlation between concentrations and sweat chloride. Other estimates of concentrations that could be determined using nonlinear mixed effect modeling (population pharmacokinetic analysis) have not been evaluated, given the scope of this project. Use of a single trough measurement may not capture genotype-driven variability, although clinically relevant differences in drug metabolism should be seen with a trough concentration at steady-state. We were not able to analyze associations with other outcome measures of interest, particularly the absolute change over time in lung function or weight, as well as certain comorbidities (such as liver function testing or diabetes status), or side effect profiles, because these variables were not collected in CHEC-SC. Fat intake at ingestion was not recorded, which could influence results. Sweat chloride measurements were measured locally according to standardized protocols, which also may introduce variability in this measure. Future studies should include the evaluation of concentration over time and other measures of interest to further understand the associations identified in this study. Lastly, our exploratory analysis suggests a complex and nuanced relationship between ETI concentrations and sweat chloride which warrants additional study with a larger sample size.

Prior studies([29](#R29)) demonstrated variability in sweat chloride after modulator use. At least in part, this variability is likely the result of modulator concentrations, as shown in the present study. Since sweat chloride is a reasonable biomarker for modulator response ([19](#R19), [29](#R29)), individuals with poor sweat chloride response may be considered for therapeutic drug monitoring. As further evidence emerges, clarifying the precise thresholds of concentrations that result in a robust clinical response, as well as the effects of dose adjustment, may be a valuable tool to tailor therapy to each individual with CF.([37](#R37)) In patients with low ETI levels and high sweat chloride, clinicians may consider assessing adherence, drug-drug interactions, and fat intake with dosing. Conversely, patients with high drug levels and good sweat chloride response may tolerate cautious dose reduction, although this requires prospective evaluation.
