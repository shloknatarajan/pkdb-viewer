## Abstract

**Aim:** 
The study aimed to examine the association of two selected candidate SNPs rs2242480 (CYP3A4) and rs1045642 (ABCB1) with metabolic ratio of plasma norfentanyl to fentanyl concentrations in patients undergoing major breast surgeries.

**Methods:** 
The retrospective cross-sectional study was done in 257 female patients. DNA extraction, genotyping of selected SNPs, and drug levels measurement were employed.

**Results:** 
A total of 257 female patients were recruited with no loss to follow up. There was no significant association between the two mentioned SNPs and the metabolic ratio (p value > 0.05). As an exploratory analysis, there was a moderately significant negative correlation between metabolic ratio and pupillary constriction to fentanyl (r = –0.27; p < 0.001). There was also a weak but significant positive correlation between metabolic ratio and time for first analgesia in the postoperative period (r = 0.17; p = 0.01).

**Conclusion:** 
There was no significant association with the selected candidate SNPs in CYP3A4 and ABCB1 genes and metabolic ratio of norfentanyl to fentanyl in South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgery.

KEYWORDS: Fentanyl, norfentanyl, pain, metabolism, pharmacokinetics, pharmacogenetics, single nucleotide polymorphisms, personalized medicine

## 1. Introduction

Surgical interventions are a major cause of acute pain in patients during the postoperative period. This acute pain is a physiological response to the tissue trauma which occurs during the surgery; however, uncontrolled and prolonged pain response is detrimental to patient recovery and would lengthen the hospital stay. The pain response may result in adverse cardiovascular and respiratory complications and also cause poor wound healing. Poor analgesic management is also known to increase the morbidity and mortality of patients undergoing surgery due to the above mentioned complications. During the postoperative period, patients will experience severe stress and dissatisfaction if the pain is poorly controlled. The costs incurred by the patient as well as the hospital increases because of the prolonged recovery and high incidence of complications [[1](#cit0001),[2](#cit0002)].

Patients experience acute severe pain in the postoperative period if adequate analgesics are not administered [[3](#cit0003)]. Regarding options for analgesia, opioids are preferred over non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDS) in the setting of postoperative analgesia. Opioids have traditionally been used in postoperative analgesia as they target central analgesic mechanisms like endogenous opioids, endogenous cannabinoids, central pain neurons, etc. Opioids are currently used for treating moderate-to-severe acute pain, according to the WHO-step ladder treatment of pain [[4](#cit0004)]. Morphine and other derivatives of opium like codeine as well as synthetic opioids such as tramadol, fentanyl, sufentanil, alfentanil, and remifentanil are some of the commonly used opioids for analgesia. The use of opioids is affected by large inter-individual variability in dose requirements, necessitating dose adjustments to overcome inadequate analgesia or avoid serious adverse effects in patients [[5](#cit0005)]. A randomized study involving multiple centers has reported 20–30% poor response (partial and non-responders) to opioid drugs administered during the postoperative period [[6](#cit0006)]. Another aspect of long-term use of opioids is that a significant fraction of patients continue to be non-responders developing persistent or refractory pain. A recent meta-analysis observed that factors like age, type of tumor, psychological factors like anxiety and lack of sleep are associated with response to opioids in the postoperative period [[7](#cit0007),[8](#cit0008)]. About 20–30% of patients treated with opioids suffer from minor adverse effects, and about 11% of individuals experience serious adverse effects such as respiratory depression, hypotension, and bradycardia [[9](#cit0009),[10](#cit0010)]. This variability in the response to opioids may also be influenced by several genetic factors (*OPRM1*, *ADRB1*, *COMT*, *ABCB1*, *CYP3A4*, etc.) and non-genetic factors (age, gender, surgery type, behavioral factors, etc.). It is pertinent to note that pain and analgesia are complex phenotypes that are influenced by several determinants in nociceptive, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic pathways.

Although several classes of analgesics are in use, opioids such as fentanyl remain the most preferred postoperative analgesics [[10–12](#cit0010)]. Fentanyl acts by binding to the OPRM1 (mu-1 opioid receptor) protein and it is cleared from the cells by the ABCB1 (ATP-binding cassette B1) protein. ABCB1 is associated with decreased dosage of opioids. Opioids are metabolized by the Cytochrome P450 enzyme of which CYP3A4 is responsible for >99% of fentanyl metabolism [[13](#cit0013)]. Hence, any structural or functional changes resulting from genetic variations like single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the above mentioned genes may alter the analgesic response to fentanyl as illustrated in [Figure 1](#f0001).

In this study, we attempted to examine the association of two candidate SNPs, namely, rs2242480 (*CYP3A4*) and rs1045642 (*ABCB1*), with metabolic ratio of plasma norfentanyl to fentanyl concentration in South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgeries. In addition, some non-genetic factors such as age, type of surgery, experimental pain tests, pupillary responses to opioids may also be associated with postoperative pain and analgesia [[14–22](#cit0014)]. Hence, we also examined for correlation between metabolic ratio and other outcomes.

## 2. Materials and methods

This study was designed as a retrospective cross-sectional study. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (human studies) of Jawaharlal Institute of Postgraduate Medical Education and Research (JIP/IEC/2019/040 dated 9 May 2019). The data analysis and statistical plan were written and posted in the Clinical Trial Registry of India (Reg.no: CTRI/2019/06/019588 dated 10/06/2019). A written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was carried out in Surgery and Surgical Oncology departments between 10 June 2019 and 30 September 2021.

A total of 257 South Indian female patients between 18 and 70 years of age, under the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification I–III, who underwent major breast surgeries under general anesthesia were recruited for this study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for recruiting patients as well as other details regarding the study procedure are described in supplementary table S1.

All patients received similar general anesthetic management intra-operatively. Patients were administered Inj. Propofol, Inj. Fentanyl (2 mg/kg), and Inj. Atracurium (0.5 mg/kg) at induction of anesthesia. Anesthesia was maintained during the surgery using sevoflurane as a volatile anesthetic. Intermittent fentanyl doses were administered to maintain stable hemodynamics, namely, pulse rate and mean arterial pressure. All patients were extubated at the end of surgery after adequate reversal of neuromuscular blockade. Inj. Paracetamol (1 g i.v.) was administered at the end of the surgery. The blood sample was collected from each patient to perform the genotyping of the shortlisted candidate genes.

Pupillary response to fentanyl (PRF) was calculated as the difference in pupillary diameters before and 10 min after an induction dose of intravenous (i.v.) fentanyl of 2 μg kg^−1^. The pupillary diameter measurement was done using a linear Ultrasound probe (Sonosite®, Washington, USA). This bedside test for examining pupillary response to opioids was adapted from pre-validated studies [[23](#cit0023),[24](#cit0024)].

In the postoperative period, patients were started on intravenous fentanyl through a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump (Carefusion IVAC®PCAM®). The PCA settings were standardized for all patients with a 20 μg demand bolus, 15-min lockout intervals, and no background infusion. A physician administered a bolus of 20 μg i.v. fentanyl was set as a rescue analgesic. The maximum dose limit was 100 μg/hour of i.v. fentanyl. The 24-h postoperative intravenous fentanyl requirement was recorded in the patient’s PCA pump. Other parameters like age, height, weight, intraoperative dose of fentanyl, and anesthesia duration were also recorded for each patient.

### 2.1. DNA extraction and genotyping of selected genetic polymorphisms

A 5 ml venous blood sample collected intra operatively 30 min after the first dose of fentanyl. DNA extraction was done using the standard phenol chloroform method from the blood sample. The quantification of the extracted DNA was performed using a BioPhotometer (Eppendorf BioPhotometer®, Hamburg, Germany). Genotyping of patients was done with Real-Time Thermocycler (Applied Biosystems Quantstudio 3, Foster City) and pre-validated Taqman assays (supplementary table S2). Allelic discrimination was performed using Quantstudio design and analysis software (Version 1.5.1). The genotyping call rate was >95% for each SNP. Genotyping for each SNP was done in triplicates (10% of samples) to validate the results of allelic discrimination.

### 2.2. Estimation of plasma fentanyl and norfentanyl levels using LC-MS/MS

The plasma concentrations of norfentanyl and fentanyl were estimated from the plasma extracted from blood samples of patients. The blood samples were collected during the intraoperative period (at 30 min after administration of induction dose of intravenous fentanyl). The different steps involved in the methodology for this objective are described in [Figure 2](#f0002).

### Figure 2.

![Figure 2.](https://cdn.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/blobs/f419/11703455/22cc751538f3/IPGS_A_2429365_F0002_OC.jpg)

Different steps involved in the methodology for association of candidate SNPs with metabolic ratio of norfentanyl and fentanyl.

From the venous blood that was collected intra-operatively, plasma was separated by centrifugation at 7500 rpm for 10 min and it was stored in −80°C till further analysis. The pure compounds of norfentanyl and deuterated norfentanyl (d5) (used as internal standard) were obtained from Cayman chemicals (Michigan, USA). Calibrators and quality control (QC) samples of different concentrations of fentanyl and norfentanyl were prepared. Internal standard (5 ng/ml of norfentanyl-d5) was added to calibrators, QCs, and the participant plasma samples. Extraction was done by a liquid-liquid extraction procedure. The method of estimation used was Liquid chromatography – tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) as shown in Supplementary figure S1.

The infusion solution for MS (first time alone) was prepared by dissolving 1000 ng/ml of fentanyl, norfentanyl, and d5-norfentanyl in 10 ml of 50:50 methanol: water in a 15 ml tube. This solution was vortexed thoroughly and then used for MS tuning. Two mobile phases were used (A and B). Mobile phase A was prepared by adding 0.1 ml of formic acid in 100 ml of Milli Q water (0.1% formic acid), whereas mobile phase B constituted 100% acetonitrile. Both mobile phases were sonicated for 15 min. Mobile phases A and B were used for the final infusion at a ratio of 15:85 with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The column used in liquid chromatography was C18 (3.9 × 150 mm) (Symmetry Waters®) column with a particle size of 5 μm. A column temperature of 40°C and an autosampler temperature of 25°C was used in our analysis. A mixture of Methanol and Milli Q Water in the ratio of 80:20 was used as needle wash solution and a mixture of Milli Q Water and Methanol in the ratio of 90:10 was used as seal wash solution. A mixture of Methanol and Milli Q Water in the ratio of 50:50 was used as column wash solution.

In the mass spectrometry, nitrogen was used as desolvation gas and argon was used in the collision cell. The detector was operated in multiple reaction monitoring mode. The run time per sample analysis was 6.25 min. The analyte response was adjusted for internal standard response. The matrix effect was evaluated by comparing analyte peaks in neat solvent and extracted matrices ([Figure 3](#f0003)). The LC-MS/MS method was validated for a) selectivity, b) linearity and c) precision

## 3. Statistical analysis

The sample size used in this study was estimated for genotype association with 24-h postoperative fentanyl requirement reported elsewhere (Kumar S and colleagues, [[25](#cit0025)]) and details regarding sample size calculations are provided in supplementary file 1. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk tests, and non-normal variables were log-transformed. Continuous data were summarized as means with standard deviations or median with interquartile range based on data distribution. Categorical data were summarized as numbers with percentages. Relevant candidate SNPs (*CYP3A4 *1 G* – rs2242480 and *ABCB1* – rs1045642) were tested for association with the outcome of metabolic ratio (norfentanyl/fentanyl plasma concentration) using generalized linear regression models (GLM) after logarithmically transforming the non-normally distributed variables under two different genetic models: dominant and recessive models. Genetic associations were adjusted for any clinical factor if they were found to be associated with the outcomes. As exploratory objectives, we also performed a correlation analysis between metabolic ratio and different variables such as intraoperative and postoperative fentanyl requirement, anesthesia duration, 24-h postoperative pain scores, time for first analgesia, and pupillary constriction to fentanyl. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 3.5.2).

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Common demographic and genotyping results

The overall characteristics of the patients included in this study are described in [Table 1](#t0001). All variables are summarized as median (quartiles) as they were found to have a non-parametric data distribution. We observed that the median 24-h postoperative fentanyl requirement was 480 μg for South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgery. Among the clinical factors, body mass index (BMI) was correlated with height and weight, and, hence, BMI was excluded from further analysis. None of the 257 patients were lost to follow-up or had missing data. A flowchart for the entire study process and data collection is depicted in Supplementary figure S2. The details of SNPs included in the study like allele and genotype frequencies are described in [Table 2](#t0002). It is observed that the minor allele frequencies of the SNP rs2242480 (*CYP3A4*) is comparable to the minor allele frequency observed in the 1000 Genome project. However, large variations in the frequencies of the SNP rs1045642 (*ABCB1*) are observed in different populations across the globe. In our study, we observed that the minor allele frequency of the SNP rs1045642 (*ABCB1*) is G = 0.414 which is comparable to the ITU (Indian Telugu in the U.K.) and STU (Srilankan Tamil in the U.K.) sub-populations in the 1000 Genome Project (G = 0.407 for both populations) [[26](#cit0026)].

### 4.2. Association of candidate SNPs with metabolic ratio of plasma levels of norfentanyl and fentanyl in South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgery

Two SNPs *CYP3A4* (rs2242480) and *ABCB1* (rs1045642) were evaluated for association as they are involved in the pharmacokinetics of fentanyl. Metabolic ratio was calculated as the ratio of plasma norfentanyl to plasma fentanyl. We observed that the median plasma norfentanyl concentration was 1.24 ng/ml (quartiles − 0.09, 7.97) and the median fentanyl concentration was 0.33 ng/ml (quartiles − 0.19, 0.57). The median metabolic ratio was observed to be 2.92 (quartiles − 0.21, 25.65) for South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgery. After conducting an association analysis between the two candidate SNPs *CYP3A4* (rs2242480) and *ABCB1* (rs1045642), we did not observe any significant association between these two SNPs and metabolic ratio ([Table 3](#t0003) and [Figure 4](#f0004)). Additionally, we did not observe any association between other clinical factors like age, height, and weight associated with the outcomes. Hence, the genetic associations were not adjusted for these factors.

### 4.3. Correlation between different outcomes and metabolic ratio

As an exploratory analysis, we performed a correlation analysis between metabolic ratio and different outcomes such as intraoperative fentanyl dose requirement, postoperative fentanyl dose requirement, anesthesia duration, 24-h postoperative pain scores, and time for first analgesia in the postoperative period and pupillary constriction to fentanyl (at anesthesia induction). A Spearman correlation analysis was performed between the different variables as they followed a non-parametric data distribution. The analysis showed that there was a moderately significant negative correlation between metabolic ratio and pupillary constriction to fentanyl (*r* = −0.27; *p* < 0.001). There was also a weak but significant positive correlation between metabolic ratio and time for first analgesia in the postoperative period (*r* = 0.17; *p* = 0.01) as shown in [Table 4](#t0004) and [Figure 5](#f0005).

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1. Association of candidate SNPs with metabolic ratio of norfentanyl to fentanyl

In our candidate gene association study (CGAS), we did not observe any significant association of SNPs in genes *CYP3A4* and *ABCB1* with metabolic ratio of norfentanyl to fentanyl in South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgery. The synthetic opioid fentanyl mainly undergoes metabolism via the hepatic microsomal enzymes. Of the six expressed human P450 isoforms (P450s 1A2, 2B6, 2C9, 2D6, 2E1, and 3A4), only P450 3A4 exhibits significant fentanyl dealkylation to norfentanyl. These results indicate the predominant role of CYP3A4 in the primary route of hepatic fentanyl metabolism. Fentanyl undergoes piperidine N-dealkylation to norfentanyl by the enzyme CYP3A4. Other minor pathways of fentanyl metabolism include amide hydrolysis to despropionylfentanyl and alkyl hydroxylation to hydroxyfentanyl. N-dealkylation of hydroxyfentanyl leads to production of a secondary metabolite hydroxynorfentanyl [[27](#cit0027)]. In-vitro studies have shown that *CYP3A4 *1 G* polymorphism is associated with reduced metabolism of fentanyl in liver microsomes [[28](#cit0028)]. The activity of the hepatic enzyme CYP3A4, which is responsible for fentanyl metabolism has been shown that homozygous carriers of the SNP rs2242480 (*CYP3A4* gene) to be significantly lower than that in patients without the SNP [[29](#cit0029)].

In a study conducted in the Chinese population, it has been observed that *CYP3A4 *1 G* polymorphism is associated with the analgesic efficacy of intraoperative fentanyl in the patients undergoing hysteroscopy under general anesthesia [[30](#cit0030)]. Other studies on the SNP rs1045642 (*ABCB1* gene) have shown that this SNP may be associated with respiratory depression after intravenous fentanyl administration and efficacy of sedative-analgesic effects of continuous intravenous fentanyl infusion in children [[31](#cit0031),[32](#cit0032)]. However, in our study, we did not find any association between the SNPs rs1045642 (*ABCB1* gene) and rs2242480 (*CYP3A4* gene) and fentanyl metabolism. The sampling time in our study for estimation of fentanyl and norfentanyl may be a factor in determining the metabolic ratio. The estimation of fentanyl and norfentanyl may be performed at multiple time points to get a better representation of the metabolic ratio. Hence, we recommend that fentanyl and norfentanyl plasma levels be measured at multiple time points in future studies and also to study the influence of other SNPs in the genes *CYP3A4* and *ABCB1*.

After we evaluated the results of the exploratory objectives, we observed that there was a negative correlation between metabolic ratio and pupillary constriction to fentanyl. This suggests that higher pupillary constriction is associated with less plasma fentanyl being metabolized to plasma norfentanyl. There was also a positive correlation between metabolic ratio and time for first analgesia, suggesting that increased time for first analgesia correlated with more plasma fentanyl being metabolized to plasma norfentanyl. These findings are interesting and have not been reported before. These specific correlations may be evaluated in future studies to bring out the mechanistic links between these factors.

### 5.2. Strengths and weaknesses of the study

Our study is the first of its kind to examine the variability in metabolic ratio of plasma norfentanyl to fentanyl concentrations due to genetic polymorphisms in the metabolic pathway of fentanyl in South Indian female patients who underwent major breast surgery, which is a homogenous population subgroup.

Future candidate gene association studies can be conducted in other population sub-groups with allele frequency variations of certain SNPs, as they are more feasible than genome-wide association studies in limited resource settings. Although we did not find a strong association between SNPs and metabolic ratio, certain SNPs included in the larger study, namely, rs1799971 (*OPRM1*), were associated with postoperative pain scores and fentanyl requirement. Two other SNPs rs4680 (COMT) and rs1045642 (ABCB1) were found to be associated with postoperative pain scores, whereas the SNP rs734784 (KCNS1) was found to be associated with time for first analgesia. All these findings are reported elsewhere [[25](#cit0025)]. These SNPs were also incorporated in a predictive algorithm to predict the postoperative fentanyl requirement, pain scores, and time for first analgesia and have been published previously [[33](#cit0033)]. The procedure for evaluating pupillary constriction is relatively easy to perform as mentioned in this study.

Other limitations of the study include the single-center and observational nature of the study design. The role of other SNPs involved in the metabolism of fentanyl may be explored in future studies with a larger sample size. Although, we covered most of the important candidate SNPs in this study, there may be other gene/SNPs which may influence the outcomes. The results of this study may change when male patients, other ethnic populations (due to variations in allele frequencies) and other types of surgeries, are included and the role of these factors may need to be evaluated further. We also did not collect data regarding the menopausal status in women to study the influence of hormonal levels on pain perception and analgesia.

## 6. Conclusions

We did not find any significant association with the selected candidate SNPs in *CYP3A4* and *ABCB1* and metabolic ratio of norfentanyl to fentanyl in South Indian patients undergoing major breast surgery. However, there were significant correlations between metabolic ratio and two outcomes, namely, pupillary constriction to fentanyl and time for first analgesia.
