# Steroid–tacrolimus drug–drug interaction and the effect of CYP3A genotypes


## Aims:

Tacrolimus, metabolized by CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 enzymes, is susceptible to drug–drug interactions (DDI). Steroids induce CYP3A genes to increase tacrolimus clearance, but the effect is variable. We hypothesized that the extent of the steroid–tacrolimus DDI differs by CYP3A4/5 genotypes.


## Methods:

Kidney transplant recipients (n = 2462) were classified by the number of loss of function alleles (LOF) (CYP3A5*3, *6 and *7 and CYP3A4*22) and steroid use at each tacrolimus trough in the first 6 months post-transplant. A population pharmacokinetic analysis was performed by nonlinear mixed-effect modelling (NONMEM) and stepwise covariate modelling to define significant covariates affecting tacrolimus clearance. A stochastic simulation was performed and translated into a Shiny application with the mrgsolve and Shiny packages in R.


## Results:

Steroids were associated with modestly higher (3%–11.8%) tacrolimus clearance. Patients with 0-LOF alleles receiving steroids showed the greatest increase (11.8%) in clearance compared to no steroids, whereas those with 2-LOFs had a negligible increase (2.6%) in the presence of steroids. Steroid use increased tacrolimus clearance by 5% and 10.3% in patients with 1-LOF and 3/4-LOFs, respectively.


## Conclusions:

Steroids increase the clearance of tacrolimus but vary slightly by CYP3A genotype. This is important in individuals of African ancestry who are more likely to carry no LOF alleles, may more commonly receive steroid treatment, and will need higher tacrolimus doses.


## INTRODUCTION

Tacrolimus in combination with mycophenolate is a commonly used immunosuppressive regimen in organ transplantation. Steroids are also frequently used as part of the immunosuppression induction regimen at time of transplant, may be added to the maintenance regimen in high-risk transplants, and are the treatment of choice for allograft rejection. Tacrolimus use is complicated by its narrow therapeutic index, high pharmacokinetic (PK) variability and numerous drug–drug interactions (DDIs). Therapeutic drug monitoring is required to avoid under- and over-exposures which place patients at risk of graft rejection and drug toxicity, respectively. Overexposure is associated with adverse effects, including infections, new onset diabetes, acute kidney injury, hypertension, tremors affecting quality of life and malignancies. Fluctuations in tacrolimus troughs over time are also associated with acute rejection and graft loss. DDIs are a common cause of over- and underexposure, and fluctuations in trough concentrations pose challenges in maintaining safe and effective immunosuppression.

Tacrolimus is a substrate of CYP3A5 and CYP3A4 enzymes and is susceptible to CYP3A induction and inhibition. It is well known that interpatient variability in tacrolimus PK and dose requirements is associated with genetic variation in these enzymes, as well as inhibition and induction by concomitantly administered drugs. Steroids have a known interaction with tacrolimus through induction of its metabolism. The total tacrolimus daily dose required to achieve target trough concentrations was higher in kidney recipients receiving high doses of prednisone than those taking low doses. Yet, even after considering steroid dose, there was still wide interindividual variability in tacrolimus dose requirements (0.05–0.39 and 0.03–0.38 mg/kg/day at the first and third months post-transplantation, respectively), suggesting the presence of additional factors. In another study, prednisolone discontinuation was associated with an average increase in tacrolimus dose-normalized troughs by 36%, but the effect was highly variable (−40% to +200%). In our previous studies, steroid use in African Americans was found to be associated with a 23% greater tacrolimus clearance, compared to only 6% in Europeans. These observations suggested a potential drug–drug–gene interaction (DDGI) and that individuals who express CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 proteins (i.e., carry the CYP3A5*1 and CYP3A4*1 alleles) may be more susceptible to steroid-related increases in tacrolimus clearance resulting in lower tacrolimus troughs than CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 nonexpressers (i.e., carriers of CYP3A4/5 low or loss-of-function [LOF] alleles). The purpose of this study was to quantify the magnitude of steroid/tacrolimus DDI effect on tacrolimus PK in kidney transplant patients while accounting for the different CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 genetic backgrounds.


## METHODS


### Patient population

We analysed data from kidney transplant recipients enrolled in two multicentre observational studies (Deterioration of Kidney Allograft Function [DeKAF] and Genomics of Kidney Transplantation [GEN03]). All patients signed written informed consent forms, which were approved by the local institutional review boards. The DeKAF study (www.clinicaltrials.gov, NCT00270712) involved seven transplant centres and participants were enrolled from 2005 to 2011. The GEN03 study (NCT01714440) includes patients recruited from 2012 to 2016 from five enrolling centres. Participants from these studies who were 18 years or older, received immediate-release tacrolimus for maintenance immunosuppression, and had CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 genotyping information available, were identified and considered eligible for this DDGI analysis.


### Immunosuppression and clinical data

Induction and maintenance immunosuppression with tacrolimus, mycophenolate, steroids (prednisone or methylprednisolone) and anti-thymocyte globulin-rabbit were based on centre-specific standard of care protocols. In general, patients were either on early steroid withdrawal protocols, receiving steroids over 3–5 days beginning on Day 0, steroid-avoidance protocols where no steroid induction or maintenance steroids were used, or on late-steroid withdrawal protocols where high-risk patients received prednisone 5–10 mg daily in the first 6 months. High-dose methylprednisolone was given for the treatment of acute rejection events. Clinical data including demographics, serum creatinine, tacrolimus troughs and doses, and concomitant medications were obtained from the study databases.


### Tacrolimus troughs

Tacrolimus trough concentrations were measured before an oral dose as a part of routine clinical care. Two tacrolimus trough concentrations and the corresponding dose were obtained per week from the medical record for the first 8 weeks and then twice a month from Months 3–6 post-transplant for a maximum of 24 trough measurements per patient, if available. The tacrolimus dose was adjusted according to the centre’s therapeutic target range and tolerability. Trough concentrations were measured at each centre and, in general, concentrations of 8–12 ng/mL were targeted for the first 3 months and 6–10 ng/mL for 3–6 months post-transplant.


### Genotyping

A blood sample was collected from each participant around time of transplantation, and DNA was extracted at a central laboratory at the University of Minnesota as previously described. A customized Affymetrix Axiom Transplant genome-wide chip was used for genotyping, and CYP3A5*3 (rs776746), CYP3A5*6 (rs10264272), CYP3A5*7 (rs41303343) and CYP3A4*22 (rs35599367) genotypes were used in this analysis.


### Evaluation of the steroid–tacrolimus–gene interaction in the development and validation cohorts


### Tacrolimus population PK model development

The steroid–tacrolimus DDI across CYP3A5 and CYP3A4 loss of function (LOF) alleles was studied by population PK modelling to develop a tacrolimus apparent oral clearance (CL/F) model. Data from the DeKAF study were used for model development with nonlinear mixed-effect (NLME) modelling software (NONMEM version 7.5; ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA) by a first-order conditional estimation (FOCE) method with interaction and Perl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN 5.2.6, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden) as the modelling interface. Exploratory analyses and model evaluation were performed, and diagnostic graphics were created with Rstudio version 2023.06.0 + 421 (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA) and PsN toolkit under the Pirana® (version 2.9.6) interface.

### Covariate analysis

To identify the extent of the tacrolimus–steroid–gene interaction, a composite variable of number of CYP3A loss-of-function (CYP3A5*3, *6, *7 and CYP3A4*22) alleles present and steroid use was created for each trough measurement in each patient. Patients were categorized as having (zero [0-LOF], one [1-LOF], two [2-LOF], and three or four [3/4-LOF]) (Table S1). Only four participants had 4-LOFs whose diplotypes were CYP3A5*3/*3 with CYP3A4*22/*22, and they were combined with the 3-LOF group. At each trough measurement, the presence of steroids (yes/no) was determined. The 2-LOF with no-steroids group was assigned as the reference. Individuals with missing covariates information were handled by the mode of the covariate approach where individuals lacking the covariate information were added to the most frequently occurring category. The effect of the LOF/steroid groups on tacrolimus TVCL/F was then tested as a categorical time-varying covariate and described in relation to the reference group.

We tested the effect of the use of other concomitant medications (calcium channel blockers [CCB], angiotensin converting enzyme [ACE] inhibitors, prophylactic antiviral agents], body weight, serum creatinine and glomerular filtration rate calculated by the modification of diet in renal disease (MDRD) equation at each trough concentration measurement as time-varying covariates. The effect of recipients’ age was tested as a continuous covariate. The effect of diabetes at time of transplant (yes or no), previous kidney transplant (yes or no), donor type (living or deceased), time post-transplant (<Day 9 or ≥Day 9) were tested as categorical covariates on tacrolimus TVCL/F by linear relation.

We used stepwise covariate modelling in the PsN tool kit. The significance of inclusion and elimination of each covariate was tested based on the likelihood ratio test that follows the  distribution. In the forward inclusion step, covariates decreasing the objective function (OFV) by 6.6 or more (P < .01) were considered significant and included in the full forward model. The full forward model was further tested by the backward elimination step. Covariates increasing the OFV by 10.8 or more  were considered significant and retained in the final covariate model.


### Model evaluation

To evaluate the precision and robustness of the final model parameter estimates, we performed a non-parametric bootstrap. The bootstrap was repeated 1000 times using random sampling with replacement to generate 1000 bootstrapped datasets while stratifying on the LOF alleles covariate to ensure representation of each of the LOF allele groups in each bootstrapped dataset. We conducted prediction-corrected visual predictive checks (pcVPCs) with 1000 simulations to evaluate the performance of the final model (Figure S1).

The GEN03 study data was used to confirm the predictive ability of the final model. ### Simulation and development of a web-based user-friendly application

A model-based simulation was performed with the mrgsolve package in R. A stochastic simulation was conducted to generate 1000 virtual individuals where one tacrolimus trough concentration was simulated for each individual (giving a total of 1000 troughs) using the estimates of both fixed and random effects from the final NLME model. The simulation extrapolates the final model to simulate diverse combinations of different covariates scenarios and their effect on tacrolimus clearance. The simulation results were integrated into an interactive web-based tool using the Shiny (shinyapps.io) and ggplot2 package in R (. The app simulates the trough concentrations by allowing the user to select different covariates and a proposed total daily dose of tacrolimus through the app’s graphical user interface. The app visualizes the distribution of simulated tacrolimus trough concentrations using a histogram and summary statistics table.

## RESULTS

We evaluated 2462 kidney transplant recipients (1608 in the development and 854 in the validation cohort) with 44 546 troughs (28 718 in the development and 15 828 in the validation cohort). There was a median [range] of 18 [1–24] troughs per patient in both cohorts. Demographic data and frequency of diplotypes are summarized in Table 1 and Table S2. In all patients, the median [range] tacrolimus trough and total daily dose were 8.2 ng/mL (0.3–75.9) and 6 mg/day (0.1–45), respectively, in the first 6 months post-transplant. Tacrolimus troughs increased with increasing number of LOFs. In patients with 0-LOF, 1-LOF, 2-LOF and 3/4-LOF, the median [range] troughs were 6.4 ng/mL [1–34.5], 7.5 ng/mL [0.7–65.6], 8.4 ng/mL [0.3–75.9] and 8.6 ng/mL [1–41.7], respectively. Patients with 0-LOF receiving steroids received the highest dose (12 mg/day) and had the lowest dose-normalized troughs (0.53 ng/mL/mg/day) whereas the patients with 3/4-LOF, who did not receive steroids, had the lowest dose requirement (3 mg/day), fourfold lower than those with 0-LOF receiving steroids, and the highest dose-normalized troughs (2.62 ng/mL/mg/day), fivefold higher than those with 0-LOF receiving steroids (Table 2).


### Tacrolimus clearance model and effects of clinical covariates

The effect of covariates on the typical tacrolimus clearance (27.2 L/h) are shown in Table 3. Typical tacrolimus clearance was lower by 10%, 7% and 4% if diabetes was present at the time of transplant, or if receiving a concomitant CCB or antiviral medication at time of trough measurement, respectively. Tacrolimus clearance was 21% and 4% higher in the first 9 days after transplantation or if receiving a concomitant ACE inhibitor, respectively. The effect of age on typical tacrolimus clearance was described by a power function and decreased by 0.67% for every year above the median age (51.4 years old). There were also effects of the LOF-steroids groups on tacrolimus clearance. Compared to the reference group (2-LOF without steroids), there was a 2.65-fold increase in clearance in the 0-LOF group with steroids and a 0.75-fold decrease in clearance in the 3/4-LOF group with steroids. The inter-site and interindividual variability estimates were 18.9% and 44.9%, respectively.


### Effect of steroids use on tacrolimus clearance

Concomitant steroid use was associated with higher tacrolimus clearance in all groups and the magnitude of the effect varied by the number of LOF alleles (range 3%–11.8%) (Table 4). The 0-LOF group showed the greatest increase (11.8%) in tacrolimus clearance in the presence of steroids compared to no steroids, whereas those with 2-LOFs had a negligible increase (2.6%) in the presence of steroids. Those with 3/4-LOFs had an increase (10.8%) in tacrolimus TVCL/F similar to the 0-LOF group.


### Model evaluation

The diagnostic scatter plots showed an acceptable overall goodness-of-fit of the final model (Figure S2). We conducted non-parametric bootstrap analysis and 99% of the runs were successfully minimized. The model estimates were comparable to the bootstrap analysis and within the 95% CIs indicating that the model is robust and reproducible (Table 3).


### External validation

The predictive performance of the development model in the Gen03 validation cohort is shown in Table 5. The MPE and the MPPE for tacrolimus troughs were −1.42 ng/mL and −17.8%, respectively. This suggests that, on median, the model underpredicted the trough concentrations relative to the observed concentrations by 1.42 ng/mL.


### Model simulation and Shiny app

The data from the final PK model was successfully implemented and used for simulations. Several clinical scenarios were input into the app (https://a-saqr.shinyapps.io/DDGI/) and are presented in Table 6, showing the percentage of simulated troughs below (<6 ng/mL), within (6–12 ng/mL) and above (>12 ng/mL) the therapeutic range with selected covariates. Across all LOF groups, the use of steroids does not substantially change the percentage of simulated troughs in the 6–12 ng/mL range, but it results in an increase in troughs <6 ng/mL and a decrease in the percentage of troughs >12 ng/mL. This is likely because trough measurements are shifted in a downwards direction in all three groups (<6, 6–12 and >12 ng/mL) during the use of steroids resulting in a higher percentage of individuals in the low trough group. The greatest effect was observed in the 0-LOF group.


## DISCUSSION

DDIs are well known to affect drug disposition and subsequent responses by increasing the risk of toxicity or treatment failure. Tacrolimus has many PK DDIs with varying degrees of severity and unpredictability and result in high variability in troughs. There is also a potent drug–gene interaction with tacrolimus and CYP3A4/5 genetic variation, but unlike DDIs, the genetic effect is stable across time.

The tacrolimus-steroid DDI is mediated through induction and is generally considered less severe than inhibition-mediated interactions. Glucocorticoids such as dexamethasone and prednisolone affect both CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 expression through glucocorticoid receptor signalling. Additionally, CYP3A4 can be exclusively induced through direct activation of the pregnane-X-receptor (PXR) in a ligand-dependent manner. In the CYP3A5 5′ regulatory region, Schuetz et al., identified two half-site glucocorticoid response elements between −891 to −1109 downstream that were responsive to dexamethasone induction (1 × 10−7 to 1 × 10−6 M) in HepG2 cells contransfected with the glucocorticoid receptor. In both CYP3A4 and CYP3A5, response elements for the PXR and constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) transcription factors are also present. Both transcription factor genes contain glucocorticoid response elements, and the modest induction observed with steroids may also be an indirect effect of increased PXR or CAR protein. A modest tacrolimus–steroid CYP3A4–CYP3A5 DDGI was observed in individuals with the CYP3A4*1/*1, CYP3A5*1/*1 genotype. Given that tacrolimus undergoes metabolism via CYP3A4 and CYP3A5, our findings align with the observed glucocorticoid-mediated induction of CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 in vitro. HepG2 hepatoma cells showed greater CYP3A4 mRNA expression inducibility than CYP3A5 when treated with dexamethasone (5 times vs. 1.5 higher than the control, respectively) and prednisolone (4–6 times vs. 1.3 times higher than the control, respectively). Hence, a steroid-related DDGI may be more pronounced with CYP3A4 variants than CYP3A5 variants. Given the low allele frequency of CYP3A4*22, it will be difficult to discriminate the absolute induction effect between the two genes in a clinical setting.

Tacrolimus–steroid DDI have not been well studied, with no controlled crossover studies, and are described mainly through retrospective analyses. Induction can be hard to detect because the time course of induction is different from inhibition with a slower onset and prolonged resolution. Induction of CYP3A4 enzyme by rifampin (a potent PXR ligand) on a steroid probe drug was detectable after 2 days of treatment, with maximum effect after 11–14 days and persisting for more than 7 days after discontinuation. The slow onset and offset of steroid induction make it more difficult to study and detect clinically; thereby, the effect may be missed or attributed to other factors. In our previous studies, we found that steroid use was associated with a 23% greater tacrolimus clearance in African Americans but only 6% in Europeans. The reasons for this difference between the populations are not clear, but the greater magnitude of effect in African Americans is possibly due to higher steroid doses, longer duration of treatment and/or greater genetic diversity in CYP3A5.

Evidence suggests significant DDGIs for clopidogrel–omeprazole, lansoprazole/pantoprazole–atorvastatin and fluvoxamine–lansoprazole and CYP2C19. We investigated a potential DDGI of tacrolimus–steroids and CYP3A4/5 variants while considering factors influencing tacrolimus clearance. Tacrolimus clearance changed in the presence of steroids but varied in extent by the number of CYP3A4/5 LOF alleles present. Individuals with no LOF alleles had the greatest but modest increase (11.8%) in tacrolimus clearance with steroids compared to the 1- or 2-LOF groups (5% and 3% increase, respectively). This suggests a greater CYP3A induction by steroids in individuals with more functional CYP3A protein and posing a risk of sub- or supratherapeutic trough levels when introducing or discontinuing steroids, especially for those with no LOF alleles who are at the fringes of the therapeutic range.

In the 3/4-LOF group, we observed an increase in tacrolimus clearance (10.3%) in the presence of steroids similar to the 0-LOF group (11.8%). The 3/4-LOF group are patients with the CYP3A5*3/*3 and CYP3A4*1/*22 or CYP3A4*22/*22 diplotypes and, as expected, have the lowest metabolism of the LOF groups. We expected a small effect of steroids on tacrolimus clearance in this group due to the presence of two CYP3A5*3 alleles and one or more CYP3A4*22 alleles, which both result in a reduction in CYP3A protein. Rifampin, a potent CYP2C9 inducer, differentially affected the oral clearance of tolbutamide across CYP2C9 diplotypes. Similar to our findings, poor metabolizers (CYP2C9*3/*3) had a greater increase in tolbutamide clearance (161%) when given with rifampin, whereas the normal metabolizers (CYP2C9*1/*1) had a 97% increase. The reason for the unexpectedly higher magnitude of the steroid effect on tacrolimus clearance in our 3/4-LOF group is unclear. Transplant patients often receive 15 or more medications, and it is possible that inhibitory and induction interactions with tacrolimus occur simultaneously. Hesselink et al. observed that kidney transplant recipients treated with steroids exhibited lower dose-normalized troughs and required a higher tacrolimus dose in the first 3 months post-transplantation than patients treated without steroids. They also observed that the induction effect of steroids on tacrolimus was lower when CCB were present in the regimen. In our study, steroids and CCB were simultaneously present in numerous patients. We observed a substantially higher proportion of troughs in the 0-LOF group compared to the 3/4-LOF group (30.9% and 17.5%, respectively) that were obtained when a CCB and steroid were simultaneously present. Therefore, it is possible that when a CCB is concomitantly administered with a steroid in the 0-LOF group, there is a lower than expected steroid induction effect due to the opposing CCB inhibition. In addition, prednisone is a prodrug which is converted to the active component, prednisolone, which is then further metabolized by CYP3A enzymes. Individuals with higher functioning CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 activities may have lower concentrations of prednisolone and less induction resulting in a lower than expected rise in tacrolimus clearance in the 0-LOF group. Other variants in genes encoding for PXR, CAR or transporters may have been induced by steroids and confound the associations. A study limitation is that we did not evaluate the steroids dose or duration, which may affect the extent of steroid induction. Finally, the number of patients in the 3/4-LOF group was small (<10%), and more patients with these diplotypes should be studied.

CCB and some antiviral drugs are CYP3A inhibitors. In vitro and clinical studies showed CCB inhibition of CYP3A5 activity in a genotype-dependent manner. In kidney transplant recipients the coadministration of a CCB with tacrolimus significantly increased tacrolimus exposure, with notable differences in the magnitude of the DDI between carriers and noncarriers of CYP3A5*3 allele. We found a 7% and 4% reduction in tacrolimus CL when CCB and antivirals were used, respectively. For drugs with a narrow therapeutic index such as tacrolimus, a 7% and 4% change may be important, particularly for patients at the edges of the therapeutic range. Moreover, covariates with small effects cumulatively have large effects and knowledge of their effects will improve dosing precision and ultimately create the best clinical models. Our goal is to also inform future studies by reporting all statistically significant and scientifically plausible covariates.

PK modelling and simulation is a well-established approach in drug development. Without clinical data, PK modelling and simulation methods are used to project a dose or dosing schedule with the goal of achieving drug concentrations similar to those attained in a well-studied patient cohort. We used our final population-PK model to simulate effects of CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 LOF alleles and the presence or absence of steroids with other significant clinical covariates on clearance in various complex scenarios. We successfully translated the simulation results into an open-source user-friendly app allowing input of expected tacrolimus total daily dose, the number of LOF alleles and steroid use, and clinical covariates while considering interindividual random variability. Our simulations also highlight a modest tacrolimus–steroids DDI which may be relevant to patients near therapeutic thresholds.

We do not recommend genotyping for purposes of averting the tacrolimus–steroid DDGI, but, in patients who have genotypes available and have 0-LOFs, additional trough monitoring may be necessary when a steroid is added or discontinued, and heightened awareness that a dose change may be required.

There are limitations to our work. We assumed that the CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 alleles (CYP3A5 *3, *6, *7 and CYP3A4*22) were similar in their activity towards tacrolimus metabolism; however, there are differences between these alleles. We also dichotomized the steroids as present or not present in the model because we do not have start/stop dates or doses. It is noteworthy, though, that the DDI between tacrolimus and steroids is likely dose-dependent. Previous studies indicate that higher steroid doses may be associated with greater tacrolimus metabolism and higher dose requirements. Future in vitro drug metabolism studies with clustered regularly inter-spaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) engineered hepatic cells to determine the effect by diplotype would be helpful. Variants in the genes encoding for the PXR transcription factor (NR1I2), CAR protein (NR1I3) and P-glycoprotein (ABCB1) were not evaluated and have been reported to affect tacrolimus disposition. We did not have access to albumin levels or haematocrit values in these patients and both play a role in tacrolimus PK. We did not have information about specific antiviral or CCB drugs or doses. For agents within these classes, there is variability in the extent of their interactions with tacrolimus, and assessment of individual drugs should be conducted. CYP3A4 activity is highly variable. While genetic variation contributes to the variability, non-genetic factors such as drug interactions, age, sex, environmental influences as well as disease also play a role, and they should be incorporated into studies of CYP3A4. Understanding and accounting for the multifactorial nature of CYP3A4 variability is crucial. Infrequent variants may also contribute to CYP3A4 variability, but we were unable to identify them in our previous genome-wide association studies (GWAS) studies despite our large population.

We present a large assessment of the tacrolimus-steroid interaction in a kidney transplant population. We further evaluated for a DDGI between tacrolimus–steroids and CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 diplotypes. We found that steroids increase tacrolimus clearance but vary according to the number of CYP3A4/5 LOF alleles. Although the effect of steroids was statistically significant, it exhibited a modest impact (3%–11.8% increase in clearance) and would not likely substantially alter clinical decision making when considered in isolation, but may matter when considered with other covariates and in patients at the fringes of the therapeutic range. In our previous models, we presumed that the effect of steroid induction was equal across all CYP3A4 and CYP3A5 diplotypes but this data will help us refine and improve personalized tacrolimus dosing algorithms. We also demonstrated how PK simulations and implementation into a Shiny app could be used in the future to implement individualized dosing. Future DDGI studies should assess the effect of steroid dose and duration, inhibition-mediated CYP3A DDGIs and gain-of-function variants such as POR*28. In vitro drug metabolism studies are needed to identify the distinct effects of each of the CYP3A alleles on tacrolimus clearance in the presence and absence of steroids and the durability of the steroids induction effect across the different diplotypes.
