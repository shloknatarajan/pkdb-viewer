## Abstract

**Background and Aims:** 
Pediatric pharmacokinetics pose unique challenges, particularly concerning drug dosing in the presence of obesity and genetic variability in drug-metabolizing enzymes such as cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2C19. This study aimed to develop a lansoprazole pediatric population pharmacokinetic (popPK) model, considering obesity, obesity-associated changes in inflammatory cytokines and the liver, and CYP2C19 genotype, and to assess implications for dosing strategies.

**Methods:** 
Pediatric subjects with and without obesity (6–21 years, n=47) received one oral dose of 1.2 mg/kg fat-free mass (FFM), not exceeding 60 mg. Plasma concentrations were measured at multiple time points, and a popPK analysis using NONMEM with stepwise covariate modeling was performed. Simulations compared exposures between children without and with obesity (BMI percentile ≥95th) after two dosing strategies: FDA-approved total body weight-tiered and FFM-based dosing.

**Results:** 
Five hundred thirty-seven lansoprazole concentrations were modeled using a two-compartment model with Weibull absorption and linear elimination. Parameters were allometrically scaled to FFM with fixed exponents of 0.75 for clearances and 1 for volumes. CYP2C19 was identified as a significant covariate for CL/F. No significant differences in lansoprazole exposure were observed between children with and without obesity, with both dosing approaches. Higher exposure was noted in Poor/Intermediate metabolizers of CYP2C19. FFM-based dosing led to similar levels of exposure between children (aged 6-11) and adolescents (aged 12-17).

**Conclusions:** 
Obesity was not associated with differences in lansoprazole pharmacokinetics in children. A FFM-based dosing approach could result in comparable exposure between children and adolescents. Dose adjustments are supported for Poor/Intermediate metabolizers of CYP2C19.

Keywords: pediatrics, population pharmacokinetic modeling, lansoprazole, PPI, CYP2C19 genotype, obesity

## 1. Introduction

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), such as lansoprazole, are among the most commonly prescribed medications worldwide, with increasing use in the pediatric setting [[1](#R1)]. Despite their widespread use and perceived safety, potential adverse effects and toxicities, such as infections, micronutrient deficiency, and increased risk of bone fractures, have been associated with PPI use, especially in long-term use [[2](#R2)]. Currently, lansoprazole is approved for the treatment of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and erosive esophagitis in pediatric patients 1 to 17 years of age [[3](#R3)]. Given that obesity is a recognized risk factor for GERD, children with obesity are more likely to receive PPIs [[4](#R4)]. Consequently, these children may be at an increased risk of experiencing adverse effects and toxicities.

Obesity is associated with changes in the body relevant to drug pharmacokinetics (PK), including changes in metabolic enzymes, increased cardiac output, altered blood flow, and a shift in body composition towards more fat and less lean tissue per kilogram of body weight [[5](#R5)]. These changes may explain differences in drug PK between people with obesity and those without [[5](#R5)]. Concerning PPIs, in a recent study, adults with obesity showed lower omeprazole apparent absolute oral drug clearance (CL/F) when compared to adults without obesity [[6](#R6)]. Similar observations were demonstrated for pantoprazole in three independent pediatric studies, where patients with obesity exhibit reduced clearance compared to patients without obesity [[7](#R7)–[9](#R9)]. Regarding lansoprazole, studies are lacking about the influence of obesity on the drug’s PK.

Lansoprazole is rapidly absorbed after oral administration in healthy adult volunteers, as well as in children and adolescents with GERD, with a time to reach maximum plasma concentration varying between 1 and 3 hours [[10](#R10)]. It exhibits linear PK over the dose range of 15 – 60 mg and is highly bound to plasma proteins (>95%) [[10](#R10)]. Similarly to other PPIs, lansoprazole is extensively metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2C19 and CYP3A4, with less than 2% of the dose excreted unchanged in urine [[11](#R11)]. The effect of CYP2C19 polymorphisms on lansoprazole PK has been previously reported in a population PK (popPK) study including healthy adult volunteers [[12](#R12)]. Following an intravenous short infusion, the mean values of CL for Intermediate and Poor metabolizers were estimated to be 60.9% and 21.7% of the CL for Normal metabolizers, respectively [[12](#R12)].

Taking into consideration the increased incidence of childhood obesity in the United States, the lack of lansoprazole popPK studies in children, and no specific dosing recommendations for patients with obesity, the present study aims to characterize lansoprazole’s PK in children with and without obesity, identify relevant covariates, and perform simulations to compare two dosing approaches (United States Food and Drug Administration [FDA]-approved Total Body Weight [TBW]-tiered dosing and Fat-free Mass [FFM]-based dosing) in this population.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Study Design

Plasma lansoprazole concentration data were obtained from a prospective, single-center, open-label study with subjects between 6-21 years of age ([www.clinicaltrials.gov](http://www.clinicaltrials.gov) [NCT04248335](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04248335)). The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent and assent were obtained from the participants and their parents or legally authorized representatives (caregivers) prior to any research-related procedures. Patients were considered ineligible for the study if they had received treatment with PPIs and/or medication known to inhibit or induce CYP2C19 and CYP3A4 in the last three days; a body weight exceeding 227 kg; a history of adverse reaction to previous PPIs exposure; impaired hepatic function (values ≥ 5 times the age-specific upper limit of normal [ULN] for aspartate transaminase [AST], alanine transaminase [ALT], and alkaline phosphatase or total bilirubin > 0.2 mg/dL); impaired renal function (creatinine ≥ 3 times the age-specific ULN); any known infection with hepatitis B, C, or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV); or were pregnant or breastfeeding.

The participants received one oral dose of lansoprazole (Prevacid^®^ delayed-release tablets), approximately 1.2 mg/kg FFM [[13](#R13)] rounded up to the nearest whole tablet not exceeding 60 mg, on an empty stomach (≥ 4 hours fasted). Blood samples were taken before and 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 6, 8, and 10 hours after study drug administration and placed into blood collection tubes containing sodium heparin, immediately mixed by inversion, and centrifuged (1,000 g for 10 minutes at 4°C). Plasma was then separated and frozen within 1 hour at −70°C until analysis. Additional blood samples were obtained for *CYP2C19* genotyping and for a single measurement of inflammatory and liver function markers. In addition, participants underwent magnetic resonance (MR) imaging of the liver on a 1.5T scanner (Avanto Fit, Siemens Healthineers). The MR images were post-processed using LiverLab software (Siemens Healthineers), in accordance with standard radiologic safety practices, including Siemens’ e-Dixon for liver volume measurement and Siemens’ MR Spectroscopy HISTO to estimate proton density fat fraction (HFF) from a single voxel in the liver parenchyma. The whole liver volumes estimations were manually verified by a pediatric radiologist.

Participants were classified in different CYP2C19 metabolizer phenotypes in accordance with the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) standardized nomenclature and their individual *CYP2C19* diplotypes [[14](#R14)]. Individuals were considered Poor metabolizers if carrying two no function alleles (*2/*2, *3/*3, or *2/*3); Intermediate metabolizers if carrying one normal function allele and one no function allele, or one increased function allele and one no function allele (*1/*2, *1/*3, *2/*17, or *3/*17); Normal metabolizers if carrying two normal function alleles (*1/*1); Rapid metabolizers if carrying one normal function allele and one increased function allele (*1/*17); and Ultrarapid metabolizers if carrying two increased function alleles (*17/*17).

### 2.2. Bioanalytical Methods

Plasma lansoprazole concentrations were determined using a validated chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method performed in positive electrospray ionization (ESI+) mode using a Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, Wilmslow, UK). Briefly, plasma proteins were precipitated by adding 200 μL of acetonitrile to 100 μL of plasma spiked with 10 μL of working internal standard solution (1000 ng/mL Pantoprazole/Pantoprazole Sulfide/Omeprazole mix) and vortexed for at least 30 seconds at 2000 rpm. Samples were then centrifuged for 5 minutes at 13,000 rpm, the supernatant (270 μL) was diluted in water (200 μL) and injected into the chromatographic system. Analytes were separated on a Waters Acquity UPLC reversed phase column (BEH C18 1.7μm, 2.1 x 100mm). The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both with 0.1% formic acid, operating at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min with gradient, starting with 2% B, ramping up linearly to 25% B at 4.0 minutes and held for 1.7 minutes. At 5.7 minutes the column was ramped up to 98.0% of B and held for 0.3 minutes at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, returning to initial conditions at 6 minutes. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 1 ng/mL. The inter- and intra- assay accuracy and precision were within ± 15% [[15](#R15)].

The concentrations of cytokines/chemokines were measured in serum using the MILLIPLEX MAP Human Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, MA, USA). The customized 21-plex cytokine/chemokine panel consisted of the following analytes: granulocyte macrophage–colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), fractalkine, interferon (IFN)-α2, IFN-γ, interleukin (IL)-10, IL-12p40, IL-13, IL-15, IL-17A, IL-1Rα, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, interferon-γ-inducible protein (IP)–10, chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2)/monocyte chemoattractant protein–1 (MCP-1), CCL3/MIP-1α, chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5)/regulated upon activation, normal T cell expressed and secreted (RANTES), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)–α. Samples were measured in duplicate, and the concentration was calculated by reference to the standard curve for each cytokine. The average coefficient of variation was measured between the duplicates, and values <30 were considered acceptable. All samples were measured undiluted. The lower detection limit for each cytokine was as follows: 7.5 pg/mL for GM-CSF, 22.7 pg/mL for fractalkine, 2.9 pg/mL for IFN-α2, 0.8 pg/mL for IFN-γ, 1.1 pg/mL for IL-10, 7.4 pg/mL for IL-12p40, 1.3 pg/mL for IL-13, 1.2 pg/mL for IL-15, 0.7 pg/mL for IL-17A, 8.3 pg/mL for IL-1Rα, 0.8 pg/mL for IL-1β, 1.0 pg/mL for IL-2, 4.5 pg/ mL for IL-4, 0.5 pg/mL for IL-5, 0.9 pg/mL for IL-6, 0.4 pg/ mL for IL-8, 8.6 pg/mL for IP-10, 1.9 pg/mL for MCP-1, 2.9 pg/mL for MIP-1α, 1.2 pg/mL for RANTES, and 0.7 pg/mL for TNF-α.

### 2.3. Base Population Pharmacokinetic Model Development

Lansoprazole concentration-time data were analyzed using nonlinear mixed-effects modeling in NONMEM v.7.4.3 (Icon Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA) [[16](#R16)] with the first-order conditional estimation with interaction method and the ADVAN13 subroutine. Perl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN) v.4.7.0 [[17](#R17)] and Pirana v.23.1.1 (Certara USA, Inc., USA) [[18](#R18)] were used to automate, manage, and evaluate NONMEM runs. R v.4.3.1 with the ‘xpose4’ v.4.7.2, ‘xpose’ v.0.4.17, ‘tidyverse’ v. 2.0.0, ‘[table1](#T1)’ v.1.4.3, ‘gridExtra’ v.2.3, and ‘ggpubr’ v.0.6.0 packages were used to support model development through data visualization and graphical analysis. Both one- and two-compartment structural models were explored. In order to characterize the delayed absorption of lansoprazole, different absorption models were evaluated, including first-order absorption with and without lag-time, zero-order absorption, transit compartments, and Weibull absorption [[19](#R19)]. Additive, proportional, and combined (proportional plus additive) residual error models were evaluated. Inter-individual variability (IIV) in PK parameters was evaluated using an exponential model ([Equation 1](#FD1)):

| Pij=θPop,j×exp(ηij) | (1) |
| --- | --- |
where *Pij* denotes the estimate of parameter *j* in the *i*th individual, *θPop,j* is the population value for parameter *j*, and *ηij* denotes the deviation from the average population value for parameter *j* in the *i*th individual. The random variable *η* is assumed to be normally distributed with a mean zero and a variance *ω*^2^. Data below the quantification limit (BQL) were evaluated with different methods: M1 (all BQL observations discarded from the analysis), M3 method (likelihood-based), M5 method (all BQL samples replaced with LLOQ/2), M6 method (BQL samples prior to the first observation replaced with 0 and samples prior to the first measurable concentration with LLOQ/2), and M7 method (all BQL observations replaced by zero) [[20](#R20)].

Body size descriptors, including total body weight (TBW), lean-body mass (LBM) [[21](#R21)], body surface area (BSA) [[22](#R22)], and fat-free mass (FFM) [[23](#R23)] were considered to account for the effect of body size on the PK of lansoprazole. The PK parameters were scaled according to the [Equation 2](#FD2):

| Pij=Pj×(wiwstandard)β | (2) |
| --- | --- |
where *Pij* is the estimate of parameter *j* in the *i*th individual, *Wi* is the measure of body size (TBW, BSA, or FFM) in the *i*th individual, *Pj* is the parameter in an individual with a standard measure of body size, and *Wstandard* is the standard measure of body size with 70 kg for TBW, 54 kg for LBM, 1.73 m^2^ for BSA, and 56 kg for FFM. The exponent *β* was either estimated or fixed to 0.75 for clearance parameters (CL and Q) and 1 for volume of distribution parameters (central and peripheral) [[24](#R24), [25](#R25)].

### 2.4. Covariate Model

Clinically relevant covariates were initially evaluated by assessing trends in plots of the individual parameter estimates or the estimates of interindividual variability parameters (ETAs) versus the covariate. Covariates considered in the analysis included patient demographics (age, race, BMI percentile, and weight category [with or without obesity]), CYP2C19 phenotype (Poor/Intermediate metabolizer, Normal metabolizer, or Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizer), hepatic fat fraction (HFF), liver volume, laboratory values (glucose, white blood cells count, hemoglobin, ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, total and direct bilirubin, albumin, total protein levels, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and electrolytes [sodium, calcium, potassium, and chloride]), and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, RANTES, IP-10, IL-1Ra, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-15, MCP-1, and TNF-α). Missing continuous covariate values were imputed using the median values of the covariate entries from children for whom covariate values were reported. Covariates with over 25% missing values were not included in the analysis. Potential covariates were implemented in the model using a stepwise forward inclusion and backward elimination procedure [[26](#R26)]. Inclusion was considered when it resulted in a drop in the objective function value (OFV) of at least 3.84 (p < 0.05, for 1 degree of freedom) and backward deletion gave an OFV increase of at least 6.64 points (p < 0.01, for 1 degree of freedom). Plots of individual influence on change in OFV (shark plots) in Xpose4 were used to ascertain whether the statistical significance was driven by a sufficient number of individuals [[27](#R27)].

### 2.5. Model Evaluation

The selected popPK model was evaluated according to visual inspection of the goodness-of-fit plots, condition number, precision of model parameters (as measured by the asymptotic standard error), and biological plausibility. The robustness of the parameter estimates in the final model were assessed using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1,000 bootstrap replicate datasets created with sampling with replacement. Also, a prediction-corrected visual prediction check (pcVPC) [[28](#R28)] with 1,000 simulation replicates was performed to visually compare simulated and observed concentration-time data. Observed and simulated data were binned by time and compared with respect to their 5^th^, 50^th^, and 95^th^ percentiles.

### 2.6. Simulations

The final PopPK model was used to simulate lansoprazole exposure metrics, including the maximum concentration in the dosing interval (C_max_) and area under the concentration-time curve from 0 to infinity (AUC_0-∞_), following two dosing scenarios: FDA-approved TBW-tiered dosing for children (15 mg for 1 to 11 years if ≤ 30 kg, and 30 mg if older or > 30 kg) and 1.2 mg/kg FFM with a maximum dose of 60 mg. The predicted C_max_ and AUC_0-∞_ (calculated based on Empirical Bayes Estimate of CL/F) were assessed without accounting for residual unexplained variability by comparing the distribution of the exposure metrics between children and adolescents with and without obesity, for each age group (6 to ≤ 12 years and 12 to 18 years), CYP2C19 phenotype, and dosing regimen.

Virtual populations of children with obesity (BMI ≥ 95th percentile for sex and age) ages 6 to ≤ 12 years and 12 to 18 years (1,000 subjects for each age group) were generated using a previously published virtual population [[29](#R29)]. The same age groups of children without obesity were generated using the White American population in the physiological-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling software PK-Sim^®^ version 11 (Open Systems Pharmacology Suite, [open-systems-pharmacology.org](https://open-systems-pharmacology.org)) where 1,000 individuals were randomly selected from 1,500 individuals, based on the criteria of a BMI percentile of less than 95th, to avoid inclusion of children with obesity randomly generated in PK-Sim. Simulations were performed considering different CYP2C19 phenotypes (Poor/Intermediate metabolizer, Normal metabolizer, or Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizer) for each simulated population.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Data

A total of 537 lansoprazole concentrations from 47 study participants were included in the analysis. Approximately 11% of the data were BLQ, primarily during the absorption phase (within 2 hours of dose administration). One participant was excluded from analysis due to apparently implausible concentration-time data, with a time to maximum plasma concentration of 8 hours. A summary of selected baseline demographics and clinical characteristics is presented in [Table 1](#T1) and a complete summary of baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, and dosing information from the study groups is presented in the [supplementary Table 1](#SD1) of the [electronic supplementary material (ESM)](#SD1). The median (range) age was 14.3 years (7.50, 20.3), with a median weight of 64.4 kg (22.0, 149), and a median BMI of 25.4 kg/m^2^ (15.0, 49.7), with 51.1% of patients with a BMI percentile ≥ 95^th^. The majority of participants consisted of CYP2C19 Normal metabolizers (48.9%), followed by Rapid and Intermediate metabolizers (25.5% and 19.1%, respectively). Poor metabolizers accounted for 4.3% of the patient population, whereas only one patient was identified as an Ultrarapid metabolizer (2.1%).

### 3.2. Population Pharmacokinetic Analysis

A two-compartment model with Weibull absorption and linear elimination best described the observed lansoprazole PK data. The process of base model selection is summarized in [supplementary Table 2A](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1). Different absorption models were tested to characterize the highly variable absorption of lansoprazole. The use of the Weibull function allowed the modeling of time-varying absorption rate ([Equation 3](#FD3)), better characterizing the absorption profile.

| Ka(h−1)=Ka,asymp×(1−e−(k×t)γ) | (3) |
| --- | --- |
In this function, *Ka,asymp* represents the asymptotic value of *Ka, k* is the time constant and *γ* is a shape factor. Interindividual variability was estimated for clearance (CL/F), central volume of distribution (V_1_/F), and the absorption time constant (*k*). Attempts to include variability in other parameters resulted in high shrinkage. Assessment of the BQL data using the different methods did not improve the model fit to the data and resulted in similar parameter estimates. Therefore, the M1 method was selected for treating BQL observations.

The model with the PK parameters allometrically scaled to FFM, with fixed exponents of 0.75 for clearance parameters (CL/F and Q/F) and 1 for volume of distribution parameters (V_1_/F and V_2_/F), performed better in terms of relative change in objective function value (ΔOFV) compared to the other body size descriptors and was selected as the base structural model ([Supplementary Table 2B](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1)). An attempt to estimate the exponents resulted in improbable estimates of allometric relationships or high uncertainty of estimates (RSE > 40%).

The covariate analysis, following forward inclusion (p-value < 0.05) and backward elimination (p-value < 0.01), revealed that CYP2C19 phenotype (Poor/Intermediate metabolizer, Normal metabolizer, or Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizer) and weight status (with or without obesity) were both significantly correlated to CL/F. Also, blood glucose levels showed a positive correlation with V_1_/F. As expected, children with the Poor/Intermediate metabolizer CYP2C19 phenotype exhibited lower CL/F levels than Normal metabolizers, which presented lower absolute CL/F values than Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizers. With regards to weight status, children with obesity presented lower absolute CL/F values when compared to children without obesity. However, only one individual contributed to the statistical significance of weight status on CL/F and blood glucose levels on V_1_/F ([Supplementary Figures 2](#SD1), [3](#SD1), and [4](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1)). Only the effect of CYP2C19 phenotype on CL/F was retained in the final model. [Supplementary Figure 1](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1) shows the correlation between the random effects of the base model and these covariates. Patients with a Poor/Intermediate metabolizer phenotype had an estimated absolute CL/F approximately 40% lower when compared to the Normal metabolizer group, whereas the Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizer group had an estimated absolute CL/F approximately 15% higher when compared to the same group. The PK parameters of lansoprazole for the final model are presented in [Table 2](#T2).

## 4. Discussion

This prospective, single-center, open-label study characterized the PK of lansoprazole in children and adolescents with and without obesity after a single oral dose. With a model-based approach, differences in exposure were evaluated considering age group, weight (obesity status), and *CYP2C19* genotype through simulation of two dosing strategies. This is the first pediatric popPK model for lansoprazole, where the concentration-time data was best characterized by a two-compartment structural PK model with a time-dependent absorption (Weibull absorption model) and first-order elimination. After accounting for differences in body size between individuals with and without obesity, the effect of CYP2C19 metabolizer phenotype on CL/F was the only significant covariate included in the model. Simulations showed that FFM-based dosing approach leads to similar exposures when comparing children (6-11 years of age) with adolescents (12-17 years of age) with the same CYP2C19 metabolizer phenotype.

Lansoprazole PK was also characterized by a 2-compartment model in adults following a 30-minutes infusion of 30 mg to 56 individuals [[12](#R12)]. The subjects were divided into three categories based on their CYP2C19 phenotype, namely Normal metabolizers, Intermediate metabolizers, and Poor metabolizers, with no patients with the Rapid or Ultrarapid metabolizers phenotype included. The results showed that the clearance estimates of Normal metabolizers were similar to the ones in the current study (12.53 L/h and 14.1 L/h, respectively). The clearance for the Intermediate metabolizers group was estimated to be 61.2% of the Normal metabolizers CL, which is similar to the current study where the Poor/Intermediate metabolizers group had an estimated CL/F of 57.8% of the Normal metabolizers CL/F. We were unable to differentiate between Intermediate metabolizers and Poor metabolizers due to the low number of Poor metabolizers patients. This may have contributed to the difference in CL estimates between patients in our study and those in adult study. Estimates for distribution volumes and intercompartmental clearance were different between the two studies. The central volume of distribution was reported to be lower (7.7 L versus 11.9 L) and the estimates for the peripheral compartment were reported to be higher (6.2 L/h vs. 1.93 L/h and 14.07 L vs. 3.14 L, for Q and V2, respectively) for adult subjects compared to our pediatric population. It is possible that the differences observed were due to the route of drug administration (intravenous versus oral) or the approach used to scale body weight. Our model employed allometric scaling with a clearance parameter exponent of 0.75, whereas their analysis relied on a linear relationship with an exponent of 1 for all model parameters.

By incorporating allometrically scaled parameters to a body size descriptor, PK parameters were standardized to a standard weight of 70 kg, which improved model performance by reducing OFV and explaining some of the interindividual variability. FFM was found to be the most effective body size descriptor based on the reduction in the OFV, reduction in interindividual variability in V_1_/F by 30% (reducing it from 59.5% to 41.9%) and CL/F by 4% (reducing it from 69.5% to 66.6%). After accounting for body size, three covariates were found to have statistically significant effects in the model: *CYP2C19* genotype and weight status (with or without obesity) on CL/F, and blood glucose level on V_1_/F. In this model with all the covariates, it was estimated that children with obesity showed a slightly lower CL/F (11.7 L/h for Normal metabolizers) in comparison to children without obesity (17.9 L/h for Normal metabolizers), which is consistent with the findings of previous studies that analyzed the pharmacokinetics of a PPIs in pediatric patients [[7](#R7)–[9](#R9)]. In a prospective study involving 62 children aged 6-17 years, with and without obesity, weight-normalized CL/F (L/h/kg) of pantoprazole was lower in children with obesity [[8](#R8)]. Similarly, an independent prospective study and a popPK analysis of pantoprazole concentrations from 40 children with obesity, aged 6–17 years, revealed that the typical estimates of CL/F were reduced compared to historical pediatric peers without obesity [[9](#R9)]. Furthermore, another recent prospective popPK study of oral and intravenous pantoprazole that included pediatric patients with and without obesity found that obesity was associated with an estimated 18% reduction in CL [[7](#R7)]. In our study of lansoprazole, however, obesity was not included as a covariate in our final model, as this relationship appeared to be driven by only one subject ([Supplementary Figure 2](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1)). Blood glucose levels and their perceived relationship to V_1_/F were excluded from the final model for the same reason ([Supplementary Figure 3](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1)). It is interesting to note that a positive correlation was observed between blood glucose levels and V_1_/F, where a 10% increase in blood glucose levels from the median of the population value was associated with a 48% increase in V_1_/F. This relationship could be linked to changes in drug absorption, as high blood sugar levels can cause transient slowing of gastric emptying [[30](#R30)]. In such cases, the absorption of an acid-sensitive drug could be reduced, potentially leading to higher estimates of apparent volume of distribution. When this relationship was explored for absorption related parameters (time constant of the time-dependent absorption), the correlation was not as strong as for V_1_/F. Further studies are needed to better understand the potential connection between blood glucose and variability in drug pharmacokinetics in children.

Other potential physiological changes associated with obesity (e.g., increased liver fat and liver volume, elevated inflammatory and cytokine burden) were also explored in our model. Although patients with obesity demonstrated a higher hepatic fat fraction and liver volume ([Supplementary Table 1](#SD1)), no clear relationship between these covariates and lansoprazole PK parameters was found ([Supplementary Figure 9](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1)). Similarly, no apparent trends were observed with clinical markers of inflammation (e.g., C-reactive protein) or inflammatory cytokines (IL-1Ra, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-15, IP-10, MCP-1, TNF-α) ([Supplementary Figures 10](#SD1) to [13](#SD1) of [ESM](#SD1)). However, the sample size of children who had these measures available was relatively small in this study, making relationship(s) assessment challenging. Consequently, these covariates were not selected in model development.

The developed popPK model was applied to investigate different dosing strategies in virtual patients with and without obesity, to provide more insights into the dosing strategy for pediatric patients with obesity. As currently there are no dosing recommendations in pediatric patients with obesity, we simulated two dosing strategies: FDA-approved TBW-tiered dosing and FFM-based dosing approach. Simulations showed that, when comparing patients within the same age group, CYP2C19 phenotype, and dosing strategies, lansoprazole exposure is similar between individuals with and without obesity. Within each group, FFM-based dosing approach resulted in slightly higher exposures compared to FDA-approved TBW-tiered dosing. Nevertheless, for CYP2C19 Normal metabolizers, both dosing strategies reach median exposures comparable to the adult values (reported means ranging from 1.76 to 3.89 mg.h/L after oral dose of 30 mg), suggesting that FFM-based dosing achieves exposure levels within adult reference values [[31](#R31)]. However, when comparing both age groups, simulations demonstrate that the FDA-approved TBW-tiered dosing results in higher levels of exposure in the younger pediatric group (6-11 years of age), and particularly those with obesity. This suggests that adopting an FFM-based dosing approach could maintain consistent exposure when treating pediatric patients.

Overall, despite exploration of multiple potential covariates, *CYP2C19* genotype predominated as the driving force behind variability in lansoprazole PK for children with and without obesity. The polymorphically expressed CYP2C19 enzyme plays a significant role in lansoprazole metabolism, with over 80% of drug metabolism dependent on this enzyme [[32](#R32)]. In general, individuals with lower CYP2C19 activity (Poor/Intermediate metabolizers) presented a simulated lansoprazole exposure (AUC_0-∞_) approximately 73% higher compared to the CYP2C19 Normal metabolizers group. The difference in exposure was less pronounced when comparing Normal metabolizers to Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizer individuals, with around 13% lower exposure in the latter group.

According to the CPIC guideline for chronic therapy with PPIs, the standard daily dose should be reduced by 50% in Intermediate and Poor metabolizers individuals [[14](#R14)]. In contrast, an increased dose between 50-100% should be considered for rapid and Normal metabolizers to treat *H. pylori* infection and erosive esophagitis. Our findings support the recommended dose reduction of approximately 50% to achieve similar exposure levels in Poor/Intermediate metabolizers as compared to Normal metabolizers in pediatric patients. In addition, no increase in dosing seems to be necessary for Rapid/Ultrarapid metabolizers.

There are noteworthy limitations to our study. First, the relatively small sample size, especially for less common CYP2C19 phenotypes (Poor and Ultrarapid metabolizers), may have influenced the analysis of covariates in this study. For instance, a statistically significant relationship between weight status and blood glucose levels and PK parameters was determined to have been driven by only one patient. Second, a substantial percentage of samples were BQL, particularly in the absorption phase. Although we found that these samples did not bias the parameter estimates by considering the M3 method during model development, they could have influenced the characterization of lansoprazole’s absorption. It is also worth mentioning that lansoprazole is approved for pediatric patients aged 1 year and older, while our model included patients aged 6 years and above. Therefore, its applicability to younger children is uncertain, particularly those under 2 years of age, as ontogeny-related differences in drug metabolism and elimination may impact lansoprazole’s PK. Last, conclusions were drawn solely based on the differences in model-predicted systemic exposure between children with and without obesity, assuming no impact of obesity status on PPI pharmacodynamics. Despite these limitations, our findings contribute important insights toward lansoprazole dosing strategies in pediatric patients with obesity and suggest further research is needed to inform decision-making in the pediatric setting.

## 5. Conclusion

A popPK model was developed to characterize lansoprazole pharmacokinetics in children with and without obesity with different *CYP2C19* genotypes. The findings suggest that the FFM-based dosing approach could result in a more consistent systemic exposure between children (6-11 years of age) and adolescents (12-17 years of age), as compared to the FDA-approved TBW-tiered dosing. Our pediatric study supports CPIC’s recommendation to consider a 50% reduction in the standard daily dose of lansoprazole for children with CYP2C19 Intermediate and Poor metabolizer phenotypes, adding valuable pediatric knowledge to the relative paucity of PPI dose individualization evidence for children, compared to adults.
