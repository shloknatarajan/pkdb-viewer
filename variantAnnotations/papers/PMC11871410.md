# Updated Analysis of the Pharmacogenomics of Pediatric Bronchodilator Response


## Abstract

This short communication serves as an update to previously published pilot study results on bronchodilator response (BDR) in children with asthma (“Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) in PRKG1 and SPATA13-AS1 are associated with bronchodilator response: a pilot study during acute asthma exacerbations in African American children.” Fishe, et al. Pharmacogenetics and Genomics, 2021;31:146–154). We expanded our cohort from 54 to 165 pediatric patients seeking emergency department care from an asthma exacerbation. We obtained measured BDR before and after albuterol administration using the Pediatric Asthma Severity Score (PASS) and collected genomic DNA. Based on a literature review, we analyzed whether 21 candidate SNPs were associated with BDR. Among the 3 SNP’s initially reported in our pilot study as significantly associated with BDR (rs912142, rs7081864, and rs7903366), we confirmed that rs7081864 was still significantly associated with suboptimal BDR (OR = 0.47, CI = [0.24, 0.92]). If externally validated in broader studies, simple outpatient testing for that SNP variant could help guide pharmacologic therapy for acute asthma symptoms.


## Introduction

Asthma is the most common chronic disease of childhood in the United States, affecting an estimated 4.6 million children.1 Of those children, 39% experience at least one acute asthma exacerbation each year.1 Asthma exacerbations result in an estimated 270,000 emergency department (ED) and 27,000 hospital admissions annually,2 as well as over 13 million missed days of school.3

The front-line, first therapy for any acute worsening of asthma symptoms are short-acting beta-2 agonist bronchodilators such as albuterol. Yet potentially 1 in 5 children do not respond well to albuterol.4 Despite that, there are no guidelines for asthma exacerbation therapy for patients with suboptimal bronchodilator response, nor are there evidence-based alternative and/or novel therapies.

Prior studies have explored variation in bronchodilator response (BDR) due to single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).5 However, those studies did not measure BDR in the context of when it matters most, during an asthma exacerbation. Our pilot study (“Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) in PRKG1 and SPATA13-AS1 are associated with bronchodilator response: a pilot study during acute asthma exacerbations in African American children.” Fishe, et al. Pharmacogenetics and Genomics, 2021;31:146–154)6 was the first study to examine BDR in the context of an acute exacerbation and study an inclusive range of children (2–18 years of age). In our analysis of 54 children seeking emergency department (ED) care for an asthma exacerbation, we examined 21 candidate SNPs in a variety of genes (NFKB1, SPATA13-AS1, ADRB2, PRKG1, TXNDC8, ADRB2, ARG1, THRB, SPATAS2L, IL33) and two non-coding regions (near EPHA3 and near ASB3).6 We found that rs912142 in SPATA13-AS1 was associated with decreased odds of having low BDR (odds ratio (OR), 0.20; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.02–0.92), and that rs7081864 in PRKG1 and rs7903366 in PRKG1 were associated with decreased odds of having high BDR (OR, 0.097; 95% CI, 0.009–0.62). In this research update, we present results of the same analyses run on a larger, expanded cohort of 165 children.


## Methods:

In brief, we enrolled pediatric patients ages 2 – 18 years seeking care for an acute asthma exacerbation in the University of Florida Health Jacksonville Pediatric ED. Our original study included only African American patients, for this expanded analysis we included all races/ethnicities. Pediatric asthma exacerbation care is guided by a clinical pathway that advises between one and three initial treatments of albuterol, either alone or in combination with ipratropium bromide, and systemic corticosteroids. Other therapies are at the treating physician’s discretion. Standard ED practice is to measure severity using the Pediatric Asthma Severity Score (PASS),7 before and after all bronchodilator treatments. The PASS is a numeric severity score validated for use in the emergent setting and ranges from 0 to 6.

BDR was calculated as the change in the PASS score from before the first bronchodilator treatment to after the last bronchodilator treatment. We adjusted the BDR for initial PASS score and number of bronchodilator treatments given, and categorized BDR into terciles (low, medium and high) based on the sample population distribution.

After initial stabilization, patients underwent informed consent, and buccal swab samples were collected for genomic DNA extraction. We examined 21 candidate SNPs based off a literature review.6 We computed SNP crude odds ratios (OR). We then adjusted the ORs for age (2–9 years versus 10–18 years old), gender, body mass index > 25, and home tobacco smoke exposure using logistic regression. We present results as ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at a P value < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R.

This study was approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board. Please see our original publication for detailed methods.6


## Results:

We enrolled a total of 165 children. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. Among the 3 SNP’s initially significantly associated with BDR (rs912142, rs7081864, and rs7903366), we confirmed that rs7081864 in PRKG1 was still significantly associated with suboptimal BDR to treatment with decreased odds (OR=0.47, CI = [0.24, 0.92]) of observing a large decrease in the PASS score.


## Discussion:

In this expanded study of children with an active asthma exacerbation, we found that rs7081864 was persistently associated with suboptimal BDR. rs7081864 is located in PRKG1, a gene that encodes for a cyclic GMP-dependent protein kinase.8 PRKG1 is ubiquitously expressed in the lung, and acts as a key mediator of the nitric oxide/cGMP signaling pathway that affects smooth muscle relaxation.8 Thus, there is a plausible biological explanation for how SNPs in PRKG1 may relate to differential BDR in children with asthma.

Although 90% of our participants self-identified as African American, our results align with those of Spear, et al, in an analysis of African American and Latino adults and children which found that rs7081864 was associated with reduced BDR.9 Related, studies in China and Saudi Arabia have found other SNPs in PRKG1 to be associated with the incidence of childhood asthma.10,11 Thus, multiple studies triangulate on PRKG1 as a gene associated with asthma incidence and BDR in multiple races and ethnicities.

How can these findings inform clinical care of children with asthma? Once widely validated, knowledge of a SNP predictive of BDR, particularly suboptimal BDR, could inform evidence-based care. In the primary care setting, patients could have genotyping performed, similar to what is routinely done now for cyp2c19 prior to initiation of anticoagulant therapy.12 Knowledge of alleles of interest that predict suboptimal BDR could then inform an asthma action plan with i) multiple rescue strategies (such as inhaled corticosteroids with long-acting beta agonists)13 and/or ii) earlier advisement to seek emergent care for adjunctive therapies such as intravenous magnesium. Integration of those results into the electronic health record could provide real-time alerts to treating physicians in the emergency setting of potential suboptimal BDR, which could guide more aggressive early therapy and potentially improved outcomes (e.g., decreased intensive care unit admissions, decreased hospital admissions).


## Conclusion:

The SNP rs7081864 in PRKG1 has been found in our initial and expanded study to be associated with suboptimal BDR in children experiencing an acute asthma exacerbation. If validated across a broader population, testing for that SNP could help inform and improve primary and emergency care for children with asthma.
