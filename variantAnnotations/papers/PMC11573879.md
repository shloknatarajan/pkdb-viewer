## Abstract

**Background and Objective:** 
Codeine metabolism in humans is complex due to the involvement of multiple cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, and has a strong genetic underpinning, which determines the levels of relevant CYP450 enzyme expression in vivo. Polymorphic CYP2D6 metabolises codeine to morphine via O-demethylation, while a strong correlation between CYP2D6 phenotype and opioidergic adverse effects of codeine is well documented. The aim of this study was to quantify the effect of CYP2D6 genotype on the biotransformation of codeine.

**Methods:** 
We conducted a prospective clinical trial with 1000 patients, during which ambulatory patients were administered 60 mg of codeine preoperatively and the association between CYP2D6 activity and morphine exposure across various CYP2D6 genotypes was quantified using a population pharmacokinetic model. Plasma concentration data for codeine and its primary metabolites were obtained from 997 patients and CYP2D6 genotype was screened for study subjects, and respective sums of activity scores assigned for each CYP2D6 allele were used as covariates in model development.

**Results:** 
Our final model predicts the disposition of codeine and the formation of morphine, codeine-6-glucuronide and morphine-3-glucuronide adequately while accounting for variability in morphine exposure on the basis of CYP2D6 genotype. In agreement with previous results, patients with decreased function alleles (CYP2D6*10 and *41) showed varying levels of decrease in CYP2D6 activity that were inconsistent with increasing activity scores. Model simulations demonstrate that morphine concentrations in ultrarapid CYP2D6 metabolisers reach systemic concentrations that can potentially cause respiratory depression (over 9.1 ng/mL), and have 218% higher exposure (19 versus 8.7 µg · h/L, p < 0.001) to morphine than normal metabolisers. Similarly, poor and intermediate metabolisers had significantly reduced morphine exposure (1.0 and 3.7 versus 8.7 µg · h/L, p < 0.001) as compared with normal metabolisers.

**Conclusions:** 
Our final model leads the way in implementing model-informed precision dosing in codeine therapy and identifies the use of genetic testing as an integral component in the effort to implement rational pharmacotherapy with codeine.

## Key Points

## Introduction

It has been estimated that no less than 30% of day-case surgery patients suffer from severe or moderate pain at 24 h after surgery [[1](#CR1), [2](#CR2)]. Multi-modal analgesia with codeine, paracetamol and non-steroidal analgesic agents has proven effective in treating severe postoperative pain after ambulatory surgery. Codeine is a prodrug that is metabolised to morphine via cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6), which is followed by a UGT2B7 to biologically inactive morphine-3-glucuronide and active analgesic morphine-6-glucuronide representing approximately 90% and 10% of morphine first-order elimination, respectively. The *CYP2D6* gene is highly variable, and different genotypes of *CYP2D6* lead to wide interpatient variation in metabolic activity and consequent drug disposition [[3](#CR3)–[6](#CR6)].

The effect of *CYP2D6* genotype on the analgesic effect of codeine has been demonstrated in various experimental and clinical settings [[7](#CR7)–[11](#CR11)]. Individuals lacking *CYP2D6* activity, that is, poor metabolisers, suffer from poor analgesia from codeine, whereas ultrarapid metabolisers may experience exaggerated and potentially life-threatening adverse effects [[7](#CR7), [8](#CR8)]. The association of CYP2D6 phenotype with morphine-linked adverse effects is well known, while the in vivo activities of various *CYP2D6* allelic variants and genotypes have not been quantified during codeine therapy.

*CYP2D6* genotypes are commonly translated into four metaboliser phenotypes: poor metabolisers (PM), intermediate metabolisers (IM), normal metabolisers (NM) and ultrarapid metabolisers (UM) [[5](#CR5), [6](#CR6)]. The CYP2D6 activity score assigns each allele an activity score value, and the sum of these forms the activity score for each genotype [[12](#CR12)]. Activity score ranges are then used to define four CYP2D6 phenotype classes. Activity scores can also be used without phenotype classification to more precisely capture CYP2D6 activity [[5](#CR5), [13](#CR13), [14](#CR14)]. Several studies have evaluated the assignment of *CYP2D6* alleles with different substrates in clinical settings [[13](#CR13)–[16](#CR16)]. However, most of these studies are retrospective and have evaluated pooled data from several small studies with a limited number of *CYP2D6* allele variants (e.g. *CYP2D6*10*).

We conducted a prospective clinical trial with 1000 patients, during which ambulatory patients were administered 60 mg of codeine preoperatively. Our aim was to quantify the effect of *CYP2D6* genotype on the biotransformation of codeine. To achieve this aim, we developed a population pharmacokinetic model using plasma codeine, morphine, codeine-6-glucuronide (C6G) and morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G) plasma concentrations and *CYP2D6* genotype data.

## Method

### Study Protocol

Data from a prospective clinical study with 1000 patients undergoing ambulatory surgery were analysed. The study was registered to EudraCT (no. 2015-005561-23) and approved by the Coordinating Ethics Committee of the Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District (3/2016) and the Finnish National Agency for Medicines (KL no. 4/2016). The data were collected at the day-surgery unit of Jorvi Hospital, University of Helsinki, and Helsinki University Hospital, Espoo, Finland from August 2016 to March 2018. When the patients were recruited to the study, medication used at home before surgery was recorded. Use of strong inhibitors of CYP2D6 was an exclusion criterion. All study patients received oral premedication with paracetamol (1000 mg) and codeine (60 mg) for preoperative prophylactic analgesia. After pre-medication, two blood samples were obtained through a cannula placed in the forearm vein. The first blood sample was drawn after 20–60 min and the second sample was drawn after 180–360 min after receiving the paracetamol-codeine combination tablets. The time of blood sampling was adapted to the progress of the treatment process, and the blood samples were commonly taken during the induction of anaesthesia and at the time of discharge from hospital.

The adverse effects of pain medication, such as nausea and vomiting (yes or no), dizziness, sleepiness and constipation (score from 0 to 3) were asked repeatedly in the recovery room after surgery and after discharge from hospital twice daily until 2 days after the surgery. Use of antiemetics in the recovery room was also recorded. Detailed information about the study protocol and conduct has been provided in Online Resource 1.

### Drug Analysis

Plasma concentrations of codeine, morphine and the glucuronide conjugates of codeine and morphine were determined with high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Morphine, codeine, morphine 6-glucuronide, codeine-6-glucuronide and stable labelled morphine-D6 and codeine-D6 were purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Canada). Prior to analysis, the plasma samples were pre-treated using a Strata X-C solid phase extraction in a 96-well format (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). Briefly, the samples (0.1 mL) were diluted with 0.15 mL of 4% phosphoric acid containing the internal standards and loaded into the pre-conditioned extraction wells. The wells were then washed with 0.15 mL of 2% formic acid, followed by 0.15 mL of methanol, and the analytes were eluted using 0.15 mL of 5% ammonium hydroxide in methanol. Finally, the sample extracts were dried using a centrifugal evaporator (GeneVac, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and reconstituted in 0.1 mL of 5% methanol.

All measurements were carried out using a Sciex 6500 Qtrap LC-MS system interfaced with an electrospray ion source (Sciex, Toronto, Ontario, Canada). The analytes were separated on a Kinetex biphenyl column (2.6 µm particle size, 2.1 × 100 mm internal diameter; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) under gradient conditions and a 0.28 mL/min flow rate. The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of 0.1% acetic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile-methanol (20:80 v/v) (solvent B), and the solvent gradient was set as follows: a linear ramp from 5 to 30% B over 3.5 min, a second linear ramp from 70 to 90% B over 1.5 min and 3 min equilibration back to the initial eluent composition. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive polarity mode, and the mass-to-charge (m/z) transitions used for quantification were 286–152 for morphine, 462–286 for morphine 6-glucuronide, 300–152 for codeine and 476–300 for codeine-6-glucuronide. The limit of quantification was 0.05 ng/mL, except for morphine-6-glucuronide (0.1 ng/mL). The day-to-day coefficients of variation (CV) values were below 11% at relevant concentrations for all analytes.

### Genotyping

Genotyping was carried out using TaqMan^R^ genotyping and copy number assays on the QuantStudio™ 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System or targeted next-generation sequencing using the Ion GeneStudio™ S5 Prime system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). All samples were genotyped for the clinically relevant *CYP2D6* sequence variations, defining the **1-, *2-, *3-, *4-, *5-, *6-, *9-, *10-, *15-, *17-, *35-, *39-* and **41*-alleles, as well as for *CYP2D6* copy number variation (CNV). The *CYP2D6-*genotype distribution in the study population is shown in additional data provided in Online Resource 2. CYP2D6 phenotypes were inferred from the genotypes using the activity scores on the basis of recent consensus [[5](#CR5), [6](#CR6), [12](#CR12)]. A detailed description of the genotyping is found in the Online Resource 1.

### Pharmacokinetic Modelling

Non-linear mixed effects modelling was conducted with NONMEM^®^ (version 7.4.3 or above) (ICON Development Solutions, Ellicott city, MD, USA) [[17](#CR17)], assisted by the Perl-Speaks-NONMEM (PsN)-toolkit [[18](#CR18)].

The pharmacokinetic modelling of concentration–time data was conducted in a stepwise manner. First, a pharmacokinetic model was developed and validated for codeine. Next, we included morphine data in the dataset and tested parent-metabolite disposition models. The C6G and M3G data were then added in the analysis data in a sequential manner to improve model performance in accounting for the complex enzymatic interplay defining codeine and morphine elimination (Fig. [1](#Fig1)). Finally, the influence of CYP2D6 activity scores (AS) was added to the model, and several parameterisations were tested. The influence of the remaining observable patient characteristics (covariates) on codeine/morphine metabolism and disposition profiles was tested using the stepwise-covariate-modelling tool in the PsN toolkit [[18](#CR18)]. Detailed description of the modelling process and the differential equations derived from the final model are given in the Online Resource 1.

The total clearance of codeine (CLcod) is divided into CYP2D6- (ke × fmor) and non-CYP2D6-mediated [ke × (1 – fmor)] clearance. Models were coded using differential equations and solved by the *ADVAN13* subroutine of the NONMEM^®^ software. The model parameters were modelled in the log-domain using MU-referencing and computed using the first-order conditional estimation with interaction estimation method.

Exponential model was used to characterise between-subject variability in model parameters, and the covariance between the random effect parameters was also tested. The residual variability in the model predictions was specified using an additive error model. Both were assumed to be normally distributed random variables with a mean of zero and a SD of one.

Improvement in model fit was measured on the basis of significant differences in objective function value (OFV) and Akaike information criteria (AIC), the numerical stability of the model parameters, standard goodness-of-fit plots and visual predictive checks [[19](#CR19)].

### Covariate Model

The pharmacokinetic parameters defining clearance and the volume of the central compartment for both codeine and morphine were allometrically scaled to the population’s median weight. The covariate effects of CYP2D6 activity on the ratio of codeine metabolised to morphine were tested sequentially using CYP2D6 phenotypes predicted from genotypes or activity scores to explain between-subject variability in codeine metabolism. Both categorical and continuous modelling approaches were tested, as described in Online Resource 1.

The stepwise covariate modelling protocol included in the PsN toolkit was used to test the influence of the additional patient covariates (age, cigarette smoking, and American Society of Anesthesiologists classification) on the fixed effects. For continuous covariates, power and exponential models were tested, as was a linear model specification for categorical covariates. Both forward and backward searches were performed, with search probabilities of 0.05 and 0.01, respectively.

### Model Simulations

Model uncertainty was evaluated with the sampling importance resampling protocol [[20](#CR20)] to determine the robustness of the final pharmacokinetic models. The protocol was run with 1000 samples and 500 resamples (M/m = 2). Secondly, the final pharmacokinetic model was used to simulate data for 1000 virtual study subjects who had designated activity scores and were receiving a clinically plausible range of codeine doses (from 30 mg o.d. for 1 day up to 60 mg q.i.d. for 4 days), using a typical population median body weight of 80 kg. Additionally, a new patient collective of 10,000 new patients was simulated using the covariate set of study subjects at codeine dose levels of 30 mg or 60 mg (t.i.d. or q.i.d.) to evaluate the effect of CYP2D6 phenotype on codeine/morphine disposition kinetics. Each phenotype class was simulated against varying codeine dosages. The non-compartmental analysis protocol in the PsN toolkit and *ncappc* R package was used to compute non-compartmental pharmacokinetic analysis.

Concentration data for one or more analytes were missing from three patients, leaving 997 patients in the final dataset (Online Resource 1, Supplementary Fig. 1).

### Pharmacokinetic Models

A one-compartmental pharmacokinetic model with a depot compartment and first-order absorption led to an adequate model fit (OFV = 211), with biologically plausible parameter estimates as compared with a two-compartmental model (∆OFV = +2). Therefore, a one-compartmental model was chosen as the final structural model for codeine disposition. The addition of between-subject variability on the model parameters (*k*_a_, *V*_cent_ and F) resulted in a significant OFV drop, with low shrinkage (< 30%) on the between-subject variability parameters. Finally, weight scaling was added on the clearance and volume parameters (∆OFV = −55).

Next, a one-compartmental model for morphine was added to the model. Initially, the total systemic codeine first-order elimination was entirely directed as input into the metabolite model (OFV = 1224, AIC =1246). Serious numerical difficulties in estimating a biologically plausible value for morphine volume were noticed in this step of the modelling study. The addition of a fraction parameter (*f*_mor_), denoting the proportion of codeine first-order elimination that gets converted to morphine, in conjunction with first-order codeine clearance, significantly improved model performance (∆OFV = −113, AIC = 733) and resulted in a biologically plausible model estimate (*f*_mor_ = 8%).

Finally, one-compartmental models were fitted for C6G and M3G, and a composite model was run with four analytes. The fraction of C6G from codeine elimination was numerically assigned as 1 – *f*_mor_, while the metabolic fraction of M3G from morphine elimination was fixed at 60%, according to a recent report [[21](#CR21)]. This resulted in a precise model fit (∆OFV = −1809, AIC = −1064) and biologically plausible parameter estimates.

### Covariate modelling

The effect of CYP2D6 phenotype on codeine metabolism was modelled with activity scores, which were treated as a continuous covariate:

| fmor=θA×expθB×ASref-ASi, |
| --- |
where *f*_mor_ is the fraction of codeine metabolised to morphine. The individual value of an activity score (AS_i_) is scaled to the reference value (AS_ref_) of 2, and *θ*_A_ is the reference value of *f*_mor_, while *θ*_B_ is the scaling factor for translating the effect of activity scores on *f*_mor_.

*CYP2D6*-based genetic effect was modelled either through categorical effect of metaboliser status (i.e. PM, IM, NM or UM), with a separately estimated model parameter corresponding to value *f*_mor_ for each of these categories (ΔOFV = −711), or by treating AS as a continuous covariate and implementing a linear (ΔOFV = −2725), exponential (ΔOFV = −2970) or power (ΔOFV = −3556) model. Further details about these implementations have been provided in the ESM. Although power model was preferable in terms of OFV drop, repeated attempts to counteract a marked lack of precision in estimated model parameters (denoted by very high %RSEs) and numerical difficulties in model estimation (lack of convergence and retrieval of variance-covariance matrix) led to the power model being discarded possibly due to the addition of a separate model parameter for AS = 0, and exponential model was retained as the final model.

Prior to the addition of a CYP2D6 effect, the morphine/codeine ratio was estimated at 8%, with 52% unexplained variability. After the implementation of a covariate model, the unexplained variability was reduced to 45% and 33% after using the phenotype classes or activity scores as the covariate, respectively.

In addition to *CYP2D6* genotype, patient weight, age, height, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical activity score, smoking status and number of smoked cigarettes (if a smoker) were tested. There was no further improvement in the predictive performance of the final model. The final pharmacokinetic model (Fig. [1](#Fig1)) provided an adequate fit to the study data (Fig. [2](#Fig2); Online Resource 1, Supplementary Figs. [S2](#MOESM2)–S5), with biologically plausible parameter estimates for the pharmacokinetic and covariate model parameters and possessed numerical and predictive stability (Table [2](#Tab2)).

### Quantification of the Effect of Different Genotypes

The *CYP2D6* genotype data were available for all 997 patients included in the population pharmacokinetic analysis. A total of 64 *CYP2D6* genotypes were observed (see Online Resource 2).

The apparent total clearance of codeine is divided into CYP2D6- (*k*_e_ × *f*_mor_) and non-CYP2D6-mediated [*k* × (1 – *f*_mor_)] metabolisms. CYP2D6-mediated metabolism reflects the formation of morphine, and Fig. [3](#Fig3)A shows the apparent CYP2D6 activity for patients on the basis of their *CYP2D6* genotypes. The median (interquartile range, %) apparent CYP2D6 activity was 0.55 (0.34–0.75) for CYP2D6 PMs, 6.82 (5.39–8.67) for IMs, 13.8 (10.9–16.7) for NMs and 19.9 (16.8–23.1) for UMs of the total codeine clearance. Patients who were heterozygous for the *CYP2D6*10* allele seemed to have higher apparent CYP2D6 activity than patients heterozygous for the *CYP2D6*41* allele.

## Discussion

Recently, several studies have evaluated the functional assignment of *CYP2D6* alleles with different substrates in clinical settings [[13](#CR13)–[16](#CR16)]. These analyses have provided support for the functional assignment of *CYP2D6* alleles, but these retrospective analyses have pooled data from separate studies or therapeutic drug-monitoring data. We conducted a prospective study in ambulatory surgical patients receiving a fixed dose of codeine preoperatively. A large dataset composed of 997 patients’ PK data, with a diverse selection of relevant *CYP2D6* alleles, was analysed, and our primary aim was to quantify the in vivo effect of *CYP2D6* genotype on the metabolism of codeine.

Our results show a 58–83% reduction in apparent CYP2D6 activity via decreased function activity scores as compared with the normal metaboliser category activity scores (i.e. NM in panel c versus IM in panel b in Fig. [3](#Fig3)B). Furthermore, patients with increased function that is, activity scores of 3 and 4 versus activity scores of 1.5 and 2, respectively, had approximately 2.1- and 1.69-fold higher apparent CYP2D6 activity as compared with patients with normal function, respectively. The guidelines for translating the genotype into phenotype used to individualise drug dosing are ambiguous. A recent study with a large amount of retrospective therapeutic drug-monitoring data demonstrated that, compared with normal *CYP2D6* alleles, the activity scores of the *CYP2D6*41* and *CYP2D6*9-10* alleles were estimated to be one-sixth and one-third as large, respectively [[16](#CR16)]. The results of this highly powered study provide a solid basis for the translation of the *CYP2D6* genotype into a drug metabolic phenotype. In accordance with these results, our study demonstrates that the *CYP2D6*10* decreased function allele shows higher apparent CYP2D6 activity than the *CYP2D6*41* allele. Thus, our results further confirm the previous findings [[16](#CR16), [22](#CR22), [23](#CR23)] comparing these alleles and challenge the consensus of assigning a lower activity score to *CYP2D6*10* than other decreased-function *CYP2D6* alleles.

A one-compartment empirical pharmacokinetic model for both codeine and its main metabolites could capture the data well. Linear first-order elimination could capture the codeine elimination well, and the model-estimated apparent clearance was in accordance with previous reports [[24](#CR24)]. Our results show that codeine is quickly and nearly completely absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, which is in good agreement with the previous literature [[25](#CR25)] The amount of codeine eliminated to morphine was estimated using a ratio parameter, and we assumed that the remainder was glucuronidated to C6G. In conjunction, M3G was assumed to account for 60% of morphine metabolism on the basis of the previous literature [[24](#CR24)], and the model fit demonstrated an adequate fit. The addition of M3G data into the model improved the performance of the morphine model in providing a plausible estimate for the apparent volume of distribution, which previously imparted numerical inconsistency to the parent/metabolite model.

Prior to the addition of a *CYP2D6*-effect, the morphine/codeine ratio was estimated at 8%, with 52% unexplained variability. After the implementation of a covariate model using CYP2D6 phenotype classes, the unexplained variability could be reduced to 45%, and after assigning CYP2D6 AS as the covariate, the unexplained variability was reduced further to 33%. This shows that there remains considerable unexplained variability in the metabolic ratio parameter. A recent study showed that, while CYP2D6 AS explained 23% of the interindividual variability in CYP2D6 activity, there were considerable inconsistencies between different activity scores [[26](#CR26)]. CYP2D6 protein level seems to be the major determinant of CYP2D6 activity according to this study [[26](#CR26)]. Previous studies have demonstrated the importance of drug interactions [[8](#CR8)], and underlying diseases and other physiological factors, together with the effect of the microbiome and inflammation, may further contribute to the expression and activity of CYP2D6 [[27](#CR27)–[29](#CR29)].

Morphine represents only a fraction of the total codeine metabolism in vivo, and previous results have shown that it accounts, on average, for only 5–10% of total codeine elimination clearance [[30](#CR30)]. The *CYP2D6* genotype causes significant variability in this regard, and our results show that the median ratio metabolised to morphine varied from 0.5 to more than 30% in AS levels 0 and 4, respectively, demonstrating a progressive change dependent on the *CYP2D6* genotype. This translates into an almost 20-fold range between the AS groups during an average morphine exposure, as demonstrated by the simulated AUCs of morphine after 60 mg of codeine, averaging 1 µg·h/L in AS 0 and 19 µg·h/L in AS 4. These results are in accordance with previous clinical findings [[5](#CR5)–[8](#CR8)].

Coetzee [[30](#CR30)] has devised a theoretical therapeutic window for morphine between 9 and 14 ng/mL in terms of its analgesic activity, in conjunction with a previously reported 9.1 ng/mL EC50 value for morphine in terms of producing analgesia and respiratory depression in 50% of patients [[31](#CR31)]. The simulations derived from our final model show that the repeated administration of codeine can result in systemic concentrations either reaching EC50 for respiratory depression or exceeding it for AS 3 and 4, respectively. Additionally, our results show considerable variability in systemic codeine concentrations between individuals with the same *CYP2D6* genotype, indicating that some patients with AS < 3 may also exhibit dangerously high exposure to morphine after codeine administration.

It is worth noting that although there are significant activity differences between the allelic forms for *CYP2D6*, the median population level exposure to morphine (as denoted by morphine AUC/F in Table [3](#Tab3)) follows the trend of increasing AS corresponding to higher morphine exposure in vivo. This supports the derivation of an AS-based system based on allelic genetic makeup, which as our modelling results suggest, is a more favourable way of individualising therapy with *CYP2D6* substrates as compared with conventional metabolic categories (PM, IM, NM and UM), but as our results also indicate, may require a careful re-evaluation of the assignment of activity scores to specific allelic components, and specifically the *CYP2D6*10* allele as per our investigation.

Although the activity scoring system for *CYP2D6* activity significantly improves the prediction of downstream metabolite (in this case morphine) exposure in comparison with metabolic categories of PM, IM, NM and UM by increasing complexity in *CYP2D6* activity-based classification, there might be subtle differences in the real-world activity of different functional alleles that may not be reflected in the current system of classification. It is clear from our analysis that while **10* allele imparts a higher functional *CYP2D6* activity as compared with the **41* allele (Fig. [3](#Fig3)A), this effect gets masked with the pooling together of functional alleles under a specific cumulative activity score. For example, it is difficult to directly compare morphine exposure between the group of AS = 1.25 (constituted by allelic combinations of **1/*10*, **2/*10* and **10/*35*) against AS = 1.5 since the latter is composed of allelic combinations other than merely **1/*41*, **2/*41* and **35/*41* (Table [3](#Tab3)). The other allelic combinations may lead to higher morphine exposure, and hence the subtle differences between **10* and **41* alleles in morphine exposure are masked when comparing only the median exposure per AS category. Therefore, it is important to keep in view relative differences between functional alleles when designing dosing schemes, especially for potent substances such as morphine for which the therapeutic window is quite narrow.

In this ambulatory surgery study, CYP2D6 phenotype was associated with only one adverse effect of codeine, constipation. The lack of other statistically significant differences in adverse effects of codeine between the CYP2D6 phenotype could be explained by short-term use of codeine; after the surgery it was used only if needed. Most of the patients did not have strong or long-lasting pain.

Despite a prospective study design and 1000 patients being recruited, only two blood samples were drawn from each patient for drug analysis. However, our analysis included all major metabolites together with the parent drug, and the model estimates show adequate precision and clinical plausibility. Furthermore, we determined a selection of 11 relevant *CYP2D6* alleles, together with copy number variation, to include 66 genotypes and thus quantify their effect. Although several other covariates were collected, we could not use them in our modelling, due to over-parameterisation issues. Thus, the effect of other covariates on codeine pharmacokinetics remains to be studied. We made several assumptions during the model development for the sake of simplicity, such as the fraction metabolised to M6G, but these were based on the previous literature. Although our findings on the utility of AS are generally in accordance with recent findings [[13](#CR13)–[16](#CR16)], the substrate-specific nature of CYP2D6 should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results.

## Conclusions

Our results show that CYP2D6 metaboliser phenotype categories include patients with markedly different CYP2D6 activities, thereby causing inaccuracies in phenotype-based predictions. Using CYP2D6 activity scores as an ordinal continuous covariate instead of translating genetic makeup into metaboliser categories, we could harness the changes in metabolic characteristics and better account for the interindividual variability. This approach shows the utility of *CYP2D6*-based AS covariates in model-informed codeine dosing in comparison with phenotype categories.

1. Methods 1.1. Subjects and Clinical Trial Outline The study protocol was approved by the Coordinating Ethics Committee of the Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District (3/2016) and by the Finnish Medicines Agency Fimea (EudraCT number: 2015-005561-23). The registration was made in the EU Clinical Trials Register prior to patients enrolment. Written consent was obtained from the patients after providing study information. The data was collected at the Jorvi day surgery unit, Espoo, during 8/2016-3/2018. The objective was to include 1,000 patients undergoing ambulatory surgery for the study. Operation types or anesthesia methods did not limit the study protocol. Study patients were randomly selected from surgical patients on a daily basis. All study patients received oral premedication with paracetamol (1000mg) and codeine (60 mg) for preoperative prophylactic analgesia. The patients were eligible for the study if they were aged between 18-70 years, ASA physical status 1-3, weight over 50 kg, BMI under 40 kg/m2, were not pregnant or breast feeding, and did not have any allergies for paracetamol- codeine combination therapy. If there were any contraindications for paracetamol or codeine, like obstructive sleep apnea, history of alcohol or drugs abuse, or unwanted interaction of medications, problems with liver or kidney func- tion; these patients were excluded from the study. Proficiency in Finnish or Swedish was deemed mandatory, while covariate data such as age, weight, ASA score, smoking status, background diseases and regular home medication were also collected in the study. 1.2. Blood Sampling After pre-medication, two blood samples were obtained through a cannula placed in the forearm vein. The first blood sample was drawn after 20-60 minutes and the second sample after 180-360 minutes after receiving the paracetamol-codeine combination tablets. The time of blood sampling could be adapted to the progress of the treat- ment process, and the blood samples were commonly taken during the induction of anesthesia and at the time of discharge from the hospital. Pharmacokinetic analysis of the concentration-time data was conducted using population pharmacokinetics. The blood samples were also subjected to genome-wide analysis and CYP2D6 genetic testing, which could translate to activity scores (AS) based on previously published literature [1]. Blood samples were obtained for codeine concentration-time data, alongside data for the major cytochrome P450 (CYP) mediated metabolites. The samples needed to determine the drug concentrations were taken in 4 ml K2-EDTA tubes, which were placed in the crushed ice immediately after sampling. Plasma was separated from K2-EDTA samples by centrifugation and divided into two freezer tubes, which were frozen as soon as possible. 1.3. CYP2D6 genotyping All samples were genotyped for clinically relevant CYP2D6 sequence variations defining the *1-, *2-, *3-, *4-, *5-, *6-, *9-, *10-, *15-, *17-, *35-, *39-, and *41-alleles, as well as for CYP2D6 copy number variation (CNV). Prior to analyses, DNA samples were diluted with 1xTE-buffer to either 50 ng/&amp;micro;l (variant genotyping) or 5 ng/&amp;micro;l (CNV experiments). Genotyping was carried out using TaqMan &reg; genotyping assays on OpenArray plates and the 3

QuantStudio 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). CYP2D6 CNV was quantified with the TaqMan &reg; Copy Number Assay Hs00010001 cn targeting exon 9 and RNAseP (Ribonuclease P RNA component H1 gene, RPPH1) as the reference assay on the QuantStudio 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System. Genotyping results were interpreted using TaqMan &reg; Genotyper Software v.1.3 (OpenArray results) and CopyCaller&reg; Software v.2.1 (CNV results). In case of missing or ambiguous genotypes or copy number calls, genotyping and copy number quantification were performed with next generation sequencing using Ion GeneStudio S5 Prime system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Barcoded sequencing libraries were prepared from 10 ng of DNA per sample using Ion Ampliseq Library Kit 2.0, Ion Xpress barcode adapters, and Ion Ampliseq Pharmacogenomics Research Panel (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following manufacturer&acute;s protocols. Libraries were purified with Agencourt AMPure XP reagent (Beckman Coultier, Brea, CA) and quantified with Ion Library TaqMan Quantitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Template preparation (emulsion PCR, bead en- richment, and chip loading) were automated with Ion Chef system using Ion 510 and Ion 520 and Ion 530 Chef Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and loaded Ion 510 chips were sequenced with Ion S5 sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All protocols were executed by following manufacturer&acute;s instructions. Sequence alignments to reference genome (hg19), base and CNV calling, coverage analysis, and variant identification were performed uti- lizing Torrent Suite software (v.5.12.1) with coverageAnalysis, PGx analysis, sampleID, and variantCaller plugins (v.5.12.0) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). CYP2D6 haplotypes were inferred computationally using Phase 2.1.1. [2, 3], and the *-alleles defined according to the CPIC allele definition table. 1.4. CYP2D6 activity scores Metabolizer status (phenotype) was inferred from the genotypes using the activity score (AS) (Gaedigk et al. 2008) according to recent consensus (Caudle et al. 2020). An AS value of 1 was assigned for each normal function allele (i.e., *1, *2, *35 and *39), 0.5 for each decreased function allele (i.e., *9, *17, 29, and *41), 0.25 for each *10 allele, and 0 for each inert allele (i.e., *3, *4, *5, and *6, and *40). Duplicated normal function alleles in combination with CYP2D6*10 (*1x2/*10 and *2x2/*10) were assigned ans AS of 2.25. Duplicated normal function alleles in combination with either a decreased function allele other than CYP2D6*10 or another normal function allele were assigned as 3 (*1 or *2 with *1xN, *2xN or *35) or 4 (*1xN with *35xN or *2xN) (Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium 2020). AS of 0 indicates a poor metabolizer (PM), AS 0 &lt; x &lt; 1.25 indicates an intermediate metabolizer (IM), AS 1.25 x 2.25 indicates a normal (NM), and AS of &gt; 2.25 indicates an ultra-rapid metabolizer (UM) (Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium 2020). 1.5. Data Analysis Concentration-time of codeine and morphine and patient covariates data was gathered and assembled with RStu- dio &reg; (version 1.0.153) [4] using R (version 4.0.3) [5]. Preliminary exploration of the datasets for identifying general trends in study population and obtaining data summaries, as well as model evaluation and validation using graphical diagnostics were also conducted using R, implemented in the RStudio integrated development environment (IDE). 4

1.6. Pharmacokinetic Modelling Pharmacokinetic modelling of codeine/morphine concentration-time data was conducted in a step-wise manner such that, in the first instance, a pharmacokinetic model was developed and validated for codeine. Once a model with adequate predictive performance was validated for the parent drug, morphine data was included in the analysis dataset and parent-metabolite disposition models were tested. Then, the influence of CYP2D6 activity score (AS) or an individual s phenotypic form (PF) were added into the parent-metabolite model and tested using several mathematical functions and/or coding strategies. Finally, the influence of remaining observable patient characteristics (covariates) on codeine/morphine metabolism and disposition profiles was tested using the stepwise-covariate-modelling (SCM) [6] tool in the PsN toolkit. These population models were coded using differential equations and were solved by the ADVAN13 subroutine of the NONMEM &reg; software, while the model parameters were computed using the First Order Conditional Estimation with Interaction (FOCE-i) estimation method. MU referencing was used to specify the population values of model parameters in the logarithmic domain, while the exponential values represented individual estimates in conjunction with BSV parameters, i.e., MUn = LOG( POP) (1) i = EXP(MUn + i) (2) 1.6.1. Pharmacokinetic structural models Pharmacokinetic modelling of codeine concentration-time data commenced with an empirical one compartment disposition model with linear first order clearance. Since only oral dosage administration was used in the study and no data for vascular drug administration was available, a depot compartment with linear first order absorption without a lag-time was coded to define codeine absorption. A bioavailability parameter was included in the model to describe systemic procurement of codeine in-vivo via oral absorption. Next, two and three compartmental disposition models alongside a depot compartment were tested for codeine data with the aim of identifying possible improvements in the model fit using elaborate pharmacokinetic models. In conjunction, a Michaelis Menten based elimination model was also fitted to the data at hand considering that cytochrome P450 (CYP) mediated codeine metabolism may be prone to saturation. Nested models were tested by an addition of degrees of freedom in subsequent models, which described either new fixed effect parameters in the model e.g. progressing to a two or three-compartmental disposition model, or random (variability) effect parameters i.e. inter-individual changes in model parameter estimates e.g. clearance, or residual variability in observations. Next, and once an adequate pharmacokinetic (absorption and disposition) model had been established for codeine; morphine data was included in the model alongside a one compartmental model for the active metabolite. The mod- elling strategy for developing a parent/metabolite system was such that, initially, codeine elimination clearance was entirely used as an input into the morphine model. It has been documented in the literature that; a) morphine repre- sents only a fraction of the total codeine metabolism that takes place in vivo, as has been shown to be approximately 5 10% of total codeine eliminatory clearance [1] and b) CYP2D6 genetic makeup has been documented to cause significant changes in area under the curve (AUC) of morphine amongst activity scores (AS) and metabolic categories [7]. 5

Therefore, a ratio parameter was estimated in the model to define the fraction of codeine metabolism as input into the morphine model. The aim behind the inclusion of this ratio parameter, in addition to improving biological plausibility of the model, was to act as a liaison parameter, which would then be used to test the effect of CYP2D6 activity scores (AS) and metabolic category assignment for each study subject (in turn dependent on AS cut-off limits) on codeine to morphine eliminatory pathway. Pharmacokinetic parameters defining clearances and volume of central compartment for both codeine and morphine were allometrically scaled to the population median weight in kilograms (80kg), as defined by the following equation (Equation 1): WTCL = (WT KGi/80)0.75 (3) WTV = (WT KGi/80) (4) POP = n &amp;middot;WTCL (5) POP = n+1 &amp;middot;WTV (6) MUn = LOG( POP &amp;middot;WTCL) (7) where WTCL defines the weight scaling of clearance parameters, WTKGi is the weight of an individual measured in kilograms, WTV defines the weight scaling of volume parameters, POP is the population value of a model parameter, while n and n+1 describe model parameters for clearance or volume and scaled with WTCL or WTV respectively. Finally, the data for C6G and M3G were also added in the analysis data set a combined model with simultaneous estimation of PK parameters for codeine, morphine, C6G and M3G was conducted. It was anticipated that model performance would be significally improved due to the addition of data for these glucuronides and would assist in figuring out precise values for the parameters, while also accounting for population and individual trends in the data. 1.6.2. Model evaluation Significant differences in objective function value (OFV) served as the main criteria for distinguishing between the predictive capability of nested models, i.e. models separated by one or more degrees of freedom with no major structural changes and identical analysis datasets. For each added degree of freedom, an OFV difference ( OFV) of at least 3.84 points at a probability of 0.05 was used as to accept or reject changes amongst nested models. For non-nested models, Akaike information criteria (AIC) was used as a discerning variable to establish improvements in model predictive efficiency due to structural changes in the model, e.g. Michaelis Menten model for codeine elimination versus a linear first order clearance. Numerical stability in the pharmacokinetic models was assessed with successful minimization to global minima for the model parameter estimates, low percent relative standard errors (%RSE) and retrieval of covariance matrices from the final models. For inter-individual and residual variability parameters, percentage shrinkage to the population median was used as an inclusion/exclusion criterion, such that, these parameters were only included in the models if percent shrinkage was less than 50%. Additionally, graphical diagnostics played a pivotal role in establishing predictive capacity of these models, using standard goodness-of-fit (GOF) plots and visual predictive checks (VPC) [9]. Precision in model parameter estimation was validated through the sampling importance resampling (SIR) [10] procedure included in the PsN toolkit. Output from the SIR tool provides information on the 95% confidence intervals for the model parameter estimates as well as graphical diagnostics (e.g. OFV vs distribution quantiles) to decipher robustness of the final pharmacokinetic models. 1.6.5. Stepwise covariate modelling (SCM) The stepwise covariate modelling (SCM) protocol included in the PsN toolkit was used in our modelling study for testing the influence of the available patient covariates on pharmacokinetic parameters (fixed effects). Biological plausibility and previous literature were used as the basis for selecting patient covariates and model parameters on 8

which the covariate effects were tested. Both forward and backward search directions were specified in the SCM log file, alongside a forward search probability of 0.05 and backward search probability of 0.01. Since our study population also consisted elderly individuals (&gt;50 years) and there have been reports about the diminishing effect of increasing age on codeine clearance in humans (Purdue Pharma Std, 2018), the effect of age on codeine clearance was modelled in the SCM run. In addition, the effect of cigarette smoking on total codeine clearance (increased glucuronidation) has been documented in the literature [11] and was therefore also included in the SCM analysis. Additionally, American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) classification was also tested as a covariate on codeine clearance, which signifies deteriorating health condition amongst elderly or due to complicated medical conditions. For continuous covariates (i.e. Age in years), power and exponential models were tested in SCM as well as a search without covariate effects as a control, while for categorical covariates, a linear model specification was included in the SCM log file in addition to no covariate effects. SCM was run in a sequential manner of testing one covariate relation in the instance by setting parallel states argument in the log file to 0. Final models from the SCM protocol runs were compared for OFV changes to establish potential ameliorations in model predictive performance. 1.7. Model Simulations NONMEM &reg;, assisted by the PsN toolkit, allow for dynamic simulations using pharmacometric models, which can then inform critical decisions in clinical drug therapy as well as commercial drug development exercises. The pharmacokinetic and covariate models for codeine/morphine were used to simulate data for 1000 virtual study subjects with designated activity scores in accordance with the data at hand (i.e. AS 0 4) and receiving a range of codeine doses (i.e. from 60 mg O.D. for one day and up to 60mg Q.I.D for four days) as clinically implemented in pain therapy, and using the population median body weight (80kg). Additionally, the models were used to simulate data for new patient collectives using the covariate set of study subjects, at codeine dose levels of 30 mg or 60 mg (T.I.D or Q.I.D), in order to obtain model predicted concentration time behavior for a genetically (CYP2D6) heterogenous population. The primary aim behind these simulations was to extract information about different AS/PF behaviors in response to varying codeine dosage administration schemes, and the resultant perturbations in morphine pharmacokinetic pro- files (i.e. maximum plasma concentrations and area under the curve values). Exposure of morphine can drastically change amongst subjects based on their AS or PF and therefore, these simulations were intended to provide pivotal information on how morphine exposure differs, as well as the relevant adjustments required for codeine administration schemes. Therefore, each AS (0 4) of codeine and PF (PM, IM, EM and UM) were tested against varying codeine dosages to get data about model predictive capability in accounting for specific differences in codeine/morphine dis- position kinetics. Additionally, the non-compartmental analysis (NCA) protocol in the PsN toolkit, assisted by the ncappc R pack- age, was used to compute NCA based pharmacokinetic analysis of the analysis dataset (observations) versus model predictions. These allowed for the determination of the AUC s of codeine and morphine in relation to CYP2D6 AS or PF. 9

2. Results 2.1. Codeine/Morphine pharmacokinetics A pharmacometric model with a depot compartment alongside first order absorption, and one compartmental dis- position was tested and led to a reasonable model fit (OFV = 211), although the value of first order absorption rate constant achieved a biologically plausible parameter estimate only after an association with an IIV parameter. In the next step, the complexity in model was elevated by two degrees of freedom and a two compartmental model was fitted to the codeine pharmacokinetic profiles. It was observed that our data did not support a two-compartment disposition model as demonstrated by an insignificant drop in OFV ( OFV = +2), while the high percent relative standard error in the added model parameters clearly established a lack of precision in the estimation of these model parameters. Therefore, a one compartmental model was maintained as the final pharmacokinetic model for codeine. IIV was es- timated as exponential (log-normal distribution) models on ka, Vcent and F (bioavailability) parameters. Predictions from the final pharmacokinetic model coincided well with the data and model parameters were estimated with high precision, which are indicative of an adequate model fit. Then, morphine concentration time profiles from the study subjects were added to the analysis data, and a si- multaneous parent-metabolite pharmacokinetic model was tested. A one compartmental pharmacokinetic model was fitted to the metabolite data. Initially, and to develop the system in a nested approach, the total systemic clearance was directed as input into the metabolite model which lacks biological plausibility, since morphine has been docu- mented to represent only a fraction of codeine clearance in vivo [12]. As anticipated, although this parent-metabolite pharmacokinetic model resulted in a successful minimization to the global minima for the model parameters (OFV = 1224, AIC =1246), the estimates lacked precision, and it was deduced that the model needed further refinement. The addition of IIV parameters (as exponential models) did not significantly improve the model and high shrink- age (&gt;70%) was noticed for both the IIV on morphine clearance as well as central volume, and these models were therefore discarded. The addition of a codeine to morphine metabolic ratio parameter significantly improved model performance ( OFV = -113, AIC = 733), while the added parameter was also estimated with a biologically plausible value (RM = 8%). IIV parameter on the metabolic ratio of codeine to morphine took a value of 0.61 (52%) in initial modelling attempts, signifying large between subject variability (BSV) that, as we hypothesized, was in part traceable to the link between CYP2D6 intrinsic activity and the genetic profile of the individual for this metabolic CYP enzyme. The IIV variable was estimated with low shrinkage (9.25%), which was indicative that the model structure was able to account for the data at hand. In the next step, the analysis dataset was enriched with data of codeine-6-glucuronide (C6G) and morphine-3- glucuronide (M3G) metabolites and a composite model was run with four analytes. The fraction of C6G from codeine elimination was numerically assigned to be 1 RM, while the metabolic fraction of M3G from morphine elimination was fixed at 60% according to a recent report (REF; Gammal et al. 2016). One compartmental models were fitted to each added metabolite (C6G/M3G) and these produced good model fit ( OFV = -1809, AIC = -1064) biologically plausible parameter estimates, which are according to previous reports (REF). The addition of these metabolites data in the model aided the estimation of a plausible value for morphine central volume, in turn by providing more information on the complex metabolism of codeine in vivo. 10

2.2. CYP2D6 AS/PF based covariate model The inclusion of CYP2D6 based covariate effects in the codeine/morphine pharmacokinetic model was tested using different modelling strategies. In the first instance, covariate model was fitted using phenotypic forms of the study subjects as a categorical co- variate. Although significant improvements in predictive performance were evident using this modelling strategy, the model encountered numerical difficulties in minimization, and graphical diagnostics demonstrated an underprediction of morphine levels for intermediate and extensive codeine PF categories. The parameter estimates for codeine to mor- phine metabolic ratio for different PF categories took biologically plausible values (i.e. 0.5% for PM, 4% for IM, 7% for NM, and 23% for UM) but, because the later covariate models provided better fit using AS ratings, it pointed to the fact that additional information was still required in the model to account for CYP2D6 based codeine metabolism than PF variables could provide. The addition of IIV parameters on PF baselines, in addition to the final composite ratio parameter, did not result in significant OFV changes, and were therefore not included in the final model from this modelling strategy. Thereafter, it was deduced that effect of CYP2D6 based genetic profile was more dynamic than PF categories allow, based on subject specific intrinsic CYP2D6 activity and therefore, it was hypothesized that an implementation of AS (ordinal continuous covariate) based effect in the pharmacokinetic model would better harness BSV in codeine to morphine metabolic ratio and improve model predictive performance. In the next step, the activity scores (AS) were used as a means of specifying the effect of CYP2D6 genetic profile on codeine to morphine metabolism, using linear, exponential or power model (Equation 10-12). These models allowed for a dynamic system, such that, each AS could potentially take on a unique value and avoided aggregating study individuals with distinct AS values in ordered categories. Hence, the effect of AS on codeine to morphine metabolism could be visualized in a gene dose vs response manner. Numerical stability, precise parameter estimates and parameter correlations supported the exponential model ( OFV = -909, AIC = 18204) over a power model, and therefore an exponential system of encoding the CYP2D6 based co- variate effects on the ratio parameter was selected as the final covariate model. The final pharmacokinetic model was able to adequately fit to the study data, provided biologically plausible parameter estimates for the pharmacokinetic and covariate model parameters, and possessed numerical and predictive stability. 2.3. Numerical diagnostics with SIR In order establish precision in the estimation of the model parameters via 95% confidence intervals, the sampling importance resampling (SIR) procedure, implemented in the PsN toolkit, was used in our modelling study. The SIR protocol was run with 2000 samples and 1000 resamples (M/m = 2). The covariance matrix from the final codeine/morphine model run was used as the proposal distribution, while the output from the SIR run was used to assess model precision through a narrowness of confidence intervals around parameter estimates and graphical output (i.e. OFV distribution plots, temporal trends in parameter estimates). The SIR procedure resulted in narrow confidence intervals for all model parameters, demonstrating precision in estimating these from the analysis dataset. Graphical output from the SIR run also supported model precision infer- 11

ence, with the OFV distribution plot showing a the proposal density consistently in line with the SIR distribution, and the parameter temporal trends plots showing no significant deviation from the SIR proposal distribution in resamples, thereby establishing an adequacy of the proposal density. 2.4. Stepwise covariate modelling (SCM) In addition to variables signifying CYP2D6 genetic profiles, other patient covariates included in the dataset were patient weight (kg), age (years), height (cm), American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) score, smoking status (yes/no), number of cigarettes (if smoking = yes), and pain ratings (PAINVRSBS). In order to test any possible effects of some of these covariates in the codeine/morphine pharmacokinetic model, the SCM protocol of PsN toolkit [6] was used. In the implementation of this protocol, however, no further improvement was noticed in the predictive performance of the final model while modelling the effect of age, ASA and smoking status on codeine clearance, ASA on codeine central volume, and smoking status on morphine clearance. These covariate relationships were, hence, unsupported by the data at hand which, incidentally, may be due to high degree of sparsity in the dataset rather than a lack of biological plausibility of these relationships in the real world. 2.5. Model Simulations Simulations for a typical patient, i.e. having a population median body weight (84 kg) and an activity score in the range as noticed in the study subjects (AS 0 4), could be used to visualize morphine plasma concentrations in relation to codeine dosage regimen, as well as an individual s CYP2D6 activity score. A theoretical analgesic therapeutic window between 9 and 14 ng/mL was selected based on previous literature [13], in conjunction with a 9.1 ng/mL EC50 value of morphine for causing a 50% analgesic and respiratory depressant action [14]. From the simulations conducted for typical patient groups, it could be observed that repeated dosing leads to steady state plasma morphine concentrations for all AS groups (60 mg Q.I.D administration, supplementary figure 4) within 24 hours, while morphine concentrations approach the corresponding EC50 value for analgesia and respiratory depression for AS 3. For AS 4, and from 24 hours and beyond, the plasma morphine concentrations are noticeably higher than the EC50 value of 9.1 ng/mL and may therefore lead to respiratory depression beyond 40%, which is clinically dangerous. For AS 0, low morphine concentrations develop systemically after codeine administration, thereby suggesting a minimal analgesic effect which may challenge clinical utility of codeine therapy. In the simulations with patient collectives based on the observed data, genetically heterogenous datasets could be simulated with also included a range of covariate characteristics; thereby useful for visualizing the generalizability of our population model. Population data trends for specific AS scores were calculated by extracting data for each AS level and thereafter mean values of plasma morphine concentrations and confidence intervals were plotted. The results demonstrate a progressive rise in morphine concentrations in relation to dose administered (i.e. from 30 mg T.I.D to 60 mg Q.I.D) for AS 0 2. However, the morphine concentrations are noticeably low (0 1 ng/mL) for AS 0, while approaching the EC50 for respiratory depression for AS 4 with Q.I.D dose administration schemes. The confidence intervals support model performance in simulating rich data for a patient collective by accounting for unexplained 12
