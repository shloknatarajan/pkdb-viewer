# Clinical actionability of the NUDT15 *4 (p.R139H) allele and its association with Hispanic ethnicity


## Abstract

Nudix hydrolase 15 (NUDT15) deficiency is strongly associated with thiopurine-induced myelosuppression. Currently, testing for NUDT15 deficiency is based on the genotyping of the most frequent and clinically characterized no-function variants, i.e., *2, *3 and *9. The Hispanic/Latino-predominant variant NUDT15 *4 (p.R139H) is classified as “uncertain function” by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium, because of insufficient data to ascertain its clinical actionability. In this study, we evaluated the association of NUDT15 *4 with mercaptopurine (6-MP) tolerance in a retrospective cohort of 1399 patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) of diverse ancestries. All patients were wildtype for thiopurine methyltransferase gene. Patients were treated with 6-MP in the context of ALL frontline clinical trials. The tolerated dose of 6-MP was used to assess drug toxicity during the maintenance phase of ALL therapy. Patients with NUDT15 *1/*4 (n = 16, all of whom self-identified as Hispanic/Latino) tolerated a significantly lower dose of 6-MP than did those with NUDT15 *1/*1: median [interquartile range] of 39.0 [21.2-52.8] mg/m2, versus 62.2 [47.9-71.6] mg/m2, P value < 0.001. No patient homozygous for *4 was detected. In a separate retrospective validation study, six patients were identified as having NUDT15 *1/*4 by routine clinical pharmacogenetics testing and tolerated a 6-MP median dose of 38.7 mg/m2 (IQR, 33.7-54.0), confirming the need for dose reduction attributed to the NUDT15 *4 variant. These results point to the need to include NUDT15 *4 in pharmacogenetics-guided thiopurine dosing algorithms.


## Introduction

Mercaptopurine (6-MP) is widely used for the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and prolonged daily exposure is critical for long-term remission1,2. However, severe myelosuppression is common in patients receiving this drug and requires cautious dose adjustments based on monitoring of the hematopoietic function3–7. Nudix hydrolase 15 (NUDT15) and thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) are two enzymes involved in the metabolism of 6-MP8–11. While TPMT directly methylates 6-MP and its metabolite thioinosine monophosphate (TIMP), NUDT15 dephosphorylates thioguanine triphosphate (TGTP), the active metabolite of 6-MP, into inactive thioguanine monophosphate (TGMP), thus preventing the accumulation of toxic metabolites. No-function germline variants in both genes have been shown to affect the clinical response (especially toxicity)9,12–16, leading to the recommendations of TPMT/NUDT15 genotype-guided thiopurine dosing by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC)17.

Prominent racial and ethnic differences in the frequency of no-function variants have been described for NUDT15. The *3 allele (p.R139C, c.415C>T) and *2 haplotype (consisting of p.R139C and p.V18_V19insGV) are mostly found in the Asian and Hispanic/Latino populations; while NUDT15 *9 (p.del17_18GV) is more frequent in Europeans18–22. The “uncertain function” allele NUDT15 *4 (p.R139H, c.416G>A)17,22, predominant in Hispanic/Latino individuals, has been shown to have deleterious effects on NUDT15 catalytic activity in vitro9,23. However, this allele is not considered an actionable allele in the CPIC guideline because data ascertaining its clinical significance have been limited.

In this study, we sought to evaluate the tolerance to thiopurine in a retrospective cohort of patients from multiple ancestries and who carry the NUDT15 *4 allele. In addition, a retrospective real-world clinical cohort was constituted to confirm the reduced 6-MP tolerance in individuals with the NUDT15 *4 allele. This study underscores the importance of including the *4 allele in NUDT15 deficiency testing and incorporating this variant into the pharmacogenetics-guided thiopurine dosing algorithms.


## Materials and Methods


### Patients and study approval

A cohort of 1399 patients with newly diagnosed pediatric ALL was retrospectively constituted from frontline ALL trials conducted in North America and Guatemala: (1) St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital Total XVI clinical trial (n = 433)24; (2) the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) AALL03N1 protocol (n = 605)25,26; (3) the COG AALL093227, AALL113128, and AALL123129 protocols conducted at Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA) (n = 152); and (4) the Guatemala LLAG-070730 study conducted at Unidad Nacional de Oncología Pediátrica (UNOP) (n = 209). Patients were included based on clinical and genomic data availability. All patients were genotyped for TPMT and NUDT15, and only patients wildtype for TPMT (*1/*1) were selected for this study. Patient demographics were collected at study enrollment by each participating institution. Patients and/or parents’ race and/or ethnicity were self-reported as Asian, Black, Hispanic/Latino, White, or “Other” if they had multiple races and/or ethnicities. Written informed consent from the parents or guardians and assent from the patients were obtained for enrollment and banking of specimens by the respective institutional review boards (IRBs). This study was approved by St. Jude’s IRB.


### ALL maintenance therapy and evaluation of 6-MP tolerance

Participants’ 6-MP tolerance was evaluated during maintenance therapy, which included a daily oral administration of 6-MP. The protocols’ starting dose of 6-MP was 50 to 75 mg/m2, and dose adjustments allowed depending on the extent of myelosuppression or the occurrence of infections, following standard care practices or protocol requirements (Table S1). Drug exposure was considered stable after at least one complete cycle of maintenance chemotherapy. The tolerated dose of 6-MP (in mg/m2) was defined as the average of the doses administered to the patient during cycle two and beyond of maintenance therapy31. In addition, the 6-MP dose was preemptively adjusted for TPMT genotype in the Total XVI cohort (including TPMT actionable variants *2, *3A, *3B and *3C)24. Similarly, the 6-MP dose was adjusted according to actionable TPMT and NUDT15 genotypes for the patients treated at CHLA, when clinical testing results were available. In addition, 11 patients with ALL who were undergoing clinical care at CHLA were identified as NUDT15 *4 carriers. Six patients were included in this study as “cases” to constitute a validation cohort, while five patients were excluded (transferred to other institution, n = 3; early relapse, n = 1; not in maintenance phase yet, n = 1). A retrospective review of the 6 selected patients’ medical charts was conducted to retrieve the following information: self-reported patient demographics, age at ALL diagnosis, TPMT and NUDT15 genotypes, hematopoietic and chemistry laboratory values, 6-MP dose through maintenance phase, number of myelosuppressive episodes, and concentration of thiopurine metabolites (thioguanine nucleotides [TGNs] and methylmercaptopurine nucleotides [MMPNs]). Data sharing was approved by CHLA’s and St. Jude’s respective IRBs.


#### TPMT and NUDT15 genotyping

Genotyping was performed at St. Jude by using Illumina Infinium Global Diversity Array-8 for the UNOP and CHLA cohorts31. Whole-exome sequencing or targeted sequencing of coding and regulatory regions of TPMT and NUDT15 were performed for the Total XVI and AALL03N1 cohorts, respectively14,24. Patients in the additional clinical validation cohort were tested for TPMT and NUDT15 as part of routine care at CHLA. The test was performed at the Center for Personalized Medicine laboratory at CHLA via Sanger sequencing (BigDye Terminator v1.1, Life Technologies) on an automated fluorescent sequencer (ABI 3730 Genetic Analyzer, Applied Biosystems, Inc) by targeting exons 3-6 and 8-9 of the TPMT gene and exons 1-3 of the NUDT15 gene (*2, *3, *4, *5, *6, *7, *8, *9, *14). The reference sequence used for TPMT was NM_000367.4, and that for NUDT15 was NM_018283.3.


### Detection of 6-MP metabolites in patients

Genomic DNA from 138 patients treated at CHLA was extracted from buffy coats collected during maintenance therapy (Autogen, Holliston, MA). DNA-TG samples were quantified after a 24-h enzymatic digestion by liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and normalized to the DNA concentration (unit: fmol deoxythioguanosine/μg DNA), according to a published method32,33. All DNA-TG concentrations were normalized for the 14-day average dose prior to sampling. Red blood cell (RBC) samples were collected for the six patients included in the clinical validation cohort at different times of their maintenance therapy. MMPNs and TGNs were measured by LC-MS/MS by an accredited clinical laboratory (reference values for TGNs: 235-450 pmol/8 × 108 RBC; and for MMPNs: less than or equal to 5700 pmol/8 × 108 RBC)34,35.


### Statistical analysis

All measurement data were tested by using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to analyze categorical values. A univariable linear regression model was used to test the association of patients’ characteristics (age at diagnosis, sex, self-identified race, and/or ethnicity, and ALL risk group) with 6-MP tolerated dose. Covariates for which the nominal P was < 0.1 were included in a multivariable bidirectional linear stepwise regression model to assess the association between 6-MP tolerated dose and NUDT15 genotypes. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed by using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA) and R software (version 4.1.3; R foundation).


## Results


### Patient cohort and distribution of NUDT15 genotypes across race and/or ethnicity categories

A retrospective cohort of 1399 children with ALL were included in this study (889 male participants [63.5%]; median age at diagnosis [interquartile range, IQR], 5.1 [3.0-9.8] years) (Table S2). The cohort included 864 participants (61.8%) who self-identified as non-White, including 115 as Asian (8.2%), 156 as Black (11.2%), 537 as Hispanic/Latino (38.4%), and 56 as “other” (4.0%), as they could not be classified into the aforementioned categories. All patients were genotyped for TPMT and NUDT15. Only those with the TPMT *1/*1 genotype were included in order to focus the association analyses on NUDT15 variants. NUDT15 *2 and NUDT15 *3 were detected in 65 subjects, with allele frequencies (AFs) of 0.014 and 0.010, respectively, consistent with prior reports9,14. In addition, 17 patients from all 4 institutions carried the *4 allele, including one patient with the NUDT15 *2/*4 diplotype, resulting in an AF of 0.006 in this multi-ancestry cohort (Table S3). No participants were observed to have the *4/*4 diplotype. As expected, the distribution of NUDT15 genotypes differed significantly across race and/or ethnicity (Chi-square test, P < 0.001). Notably, all patients with the *4 allele self-identified as Hispanic/Latino; this allele was the second most frequent variant allele among this group of patients (following NUDT15 *2) with a Hispanic/Latino-enriched AF of 0.016.


### Mercaptopurine tolerance in patients with NUDT15 *4

All patients received 6-MP as part of their maintenance therapy, with the protocol starting doses between 50 and 75 mg/m2 (Table S1). Overall, a large interindividual variability in the tolerated dose was observed across all genotypes (Figure 1a). Patients with NUDT15 *1/*1 tolerated the highest dose of 6-MP (median [IQR], 62.2 [47.9-71.5] mg/m2 (Table 1). NUDT15 *1/*4 heterozygous patients were more sensitive and tolerated 39.0 [21.2-52.8] mg/m2, or 60% of the median dose administered to NUDT15 *1/*1 patients (P = 1.8 × 10−5). 6-MP dose was lower for patients heterozygous for NUDT15 *3 (41.6 mg/m2, 29.7-48.4) or *2 (42.5 mg/m2, 30.4-55.2), to an extent similar to that of NUDT15 *1/*4 patients. NUDT15 *3/*3 patients tolerated extremely low doses of 6-MP (7.2 mg/m2; IQR, 5.8-8.7), as did the one patient with the NUDT15 *2/*4 diplotype (2.4 mg/m2). NUDT15 *2, *3 and *4 alleles, as well as clinical covariates such as sex, age at diagnosis, race and/or ethnicity and ALL risk group, were significantly associated with 6-MP tolerance in a univariable model (nominal P value < 0.1) (Table 1). NUDT15 genotypes, sex, race and/or ethnicity and ALL risk group remained significant predictors of 6-MP tolerance in the final multivariable regression model, with similar estimates observed for NUDT15 *1/*3 and *1/*4 genotypes (−17.7 mg/m2, standard error [SE] of 3.9 and −16.7 mg/m2 [4.5], respectively, both P values < 0.001).


### Alteration of 6-MP disposition in patients with NUDT15 *4

To investigate how NUDT15 variants influence the disposition of 6-MP, 138 patients from the CHLA cohort were sampled during the maintenance phase to measure the peripheral leukocyte concentrations of 6-MP active metabolite, DNA-TG. A total of 368 samples were analyzed by LC-MS/MS (median of 2 samples per patient, range, 1-6). For each sample, the DNA-TG concentration was normalized against the 14-day stable 6-MP dose prior to sampling, and the average normalized DNA-TG concentration was determined for each patient. Overall, patients with a decreased function of NUDT15, i.e., carriers of *2, *3 or *4 alleles (n = 14), accumulated more DNA-TG than patients with normal enzyme activity, i.e., NUDT15 *1/*1 (n = 124) (median of 8.9 fmol/μg DNA/MP, IQR 5.4-14.4, vs 6.1 fmol/μg DNA/MP, IQR 4.5-8.9, respectively, P value of the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank sum test = 0.018) (Figure 1b). Among the carriers of a decreased function allele (i.e., *2, *3, or *4), patients NUDT15 *1/*4 displayed the highest median [IQR] concentration of DNA-TG (n = 4, 16.5 fmol/μg DNA/6-MP, [11.4-20.7]), compared with NUDT15 *1/*2 patients (n = 6, 6.3 fmol/μg DNA/6-MP, [5.3-9.0], P = 0.038) or NUDT15 *1/*3 patients (n = 4, 8.2 fmol/μg DNA/6-MP, [6.8-15.2], P = 0.48).


### Real-world evaluation of NUDT15 *4 clinical relevance in patients with ALL

A retrospective study was conducted at CHLA, and 6 patients with ALL were identified as having TPMT *1/*1 and NUDT15 *1/*4 genotype (Table 2) by routine clinical pharmacogenetics testing. All of them self-identified as Hispanic/Latino and received daily 6-MP as part of their maintenance therapy, which was completed for 3 of them at the time of inclusion in this study. For the maintenance phase, three patients initiated 6-MP at the full dose of 75 mg/m2; the other three patients initiated 6-MP at a reduced dose of 60 mg/m2 (80% of full dose). We elected to restrict the analysis of the tolerated dose to the second and subsequent cycles of maintenance phase to ensure that the 6-MP dose was stable for each patient. Considering all 6 patients, a large interindividual variability was observed in the tolerance to 6-MP. The median tolerated dose was 38.7 mg/m2 (IQR, 33.7-54.0), which was similar to the dose tolerated by the NUDT15 *1/*4 patients in the retrospective multi-ancestry cohort (Figure 1a).

Of the 5 patients NUDT15 *1/*4 in whom severe neutropenia developed (defined by ANC < 500/mm3), 4 experienced it within the first maintenance cycle (range, 22-57 days) (Table 2 and Figure 2a), leading to multiple instances of held doses (Figure 2b). In the 5th patient, neutropenia first occurred during the 3rd maintenance cycle. For this patient, 6-MP was empirically started at a lower dose (60 mg/m2), a possible explanation for better tolerance early on. Erythrocyte TGN, and MMPN concentrations were available for 4 of the 6 patients. All patients had high values of MMPNs throughout their maintenance therapy (over 5700 pmol/8 × 108 RBC). TGN concentrations were within the reference range of 235-450 pmol/8 × 108 RBC, for all 4 patients when measured in cycle 1 and 2, reflecting the dose adjustments based on tolerance to the treatment during the first cycle.


## Discussion

The identification of NUDT15 as a major determinant of thiopurine metabolism has significantly improved pharmacogenetic-based dose adjustment of thiopurine therapy17. Currently, testing for NUDT15 deficiency is based on the genotyping of the most frequent and clinically characterized loss-of-function variants (i.e., NUDT15 *2, *3 and *9)9,14. However, rare “uncertain function” variants, such as NUDT15 *4, may also contribute to inter-patient variability in NUDT15 activity and thus thiopurine tolerance, although there is a paucity of clinical evidence about their impact on drug toxicity38.

Our comprehensive analysis of the association between NUDT15 genotypes and thiopurine tolerance, coupled with real-world clinical observations, shows that patients with the NUDT15 *4 allele face a higher risk of toxicity than those with wild-type NUDT15. Overall, patients with one copy of NUDT15 *4 tolerated 39.0 mg/m2 (IQR, 19.8-52.7), representing 50% to 80% of a standard starting dose in ALL protocols. This suggests that the current dose reduction of 30% to 80% of a standard dose recommended by CPIC for NUDT15 intermediate metabolizers (IM), may efficiently mitigate the risk of myelosuppression in patients carrying the *4 allele17. Although NUDT15 *2/*4 diplotype is likely to be rare (estimated to 0.13% in the Hispanic/Latino population17), we detected one patient who tolerated extremely low doses of 6-MP, as similarly observed in NUDT15 “poor metabolizer” (PM) patients, raising the possibility of reclassifying these individuals from “possible IM” to PM. Finally, genetic variants in both NUDT15 and TPMT can co-occur and expose patients to an increased risk of 6-MP induced toxicity31. In this particular cohort, we identified two patients who were NUDT15 *1/*4 and had an actionable variant in TPMT (Figure S1).

NUDT15 *4 (c.416G>A, p.R139H) is characterized by the replacement of an arginine by a histidine at codon 139 of NUDT15, which is also impacted by NUDT15 *3 (c.415C>T, p.R139C). This change results in an unstable protein with reduced catalytic activity9,23, as confirmed by the accumulation of active thiopurine metabolite DNA-TG in patients carrying the allele. NUDT15 *4 is highly represented in the admixed American population (AF of 0.018 in gnomAD), with local ancestry comprising Amerindian and European ancestries (AF of 0.0645 and 7.0 × 10−5 respectively)39. This variant has also been detected in Brazilian, Amazonian40, and Uruguayan41 populations. This population predominancy associated with this allele, as observed in our multi-ancestry cohort, highlights the intricate relationship between pharmacogenetic variants and ancestry.

Encompassing independent, multinational cohorts and representing diverse ancestries, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of the tolerance to thiopurine in patients carrying rare genetic variants of NUDT15. We present compelling evidence, supported by real-world observations, that individuals with the NUDT15 *1/*4 genotype may be considered as NUDT15 IM according to the CPIC classification. This new categorization would have significant implication for clinical practice, especially for Hispanic/Latino patients17. Nonetheless, this study has limitations. By restricting the analyses to patients with ALL who were receiving 6-MP for the maintenance phase, we did not assess the effects of NUDT15 *4 on other dose regimens and other thiopurine-based protocols (i.e., 6-TG in ALL and azathioprine in autoimmune diseases), although pharmacogenetics-guided dose adjustment is warranted for these drugs to prevent thiopurine-related toxicity44,45. In addition, all patients self-reported their race and/or ethnicity, which may not be as accurate as the assessment of ancestry with individual genomic markers. This could introduce a bias in the interpretation of the associations between ancestry and treatment outcomes. Furthermore, no *4 homozygous cases were detected, which, based on available data for other NUDT15 no-function alleles, might suggest that these patients could be classified as NUDT15 PM and would require further dose reduction. Overall, these findings suggest that NUDT15 *4 is a no-function allele, similar to NUDT15 *2 and NUDT15 *3, and underscore the importance of incorporating this allele into pharmacogenetics-guided dose adjustment algorithms to optimize the use of thiopurine in ALL. Moreover, these data highlight the necessity for pharmacogenetic studies to include patients from various ancestries and to develop more-diverse genetic panels, covering a wider set of variants to provide the clinical-based evidence required to validate novel genotype-phenotype associations.
