## Abstract

**Introduction:** 
Interindividual variability in the efficacy and toxicity of 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP) and methotrexate (MTX) drugs remains a major challenge in the treatment of pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Germline variation in pharmacogenes involved in drug metabolism, transport, and folate pathways may contribute to this variability.

**Methods:** 
In this retrospective cohort study, 43 pediatric patients with ALL treated at a tertiary referral center in Croatia according to ALL IC-BFM 2002 or 2009 protocols were genotyped for eleven variants in TPMT, ITPA, NUDT15, PACSIN2, MTHFR, TYMS, SLC19A1, and SLCO1B1 genes. Associations with average 6-MP dose during maintenance therapy, MTX pharmacokinetics during consolidation and MTX-related toxicity were analyzed. A polygenic risk score (PRS) was constructed to assess cumulative genetic risk of developing toxicity when administering these two drugs.

**Results:** 
Carriers of the ITPA rs1127354 variant required significantly lower average 6-MP doses compared with wild-type carriers. The NUDT15 rs61973267 variant was associated with higher tolerated 6-MP doses after exclusion of outliers. No significant associations were observed between individual variants and MTX clearance. A trend toward reduced risk of oral mucositis was observed in carriers of the SLCO1B1 rs4149056 variant. PRS analyses that included carriers of the TPMT rs1142345 and ITPA rs1127354 variants showed a borderline association with the requirement for 6-MP dose reduction.

**Conclusions:** 
Our findings support a role for ITPA and NUDT15 variants in modulating 6-MP dose requirements, while single-variant associations with MTX pharmacokinetics and toxicity were limited and not supported. These results underscore the complexity of antimetabolite pharmacogenetics and suggest that integrative polygenic approaches may better capture clinically relevant pharmacogenetic risk.

## Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common malignancy of childhood, characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature lymphoid precursor cells of either B- or T-cell lineage [[1](#CR1)]. Contemporary risk-adapted treatment protocols have achieved survival rates exceeding 85% [[2](#CR2)], with therapy guided by standardized multiagent chemotherapy protocols, such as the European Berlin–Frankfurt–Münster (BFM) protocol [[3](#CR3)]. A cornerstone of pediatric ALL therapy is the prolonged administration of antimetabolite drugs, particularly 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP) and methotrexate (MTX), whose efficacy and toxicity show marked interindividual variability. This variability cannot be fully explained by clinical or treatment-related factors alone and is thought to be influenced, at least in part, by genetic variation in pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic pathways.

Both 6-MP and MTX have complex absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination (ADME) profiles that involve multiple enzymes and transporters encoded by a range of pharmacogenes. For 6-MP, intracellular activation and inactivation depend on a network of enzymes regulating thiopurine metabolism [[4](#CR4), [5](#CR5)], while for MTX, cellular uptake, polyglutamation, efflux, and systemic clearance are mediated by several solute carrier and ATP-binding cassette transporters [[6](#CR6), [7](#CR7)]. Consequently, numerous genes have been proposed as candidate pharmacogenes that may modulate treatment response, toxicity, or dose intensity during ALL therapy.

In addition to its transport and metabolism, MTX exerts its antileukemic effect through inhibition of key enzymes in folate-dependent one-carbon metabolism. MTX directly inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), as well as thymidylate synthase (TYMS) and AICAR transformylase, thereby disrupting *de novo* purine and thymidylate synthesis. These effects also influence methylation reactions, which are critically dependent on folate availability and regulation of the folate cycle. Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) plays a central role in this pathway by controlling the balance between nucleotide synthesis and methylation capacity, providing a strong biological rationale for investigating genetic variation in this gene in relation to MTX response and toxicity [[8](#CR8)].

Over the past two decades, numerous association studies have examined polymorphisms in candidate pharmacogenes involved in thiopurine and MTX pathways, including genes encoding metabolic enzymes, transporters, and targets of antifolate activity. However, the clinical relevance of many of these variants remains unclear. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported inconsistent or conflicting results, particularly for commonly studied variants such as *MTHFR* rs1801133 and rs1801131 with most meta-analyses failing to demonstrate robust or clinically actionable associations with MTX toxicity or treatment outcomes [[9](#CR9)–[11](#CR11)]. Discrepancies among studies are likely attributable to differences in treatment protocols, MTX dosing, toxicity definitions, sample size, and population genetic background.

Given the lack of strong evidence for clinical actionability of many other pharmacogenes and pharmacovariants, current clinical pharmacogenetic guidelines reflect a conservative approach. At present, the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) and ClinPGx recognize only *TPMT* and *NUDT15* as clinically actionable pharmacogenes for thiopurine dosing in ALL [[12](#CR12), [13](#CR13)]. Nevertheless, several other genes, including *MTHFR* and *SLC19A1*, have been assigned a moderate level of evidence (ClinPGx Level of Evidence 2), indicating biologically plausible and repeatedly observed associations that remain insufficiently validated for routine clinical implementation. This highlights the ongoing need for well-characterized population-specific studies to further clarify the role of these candidate genes. Given the multifactorial and polygenic nature of antimetabolite response, there is increasing clinical interest in integrative approaches that move beyond single-gene testing. Polygenic risk score (PRS) represents a potential strategy to aggregate the combined effects of multiple genetic variants across relevant metabolic and transport pathways. In a clinical context, PRS-based models may improve risk stratification for drug-related toxicity, identify patients at higher risk for dose-limiting adverse events, and support more individualized dosing strategies while maintaining protocol intensity. In this context, additional pharmacogenetic studies in distinct and underrepresented populations are essential to improve the understanding of genetic contributors to antimetabolite response in pediatric ALL.

The present study analyzed pharmacogenetic variants representing the most clinically relevant and commonly studied genetic determinants of interindividual variability in response and toxicity to antimetabolite therapy in pediatric ALL. Specifically, variants in genes related to folate metabolism (*MTHFR*, *TYMS*, *SLC19A1*), drug transport (*SLCO1B1*), and thiopurine metabolism (*TPMT, ITPA*, *NUDT15, PACSIN2*) were investigated within the Croatian population. Furthermore, we evaluated genotype-dependent risks of severe MTX-related toxicity and variations in average 6-MP dose using a PRS approach. By contributing data from a homogeneous treatment setting and a specific population background, this study aims to add to the existing body of evidence and to help clarify which pharmacogenetic markers may warrant further consideration in future clinical and translational research.

## Methods

### Subjects Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

This retrospective cohort study enrolled 43 pediatric patients with ALL; 28 males, 15 females diagnosed between 2007 and 2020 at the Division of Pediatric Hematology, Oncology and Clinical Genetics, Department of Pediatrics, Clinical Hospital Center Rijeka, Croatia. Patients were treated according to the ALL IC-BFM 2002 (diagnosed before April 2010) or ALL IC-BFM 2009 protocol (diagnosed from April 2010 onward). Patients were stratified according to the risk of treatment failure conferred by standard prognostic factors into three risk groups: standard risk group (SR), intermediate risk group (IR), and high risk group (HR). Treatment was adapted according to risk group and leukemic cell immunophenotype, classified as either B-cell precursor or T-cell precursor. Patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table [1](#Tab1). Peripheral blood samples were taken with the consent of parents or guardians of each patient, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Clinical Hospital Center Rijeka (No. 2170-29-02/1-23-4 from 28 July 2023.).

### Blood Sampling and DNA Extraction

Peripheral blood samples of 43 patients were collected into 4.5 mL sodium citrate anticoagulant tubes (Vacutainer, Becton–Dickinson, Plymouth, UK). Genomic DNA extraction from whole peripheral blood was performed using QIAampDNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany), and samples were stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

### 6-MP in the Maintenance Therapy Phase

All patients received maintenance therapy, consisting of daily oral 6-MP at a standard dose of 50 mg/m^2^ and weekly oral MTX at a dose of 20 mg/m^2^. The maintenance phase typically began 2 weeks after the completion of reinduction (delayed intensification) phase, and lasted between 64 and 98 weeks. White blood cell (WBC) counts were monitored with a target range of 2.0–3.0 × 10^9^/L. Clinical evaluations were performed biweekly throughout the maintenance period. Dose adjustments of 6-MP were made on the basis of WBC levels: if the WBC count decreased below 2.0 × 10^9^/L, the 6-MP dose was reduced by 25% in weekly increments. In cases where WBC levels dropped below 1.0 × 10^9^/L, 6-MP administration was temporarily discontinued until hematologic recovery was observed. Toxicity and adverse effects associated with 6-MP were monitored using surrogate markers, specifically the number of off-therapy weeks and the average administered dose (expressed as a percentage relative to the standard 50 mg/m^2^ dose, considered 100%).

### MTX in the Consolidation Therapy Phase

The consolidation phase of the BFM treatment protocols included MTX administration at risk-adapted doses: patients classified as SR B-ALL received medium-dose MTX (MD-MTX, 2 g/m^2^); patients with T-ALL in either the SR or IR groups, as well as all patients classified as HR, received high-dose MTX (HD-MTX, 5 g/m^2^); in patients classified as IR BCP-ALL, the MTX treatment protocol included randomization to either 2 g/m^2^ or 5 g/m^2^ MTX. A total of four MD-MTX or HD-MTX, followed by leucovorin rescue therapy to mitigate MTX toxicity, was administered to each patient. Pharmacokinetic monitoring of MTX plasma concentrations was performed exclusively in patients receiving the high-dose regimen. This monitoring aimed to distinguish between normal and delayed MTX elimination profiles, since the latter is associated with increased incidence of adverse drug reactions. Plasma MTX levels were quantified at four specific time points postinfusion start: 24, 36, 42 and 48 h. According to the BFM protocol standards, plasma MTX concentrations exceeding 150 μmol/L at 24 h, 3 μmol/L at 36 h, 1 μmol/L at 42 h, or 0.4 μmol/L at 48 h were indicative of slow MTX clearance. Patients with slow elimination might require intensified monitoring and supportive interventions owing to higher toxicity risk. Nonhematological toxicities—including oral mucositis, gastrointestinal toxicities, hepatotoxicity, renal impairment and neurotoxicity—were clinically assessed and graded on the basis of the World Health Organization (WHO) toxicity criteria. These grading scales ranged from 0 (no toxicity) to 4 (severe toxicity) and incorporated clinical evaluation, along with laboratory and radiological findings. For purposes of analysis in the referenced study, patients were dichotomized into groups based on the severity of toxicity: no or mild toxicity (grade 0–1) or moderate-to-severe toxicity (grade 2–4).

### Genotyping

Participants in this study were analyzed for genetic variants in *TPMT* rs11242345, *ITPA* rs1127354, *NUDT15* rs45465203, rs79687000, rs61973267, and *PACSIN2* rs2413739 for monitoring response to 6-MP therapy in maintenance phase (Table [2](#Tab2)). In addition, genotyping of *TPMT* rs1800460 was performed in carriers of rs11242345 to further characterize possible *TPMT**3A genotype status, given that these variants are mostly coinherited in European populations. Variants in *MTHFR* rs1801133, *TYMS* rs34743033, and rs11280056, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, *SLCO1B1* rs4149056, and *PACSIN2* rs2413739 were analyzed for monitoring response to MTX therapy in consolidation phase (Table [3](#Tab3)). Detection of variants in *TPMT*, *ITPA* and *MTHFR* genes was performed by a polymerase chain reaction restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR–RFLP) method, as previously described [[14](#CR14)]. For variant detection in *NUDT15, PACSIN2, TYMS, SLC19A1, and SLCO1B1* gene, direct sequencing of PCR product using BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) on 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) was carried out, as previously described [[15](#CR15)]. For *NUDT15*, the sequencing approach covered not only coding regions but also adjacent 5′UTR, 3′UTR, and selected intronic regions to cover for all potential pharmacogenetic variants. All detected variants within these regions were included in the further analysis. Primer sequences and annealing conditions used for amplification of each variant are available in Supplementary Materials.

Allelic frequencies of variants of interest were compared with the European population, and data were obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project [[16](#CR16)] via the Ensembl database, which includes genomic data from 503 individuals of European ancestry. Variant frequencies for all genes of interest, except *TYMS*, were extracted from the Ensembl database. Genetic variants were selected on the basis of prior evidence of association with 6-MP and MTX pharmacokinetics, toxicity, and dose requirements in pediatric ALL. Candidate genes included key pharmacogenes involved in thiopurine metabolism and MTX transport and folate metabolism. Variants were prioritized on the basis of previously reported clinical relevance, functional impact, and evidence from genome-wide association studies, candidate gene studies, or meta-analyses.

### Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using software R version 4.4.0. To assess genotyping performance, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was examined for all detected variants using the exact test from *HardyWeinberg* package in R. A dominant genetic model was applied for genotype comparisons, in which individuals homozygous for the major allele were compared with those who were heterozygous or homozygous for the minor allele. Genetic variants involved in the 6-MP metabolic pathway (*TPMT, ITPA, NUDT15*, and *PACSIN2*) were compared with average 6-MP dose using Mann–Whitney *U* test and Kruskal–Wallis test. Variants in transporter genes (*SLC19A1* and *SLCO1B1*) were tested for association with a number of low MTX clearance episodes using linear regression. Impact of genetic variants (*TYMS*, *MTHFR, SLC19A1*, *SLCO1B*, and *PACSIN2)* on MTX-induced toxicity was analyzed using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived from logistic regression models, adjusting for confounding factors including demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as MTX dose. As explained before, the effect of the variants rs1142345 in *TPMT* gene and rs1127354 in *ITPA* gene on 6-MP metabolism is well described in the literature [[4](#CR4), [5](#CR5)]. However, the combined effect of these variants is not yet fully understood. In contrast, although multiple genetic variants have been associated with MTX pharmacokinetics and toxicity, their individual effects are generally modest and not considered clinically actionable [[9](#CR9)–[11](#CR11)], and their combined contribution remains insufficiently characterized. Therefore, we calculated a PRS for each patient, using the formula PRS = Σ(β_i_* *G*_i_). PRS models were made to assess the genetic risk of developing the following phenotypes: oral mucositis (*MTHFR* rs1801133, *TYMS* rs34743033, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, *SLCO1B1* rs4149056, Supplementary Table S2) [[9](#CR9)], GIT toxicity (*MTHFR* rs1801133, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, Supplementary Table S2) [[9](#CR9)], liver toxicity (*MTHFR* rs1801133, *TYMS* rs34743033, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, *SLCO1B1* rs4149056, Supplementary Table S2) [[9](#CR9)], neuro toxicity (*MTHFR* rs1801133, *TYMS* rs34743033, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, Table S2) [[9](#CR9)], renal toxicity (*MTHFR* rs1801133, *TYMS* rs34743033, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, *SLCO1B1* rs4149056, Supplementary Table S2) [[9](#CR9)] and MP dose adjustment (*TPMT* rs11242345 [[17](#CR17)], *ITPA* rs1127354 [[18](#CR18)], Supplementary Table S3); In this formula, *β*_i_ denotes the effect size of each single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), extracted from previously published meta-analysis and GWAS studies; *G*_i_ represents the genotype score reflecting the number of variant alleles carried. Genotypes were coded on the basis of the predicted functional impact on enzymatic activity, with 0 assigned to homozygous carriers of the reference allele, heterozygotes were given 0.5 points and homozygous carriers of the minor allele, associated with the impaired protein function, were given 1 point; Σ denotes the sum over all SNPs included in the PRS. Risk alleles associated with impaired enzymatic function and *β*_i_ of each effect of each variant are presented in Supplementary Tables S2–3. Variants were included in the PRS if *β*_i_ were available from published GWAS or meta-analyses and if they were functionally relevant to MP dose or MTX toxicity. When multiple sources were available, *β*_i_ estimates were selected from the largest and most relevant studies. Owing to limited availability of *β*_i_ for some variants, the PRS was restricted to a subset of SNPs. Differences in distribution of the calculated PRS for the impaired enzymatic function between patients who did versus did not experience MTX-related toxicity or MP dose adjustment were assessed using the Mann–Whitney *U* test for continuous data. Probability values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Multiple comparison correction of all probabilities was applied using false discovery rate (FDR) method.

## Results

### Patients

Median age was 5 with interquartile range 3.9–7.1. Most patients, 88%, were treated according to the ALL IC-BFM 2009 protocol and 12% were treated according to ALL IC-BFM 2002 protocol. According to risk stratification, 14% of patients were classified as SR, 79% as IR, and 7% as HR. In the consolidation phase, 35%, 58%, and 7% of patients were treated according to the mM (MD-MDX), M (HD-MDX) and HR protocol, respectively, whereas both M and HR protocol incorporated HD-MTX (5 g/m^2^ MTX).

### Population PGx

A comprehensive genetic analysis was conducted to investigate variants associated with the pharmacogenetics of 6-MP and MTX therapy. Specifically, two SNPs located within the coding regions of the *ITPA* rs1127354 and *TPMT* rs11242345 genes were examined to assess their influence on the average dosing requirements of 6-MP. Additionally, three SNPs located within intronic regions of *PACSIN2* rs2413739 and *NUDT15* rs45465203, and rs79687000 genes were analyzed, along with one SNP located in the 3ʹUTR of *NUDT15* rs61973267. Concurrently, the study incorporated the analysis of four SNPs situated in the coding region of *MTHFR* rs1801133, two variants located in the noncoding regions of *TYMS*: rs34743033 in the 5′ UTR and rs11280056 in the 3′ UTR two, *SLC19A1* rs1051266, *SLCO1B1* rs4149056, and *PACSIN2* rs2413739 genes, targeting genetic determinants relevant to MTX therapeutic response. Furthermore, two variants within the *TYMS* gene were included: a variable number of tandem repeats (VNTR) and an insertion-deletion (indel) polymorphism located within the 5’UTR and 3’UTR. Variant frequencies in Croatian pediatric patients with ALL and in European descent population are summarized in Tables [2](#Tab2) and [3](#Tab3). To explore potential population-specific genetic differences, we directly compared allele frequency distributions between the ALL cohort and the European reference population. This analysis identified statistically significant differences for two SNPs: rs1127354 in *ITPA* (*p* = 0.02) and rs4149056 in *SLCO1B1* (*p* = 0.03). Notably, rs4149056 in *SLCO1B1* deviated from HWE in Croatian population, whereas all other analyzed variants were in HWE. After FDR correction, none of the analyzed variants showed statistically significance violation of HWE. Both variants were more common in the pediatric patients with ALL than in the European population. These significant differences highlight the importance of considering ancestral genetic background when interpreting pharmacogenetic data and designing studies involving diverse populations.

### Association of Genetic Variants with the Average Dose of 6-MP

During maintenance therapy, the median dose of 6-MP for the ALL cohort was approximately 67% [IQR 60–74%] of the standard 50 mg/m^2^/day dose, respectively. The administered average 6-MP dose ranged from a minimum of 43% to a maximum of 88%. None of the patients missed more than 10% of their therapy time due to marked leukopenia.

The investigated intronic and 3’UTR variants of *NUDT15*, located within 500 bp of each other, are not in strong mutual linkage disequilibrium and were therefore analyzed as individual SNPs. Carriers of the *TPMT* rs11242345 variant, who were additionally genotyped for rs1800460 (*n* = 3) were also found to carry rs1800460 variant, indicating that all three patients most likely carried the *1/*3A diplotype. No statistically significant difference in the mean 6-MP dose was observed compared with the patients with wild-type genotype (*p* = 0.80, Mann–Whitney *U* test). Given the established role of *TPMT* variants as strong predictors of 6-MP-induced myelotoxicity and the need for dose reduction, these individuals were excluded from further analyses involving other genetic variants to enhance analytical accuracy. A total of 12 patients were heterozygous for the rs1127354 *ITPA* variant, and statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in the average 6-MP dose between rs1127354 CC and CA genotypes—68.7% versus 59.7%, respectively (*p* = 0.045, *T*-test) (Fig. [1](#Fig1). A) However, the result did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction. We also identified an association between the rs61973267 variant in the 3′UTR region of the *NUDT15* gene and the average dose of 6-MP. Patients with the GA genotype tolerated higher average 6-MP dose compared with those carrying the GG genotype—75.3% versus 65.4%, respectively; however, these results were not statistically significant (*p* = 0.30, Mann–Whitney *U* test) (Fig. [1](#Fig1)B). No statistically significant associations were found between the intronic and 3’UTR variants in the *NUDT15* gene or the intronic variant in the *PACSIN2* gene and average dose of 6-MP (Table [4](#Tab4)).

### MTX Kinetics During Consolidation Therapy Phase

MTX plasma concentration data during the consolidation phase were available for all 26 pediatric patients who underwent the HD-MTX therapy, comprising 23 patients treated according to the M protocol and three patients treated per HR protocol. Plasma MTX concentrations were measured following each of the four scheduled HD-MTX infusions to characterize the MTX elimination profile, which was classified as either normal clearance or delayed clearance. For each patient, the MTX elimination profile after each HD-MTX administration was used to determine the number of MTX low clearance episodes (from 0 to 4). The number of MTX delayed clearance episodes was correlated against rs1051266 in *SLC19A1* and rs4149056 *SLCO1B* variants in two key transporter genes implicated in MTX pharmacokinetics*.* Furthermore, the potential combined influence of these variants on MTX clearance was assessed to identify possible joint effects. However, our results show that there is no significant correlation between individual gene variants or their joint genetic effect and MTX clearance.

### Polygenic Risk Score

To evaluate the cumulative genetic contribution to MTX-related multi-organ toxicity and the subsequent need for 6-MP dose adjustment, a PRS was constructed by integrating all pharmacogenetically relevant variants analyzed in this study. Each SNP was weighted according to its reported effect size (*β*) on enzymatic activity, derived from previously published meta-analyses [[9](#CR9)] (Supplementary Table [S1](#MOESM1)) and GWAS data [[17](#CR17), [18](#CR18)] (Supplementary Table S2). Genotype scores reflected the predicted degree of enzymatic impairment, with higher PRS values indicating a greater burden of risk alleles associated with reduced drug metabolism. Comparison of PRS distributions did not reveal statistically significant differences between patients who did and did not develop MTX-related multi-organ toxicity (Supplementary Table S3). Patients requiring lower average 6-MP doses tended to have higher PRS values than those maintained on higher average doses, consistent with a greater cumulative burden of function-impairing alleles. The PRS model incorporated the *TPMT* rs1142345 and *ITPA* rs1127354 variants. Although the association did not reach statistical significance, a borderline trend was observed (Mann–Whitney *U* test, *p* = 0.08), suggesting that combined genetic risk may contribute to 6-MP intolerance (Table [7](#Tab7)).

## Discussion

Pediatric ALL treatment protocols rely heavily on chemotherapeutic agents such as 6-MP and MTX. Although these drugs have markedly improved survival rates, substantial inter-individual variability in drug response and toxicity remains a significant clinical challenge. Pharmacogenetic markers for thiopurines are already implemented in clinical practice; however, this is not the case for other drugs used in ALL treatments, including MTX. Therefore, there is a clear need for further investigation of pharmacogenetic variants with the potential to personalize ALL therapy. In this study of Croatian pediatric patients with ALL treated at the tertiary care center, we explored associations between germline variants in pharmacogenes and both 6-MP dosing as well as MTX pharmacokinetics and toxicity.

Thiopurine S-methyltransferase (*TPMT*) is a key enzyme in thiopurine metabolism[[19](#CR19)]. and well-established pharmacogenetic marker with CPIC dosing recommendations[[13](#CR13)]. Loss-of-function *TPMT* variants are associated with reduced enzymatic activity and a markedly increased risk of thiopurine-induced myelosuppression.

Unexpectedly, our study did not identify a statistically significant association between *TPMT* variant and thiopurine-related phenotypes in our cohort. This finding differs from the majority of published studies and suggests that, in the Croatian population, *TPMT* genotype may have limited predictive value for thiopurine tolerance. This discrepancy may reflect population-specific genetic structure, limited statistical power, or the contribution of additional genetic modifiers and nongenetic factors. Notably, thiopurine intolerance has also been reported in patients with normal TPMT activity, suggesting that TPMT alone may not full explain inter-individual variability. Our finding support the need for population-specific validation of established pharmacogenetic markers and highlight the potential value of multigene approaches.

The *ITPA* gene, encodes inosine triphosphate pyrophosphohydrolase, which prevents the accumulation of noncanonical and potentially mutagenic nucleotides. Reduced *ITPA* activity has been associated with increased susceptibility to thiopurine-related toxicity [[20](#CR20), [21](#CR21)].The most extensively studied *ITPA* variant, rs1127354 has been linked to thiopurine intolerance, including risk of neutropenia and lower tolerated 6-MP doses, although findings vary across populations [[22](#CR22)–[25](#CR25)].

In our pediatric ALL Croatian cohort, *ITPA* rs1127354 heterozygosity was associated with a significantly lower average 6-MP dose compared with wild-type patients (*p* = 0.045), supporting a reduced-tolerance phenotype consistent with prior reports. This interpretation is consistent with existing meta-analytic evidence where Lee and coworkers [[26](#CR26)], reported that *ITPA* rs1127354 is associated with an increased risk of neutropenia and hepatotoxicity in pediatric patients with ALL, with carriers more frequently requiring dose reductions. Similarly, studies from Asian populations have demonstrated that *ITPA* rs1127354, either alone or in combination with *NUDT15* variants, modulates 6-MP intolerance [[27](#CR27)–[30](#CR30)]. We also observed a higher frequency of the variant allele compared with reference European populations, suggesting potentional population specific relevance, while this findings should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size. *NUDT15* is a key pharmacogene involved in thiopurine metabolism, encoding an enzyme that hydrolyzes cytotoxic thioguanine triphosphates (TGTP/TdGTP) and prevents their incorporation into DNA. Loss of function variants are associated with excessive metabolite accumulation and severe thiopurine-related toxicity [[31](#CR31)], and *NUDT15* is therefore considered clinically actionable according to CPIC guidelines [[32](#CR32)].

In our cohort, the rs61973267 variant in the 3’UTR of *NUDT15* was associated with higher 6-MP dose tolerance in heterozygous carriers compared with wild type individuals, although this finding did not reach statistical significance. A similar trend has been reported in a Slovenian pediatric ALL cohort [[33](#CR33)], supporting a potential modulatory role of this regulatory variant, although no significant association was observed in two other studies [[34](#CR34), [35](#CR35)].

While most prior studies have focused on coding loss-of-function variants such as rs116855232 [[36](#CR36), [37](#CR37)], our findings suggest that noncoding variants may also contribute to interindividual variability in thiopurine response, potentially through regulatory mechanisms. Although the clinical relevance of rs61973267 remains uncertain, the consistent direction of effect across studies supports further investigation of regulatory *NUDT15* variants [[12](#CR12), [38](#CR38), [39](#CR39)]. These results support current recommendations to include *NUDT15* in pharmacogenetic testing, while also suggesting that a broader range of variants may improve prediction of thiopurine tolerance.

Previous studies have linked the *PACSIN2* rs2413739 variant to MP-related toxicity during maintenance therapy in pediatric ALL where the TT genotype has been associated with an increased risk of severe GITtoxicity independent of *TPMT* status [[40](#CR40), [41](#CR41)] and these findings have been replicated across protocols [[42](#CR42)], suggesting a potential modifying role of *PACSIN2* on thiopurine response through effects on TPMT activity and vesicular trafficking. However, this variant is not currently considered clinically actionable under CPIC guidelines and appears to function as a context-dependent modifier influenced by MP dose intensity and treatment phase.

Pharmacogenetic studies have identified multiple genes contributing to the marked inter-individual variability in MTX response, including genes involved in folate metabolism (e.g., *MTHFR*, *MTR*, *MTRR*, *DHFR*), and drug transport (e.g., *SLCO1B1*, *SLC19A1*, *OAT1*, *OAT3*). However, no pharmacogenetic marker is currently considered clinically actionable for MTX dosing according to CPIC guidelines, and genotyping is therefore not routinely recommended.

The *SLCO1B1* gene encodes the hepatic organic anion transporter OATP1B1, a key mediator of hepatic MTX uptake and systemic clearance. The rs4149056 variant has been associated with reduced transporter function and altered MTX pharmacokinetics, and is among the extensively studied polymorphisms in this context ([43](#CR43)–[45](#CR45)). In our cohort, we did not observe a significant association between rs4149056 and MTX pharmacokinetic parameters. However, we identified a trend suggesting that patients with the wild-type genotype were more likely to develop oral mucositis compared with variant carriers. This is consistent with findings reported by Liu et al. [[6](#CR6)], who observed a reduced risk of mucositis among carriers of the variant allele, although no association was observed in several other studies [[44](#CR44), [46](#CR46)], while at least one study has described an association with mucositis risk [[47](#CR47)], highlighting inconsistency across cohorts. Although not statistically significant, this observation is biologically plausible. Reduced OATP1B1 activity in variant carriers may limit hepatic MTX uptake and intracellular polyglutamation, potentially decreasing toxicity in rapidly proliferating tissues such as the oral mucosa.

Taken together, these findings suggest that rs4149056 may act as a nonactionable modifier of MTX toxicity, with clinical relevance influenced by additional genetic and treatment-related factors. Multiple studies have evaluated variants in *MTHFR*, *TYMS*, and *SLC19A1* in relation to MTX pharmacokinetics and toxicity, with inconsistent results across populations. While some reports associate these variants with altered MTX plasma levels, hepatotoxicity, or GIT, none of these genes are regarded as CPIC-actionable for MTX dosing. In our cohort, we did not observe any significant associations between these variants and MTX pharmacokinetics or MTX-related toxicity, supporting the notion that their individual predictive value is limited.

Taken together, our findings reinforce the distinction between CPIC-defined actionable pharmacogenes and nonactionable modifier genes, emphasizing that variants such as *PACSIN2*, *MTHFR*, *TYMS*, and *SLC19A1* may contribute to interindividual variability only in specific clinical contexts and are insufficient as standalone predictors of MTX response or toxicity.

Finally, we evaluated the cumulative genetic contribution to MTX-related multiorgan toxicity and 6-MP dose requirements by constructing a PRS incorporating pharmacogenetically relevant variants. Variants associated with reduced enzymatic activity would be expected to contribute to increased drug intolerance. Although no statistically significant differences in PRS distributions were observed between patients with and without MTX-related multiorgan toxicity, patients maintained on lower average 6-MP doses tended to have higher PRS values. This suggests a possible association between cumulative genetic burden and reduced drug tolerance, although the observed trend did not reach statistical significance (*p* = 0.08).

Limitations of the present study include the relatively small cohort size (*n* = 43), which substantially limits statistical power, particularly for the detection of associations involving rare variants. As a result, the study may be underpowered to detect modest genetic effects, increasing the likelihood of both false-negative and false-positive findings. Consequently, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution and considered as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Furthermore, given the small sample size and low frequency of certain variants (e.g. for *TPMT* gene), the absence of expected association may reflect limited statistical power rather than true absence of effect. Larger, multicenter studies integrating *ITPA* into multigene pharmacogenetic models will be essential to clarify its independent clinical utility and to determine whether genotype-guided 6-MP dose optimization improves toxicity management without compromising treatment efficacy. However, the observed interindividual variability in treatment response suggests that a limited candidate-gene approach may not fully capture the complexity of pharmacogenetic influences. This highlights the need for broader analyses, such as next-generation sequencing, incorporating a wider spectrum of pharmacogenetic markers, including genes relevant to other drugs administered concomitantly during ALL therapy. In addition to genetic determinants, variability in MTX pharmacokinetics and 6-MP tolerance is influenced by a range of clinical factors. These include hydration status, renal and hepatic function, infection status, concomitant medications, and the timing of toxicity assessments, all of which can significantly affect drug clearance and toxicity profiles. Owing to the retrospective nature of this study, comprehensive and standardized data on these parameters were not consistently available and therefore could not be systematically included in the analysis. This represents an important limitation, as such factors may confound or modify the observed associations between candidate genetic variants and treatment outcomes, potentially masking true genetic effects. Future prospective studies integrating detailed clinical and pharmacological data alongside genetic profiling are warranted to enable a more accurate assessment of determinants of drug response and toxicity.

The PRS included *TPMT* rs1142345 and *ITPA* rs1127354, variants previously implicated in thiopurine metabolism. The absence of statistically significant associations may reflect the multifactorial nature of MTX toxicity and thiopurine intolerance, where genetic effects interact with clinical and treatment-related factors. Given the small sample size and heterogeneity of the study population, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

## Conclusions

This study suggests that germline variations in thiopurine-related pharmacogenes contribute to interindividual differences in antimetabolite tolerance in Croatian pediatric patients with ALLs. Within the limitations of the small cohort size, *ITPA* rs1127354 was associated with lower tolerated 6-MP doses, while regulatory variation in *NUDT15* rs61973267 showed a consistent, though not statistically significant, effect on dose tolerance. In contrast, single variants in folate pathway and transporter genes showed limited associations with MTX pharmacokinetics and toxicity, with only a trend toward reduced oral mucositis observed for *SLCO1B1* rs4149056.

PRS analyses indicated a borderline association between cumulative genetic burden and the need for 6-MP dose reduction, supporting the value of integrative, multigene approaches over single-variant testing. Also, our finding highlight population-specific differences in allele frequencies compared with European reference population, which may affect the interpretation and clinical applicability of pharmacogenetic markers and limit the applicability of findings to other populations. Overall, these findings underscore the complexity of antimetabolite pharmacogenetics and highlight the need for larger, prospective studies to validate population-specific markers and refine personalized dosing strategies in pediatric ALL.
