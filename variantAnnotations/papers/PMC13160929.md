# Effect of Individual and Combined Cytochrome P450 Activity Scores on Symptom Improvement and Side Effects in Youth Treated With Sertraline

## Abstract

Sertraline is commonly used to treat mental health conditions in youth. However, its tolerability and efficacy vary between individuals, partly due to interindividual differences in drug metabolism. This study assessed the individual and combined impact of genetic variation in cytochrome P450 drug‐metabolizing enzymes on patient‐reported side effects and symptom improvement in 347 youth, aged 6–24, receiving sertraline treatment. Participants reported sertraline dose, duration, adherence, concomitant medications, side effects, and symptom improvement. DNA was extracted from saliva samples and genotyped for CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4. Individual activity scores and a weighted combinatorial CYP450 activity score were created. Binomial logistic regression models were used to assess the impact individual and combined CYP450 activity scores had on patient‐reported side effects and symptom improvement, both with and without correction for phenoconversion. After adjusting for phenoconversion, other CYP450 activity scores, and covariates, higher activity scores for CYP2D6 (OR = 0.288, 95% CI = 0.091–0.913) and CYP3A4 (OR = 0.002, 95% CI = 0.001–0.375) were nominally associated with reduced odds of symptom improvement. A higher combined CYP450 activity score was associated with reduced odds of symptom improvement (OR = 0.060, 95% CI = 0.004–0.797) and side effects (OR = 0.097, 95% CI = 0.010–0.925). These findings suggest that a combined CYP450 activity score may serve as a marker of symptom improvement and side effects in youth, but validation using sertraline/desmethylsertraline concentrations is required. If validated, these results may inform updates to pharmacogenetic prescribing guidelines for sertraline and support more personalized sertraline use in youth.

Keywords: adolescents, children, emerging adults, pharmacogenetics, psychiatry, sertraline

## 1. Introduction

Sertraline is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the management of adult major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive‐compulsive disorder (OCD), posttraumatic stress disorder, social anxiety disorder, panic disorder, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder [[1](#cts70590-bib-0001)]. In children and adolescent populations, sertraline is FDA approved for OCD (ages 6–17), but is frequently used off‐label and included in clinical practice guidelines for the management of MDD and anxiety disorders [[2](#cts70590-bib-0002), [3](#cts70590-bib-0003)]. Clinical trials in youth and adults indicate that sertraline is an effective treatment for managing MDD and OCD symptoms [[4](#cts70590-bib-0004), [5](#cts70590-bib-0005)]. However, 33%–79% of children and adolescents treated with sertraline do not achieve a complete therapeutic response [[4](#cts70590-bib-0004), [5](#cts70590-bib-0005), [6](#cts70590-bib-0006)]. Furthermore, up to 75% of sertraline users experience intolerable adverse effects from treatment [[4](#cts70590-bib-0004), [7](#cts70590-bib-0007)]. Individual differences in sertraline metabolism may contribute to the variability observed in drug response and tolerability.

Sertraline clearance is a complex process mediated by multiple cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, with no single enzyme contributing more than 25%–35% of the total metabolism (Figure [S1](#cts70590-supitem-0001)) [[8](#cts70590-bib-0008), [9](#cts70590-bib-0009)]. Key enzymes in the sertraline metabolic pathway include CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4—each capable of demethylating sertraline into desmethylsertraline, the primary metabolite with little to no therapeutic effect [[8](#cts70590-bib-0008), [10](#cts70590-bib-0010)]. An in vitro study using human liver microsomes estimated the relative contributions of CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 to sertraline metabolism—after normalization to their relative in vivo abundances—to be approximately 1.6%, 27%, 24%, 24%, and 24%, respectively, of total CYP‐mediated sertraline metabolism [[9](#cts70590-bib-0009)]. Previous research has shown that CYP2C19 and CYP2B6 poor metabolizers have reduced plasma concentrations of desmethylsertraline [[11](#cts70590-bib-0011), [12](#cts70590-bib-0012), [13](#cts70590-bib-0013)]. In contrast, reduced activity of CYP2D6 and CYP2C9 has not been associated with lower desmethylsertraline plasma levels [[11](#cts70590-bib-0011), [12](#cts70590-bib-0012), [14](#cts70590-bib-0014)]. Previous work has evaluated a combinatorial pharmacogenomic algorithm involving CYP2C19, CYP2B6, and CYP3A4 in relation to sertraline plasma concentrations [[15](#cts70590-bib-0015)].

To our knowledge, there are no studies that have examined the role of combined CYP enzyme activity on symptom improvement or side effects of sertraline treatment. Moreover, combinatorial pharmacogenomic testing has demonstrated greater potential effectiveness than single‐gene approaches when assessing psychotropic treatment outcomes in depression [[16](#cts70590-bib-0016), [17](#cts70590-bib-0017)]. Moreover, clinical trials examining the impact of sertraline metabolism on adverse events observed a trend where *CYP2C19*, *CYP2B6*, and *CYP2D6* poor or intermediate metabolizers experienced more adverse events [[18](#cts70590-bib-0018)]. Furthermore, *CYP2C19* poor metabolizers have been associated with increased side effects from sertraline treatment and antidepressant switching rates compared to *CYP2C19* normal and intermediate metabolizers [[12](#cts70590-bib-0012), [19](#cts70590-bib-0019), [20](#cts70590-bib-0020)]. Other research has focused on *CYP2C19* genetic variation, leaving a significant gap in the research with no studies considering the entire sertraline metabolic pathway.

To address this knowledge gap, we investigated whether the genotype‐predicted activity scores of *CYP2B6*, *CYP2C19*, *CYP2C9*, *CYP2D6*, and *CYP3A4* were associated with patient‐reported symptom improvement and side effects in youth being treated with sertraline for a mental health condition. We hypothesized that increased activity scores would be associated with reduced odds of reporting both side effects and symptom improvement.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1. Participants

Participants were enrolled in the Pharmacogenetic‐Supported Prescribing in Kids (PGx‐SParK) study, an ongoing mirror‐image trial examining the effectiveness of pharmacogenetic testing in Western Canada ([NCT04797364](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04797364)). Participants were eligible for the PGx‐SParK study if they: (1) were aged 6–24 years; (2) were receiving care from a physician who would consider using pharmacogenetic testing results to adjust pharmacotherapy; (3) were planning on starting or changing psychiatric medications; and (4) had no history of bone marrow or liver transplant. Participants were selected for the current analysis if they had a history of sertraline use at the time of entry into the PGx‐SParK study. Concomitant use of other medications was allowed and documented. All participants gave informed consent or assent, with parental consent obtained as necessary, in accordance with the Canadian Tri‐Council Policy Statement. All procedures were conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB20‐0900).

### 2.2. Measures

The current study utilized assessments completed during the baseline visit of the PGx‐SParK study. The Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) application was used to gather participants' demographic information (age, sex, biogeographical group) along with prescribed and over‐the‐counter medications, as well as cannabidiol or cannabis use [[21](#cts70590-bib-0021)]. For all the medications, participants were asked to report: daily dosages, start and end dates, adherence (i.e., did they forget to take this medication), side effects, and symptom improvement. Symptom improvement was collected after study enrollment using a single ordinal item adapted from the Clinical Global Impression–Improvement scale (CGI‐I) that asked participants to indicate how much improvement in symptoms they had experienced from sertraline treatment [[22](#cts70590-bib-0022)]. Symptom improvement was recorded as present (i.e., slight, partial, or vast improvement) or not present (i.e., no or worse improvement). Side effects and adherence were each assessed by a single question with a binary (yes/no) response format.

### 2.3. Pharmacogenetic Testing and Activity Score Calculations

Genomic DNA was extracted from saliva samples collected using Oragene OG‐600 kits (DNA Genotek, Ottawa). Extracted DNA was genotyped for *CYP2D6*, *CYP2B6*, *CYP2C9*, *CYP2C19*, and *CYP3A4* by Alberta Precision Laboratories using an Agena Veridose v1.0 custom assay and the *CYP2D6* Veridose v.1.0 copy number variation assay (Table [S1](#cts70590-supitem-0001)).

Activity scores were calculated for *CYP2D6* using the standardized definitions established jointly by Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) and the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG), incorporating the recent reduction in the activity values for *9 and *41 from 0.50 to 0.25 [[23](#cts70590-bib-0023)]. *CYP2C9* activity scores were determined according to associated PharmGKB diplotype–phenotype tables [[24](#cts70590-bib-0024)]. For the *CYP2B6* and *CYP2C19* genes, activity values were assigned based on allele function defined by the Pharmacogene Variation Consortium (PharmVar): no function, decreased function, normal function, and increased function alleles were assigned scores of 0.00, 0.50, 1.00, and 1.50, respectively [[25](#cts70590-bib-0025)]. For *CYP3A4*, activity values were similarly determined based upon no‐function, decreased function, and normal function alleles that were assigned scores of 0.00, 0.50, and 1.00, respectively, defined by the DPWG definitions curated in the Pharmacogenomics Knowledge Base [[26](#cts70590-bib-0026)]. The activity score for each gene was calculated by summing the activity values corresponding to the participant's diplotype.

A combined activity score was calculated based on data from an in vitro study of sertraline metabolism in human liver microsomes, corrected for in vivo abundance [[9](#cts70590-bib-0009)]. The study reported contributions of 1.6%, 27%, 24%, 24%, and 24% for CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4, respectively [[9](#cts70590-bib-0009)]. Prior to calculating the combined activity score, each participant's activity score for each gene was normalized by dividing the observed activity score (numerator) by the maximum possible activity score (denominator). The normalized activity scores were then weighted according to their respective contributions to sertraline metabolism and summed to generate the combined activity score. The resulting combined activity score could range from 0 to 1, with 0 suggesting no/limited CYP450 metabolic capacity and 1 indicating full/nearly full capacity to metabolize sertraline.

### 2.4. Phenoconversion Adjustment

For participants taking concomitant strong or moderate inhibitors and inducers of *CYP2B6*, *CYP2C9*, *CYP2C19*, *CYP2D6*, and *CYP3A4*, assigned activity scores were multiplied by 0.0, 0.5, and 1.5, respectively. No phenotype changes were made for individuals concurrently taking both an inhibitor and an inducer of the same enzyme. Participants taking multiple moderate inhibitors of the same enzyme were classified as using a strong inhibitor and subsequently categorized as poor metabolizers (i.e., score = 0). The classification of inhibitors and inducers was based on the “Flockhart Table” and Sequence2Script [[27](#cts70590-bib-0027), [28](#cts70590-bib-0028)]. Regarding cannabidiol, the FDA product label indicates that its concurrent use with CYP2C19 substrates (e.g., clobazam) can increase the substrate's plasma concentrations up to threefold [[29](#cts70590-bib-0029)]. Consequently, cannabidiol and cannabis were classified as moderate CYP2C19 inhibitors, in accordance with Flockhart Table criteria [[27](#cts70590-bib-0027)].

### 2.5. Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted using Jamovi software (version 2.3.21). Binomial logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the effects of individual and combined *CYP450* activity scores, both with and without adjustment for phenoconversion, on symptom improvement and side effects. For the individual *CYP450* activity score model, all *CYP450* activity scores were included along with covariates: age, sex, dose, treatment duration, and adherence. For the combined activity score model, only the covariates were included. Participants missing genotype or treatment response data were excluded. Outliers were defined as marginal means values exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range and were removed in a sensitivity analysis to assess whether they influenced the results of the primary analyses. When evaluating the effects of individual CYP450 activity scores, a Bonferroni adjustment (alpha = 0.01) was used to account for multiple comparisons.

## 3. Results

A total of 347 participants were included in the analysis (Table [S2](#cts70590-supitem-0001)). The mean age was 14.6 years (median = 15, IQR = 13.0–17.0), 64% were female, and 76% self‐identified as being part of the European biogeographical group. The mean daily dose of sertraline reported by participants was 98.8 mg (median = 100, IQR = 50–150) for an average duration of 576 days (median = 189, IQR = 61–522). Over one third (38.5%) reported forgetting to regularly take their medication. About one‐quarter (22%) of participants reported concomitantly taking a CYP450 inhibitor or inducer (Table [S3](#cts70590-supitem-0001)). Detected diplotype frequencies and associated activity scores are reported for each of the *CYP450* genes studied in Table [S4](#cts70590-supitem-0001).

### 3.1. Symptom Improvement

One‐third (33.0%) of the participants reported experiencing no symptom improvement from sertraline treatment. After correcting for phenoconversion, age, sex, dose, duration, adherence, and activity scores of other CYPs, *CYP2D6* (OR = 0.287, 95% CI = 0.091–0.910, *p* = 0.034) and *CYP3A4* (OR = 0.002, 95% CI = 0.001–0.371, *p* = 0.019) activity was nominally associated with a decrease in the odds of symptom improvement (Figure [1](#cts70590-fig-0001)). The observed distribution of the combined activity score spanned from 0.398 to 0.961 and was significantly associated with a decrease in the odds of symptom improvement (OR = 0.060, 95% CI = 0.004–0.797, *p* = 0.033), following correction for phenoconversion, age, sex, dose, duration, and adherence (Figure [2A](#cts70590-fig-0002), Table [S5](#cts70590-supitem-0001)). Similar results were observed for individual and combined activity scores when not adjusting for phenoconversion (Table [S6](#cts70590-supitem-0001)). No outliers were detected.

### 3.2. Side Effects

Approximately half (49.7%) of participants reported experiencing side effects from sertraline treatment. Binomial logistic regression analysis revealed that no individual gene activity scores were significantly associated with the odds of reporting side effects after adjusting for phenoconversion, age, sex, dose, duration, and adherence (Figure [1](#cts70590-fig-0001)). However, after covariate and phenoconversion adjustment, the combined activity score (Figure [2](#cts70590-fig-0002), Table [S7](#cts70590-supitem-0001)) was significantly associated with reduced odds of reporting side effects (OR = 0.101, 95% CI = 0.011–0.966, *p* = 0.047). Similarly, the results of the primary analyses remained significant without correction for phenoconversion (OR = 0.083, 95% CI = 0.008–0.888, *p* = 0.040; Table [S8](#cts70590-supitem-0001)) as well as after the removal of two outliers (OR = 0.101, 95% CI = 0.011–0.966, *p* = 0.047).

## 4. Discussion

Our findings suggest a combined *CYP450* activity score may be useful in determining the odds of symptom improvement and side effects in a youth population receiving sertraline treatment.

We found that greater capacity to metabolize sertraline, reflected by a higher combined *CYP450* activity score, was associated with lower odds of reporting both symptom improvement and side effects. This pattern aligns with CPIC recommendations and the notion that participants with higher activity scores are less likely to be exposed to sufficient sertraline concentrations to experience symptom improvement or side effects from treatment [[19](#cts70590-bib-0019)].

Conversely, individuals with reduced capacity to metabolize sertraline had higher odds of reporting symptom improvement and side effects. While previous research and CPIC guidance support the association between reduced metabolic activity and increased risk of adverse events—often necessitating lower starting doses or slower titration schedules—they do not support improved clinical response among those with reduced metabolism [[18](#cts70590-bib-0018), [20](#cts70590-bib-0020), [29](#cts70590-bib-0029)]. In fact, two studies have shown that lower CYP2C19 activity (i.e., IMs and PMs) is associated with higher medication switch rates compared to NMs [[12](#cts70590-bib-0012), [30](#cts70590-bib-0030)].

This discrepancy may partly reflect our use of a combined activity score. Although previous work has shown that commercial combinatorial algorithms can outperform single‐gene approaches, these algorithms have generally lacked transparency regarding gene weighting and have not examined sertraline response specifically [[15](#cts70590-bib-0015), [16](#cts70590-bib-0016), [17](#cts70590-bib-0017)]. Further research using transparent, biologically informed combined CYP activity scores, such as that used in this study, will be important to clarify these relationships.

This study has several notable strengths. First, we implemented a transparent and reproducible algorithm, enabling future studies to apply the same methodology in different populations and independently replicate our findings. Second, the low participant burden enhances scalability relative to more invasive approaches such as blood sampling, intensive questionnaires, or therapeutic drug monitoring. This feature is particularly important for real‐world implementation. Lastly, the metabolic activity and weighting of each individual *CYP450* enzyme was based on in vitro human liver microsomes results which are corrected based on in vivo *relative abundance*. Although these estimates may not perfectly reflect true in vivo contributions, the weights represent the current state of knowledge and can be adjusted as new data emerges.

This study also has several limitations. First, outcomes were patient‐reported, and results may differ in studies using clinician‐rated or other validated assessment tools. Second, our side‐effect and adherence measures did not allow for detailed characterization of side‐effect severity or subtype, nor did they permit precise quantification of adherence, which may have obscured associations with the combined CYP450 activity score. Third, we did not collect blood samples to measure sertraline or its metabolite concentrations. Consequently, we were unable to evaluate associations among the combined CYP450 activity score, drug or metabolite exposure, and treatment outcomes. The combined activity score should therefore be interpreted as a proxy rather than a substitute for therapeutic drug monitoring. However, genotype‐based activity scores are widely used and align with CPIC‐ and PharmVar‐endorsed approaches for estimating enzyme function when pharmacokinetic sampling is not available. As such, our findings remain relevant for real‐world pharmacogenetic implementation, where plasma monitoring is rarely performed and clinical decisions typically rely on genotype‐informed predictors. Finally, our analysis focused exclusively on CYP450 genetic variation and its role in the conversion of sertraline to desmethylsertraline. Other metabolic pathways may also be relevant. For example, the UGT system contributes to sertraline metabolism through formation of sertraline carbamoyl‐O‐glucuronide; however, to our knowledge, there is no established association between UGT‐mediated metabolism and sertraline treatment outcomes [[10](#cts70590-bib-0010), [31](#cts70590-bib-0031)]. Future studies incorporating both CYP450 and UGT pathways may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic influences on sertraline metabolism and clinical response.

In summary, a combined *CYP450* activity score may have value in determining an individual's capacity to metabolize sertraline and determine their odds of symptom improvement and side effects in a youth population. In clinical terms, a lower combined CYP450 activity score may identify patients who metabolize sertraline more slowly and could be at higher risk for side effects, while higher scores may suggest faster metabolism and possible reduced efficacy. Although promising, our findings should be independently confirmed in larger and more diverse populations before clinical implementation is considered.
