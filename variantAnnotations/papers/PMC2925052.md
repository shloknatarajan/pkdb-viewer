# A genetic determinant of the striatal dopamine response to alcohol in men

## Abstract

Excessive alcohol use, a major cause of morbidity and mortality, is less well understood than other addictive disorders. Dopamine release in ventral striatum is a common element of drug reward, but alcohol has an unusually complex pharmacology, and humans vary greatly in their alcohol responses. This variation is related to genetic susceptibility for alcoholism, which contributes more than half of alcoholism risk. Here, we report that a functional OPRM1 A118G polymorphism is a major determinant of striatal dopamine responses to alcohol. Social drinkers recruited based on OPRM1 genotype were challenged in separate sessions with alcohol and placebo under pharmacokinetically controlled conditions, and examined for striatal dopamine release using positron emission tomography and [11C]-raclopride displacement. A striatal dopamine response to alcohol was restricted to carriers of the minor 118G allele. To directly establish the causal role of OPRM1 A118G variation, we generated two humanized mouse lines, carrying the respective human sequence variant. Brain microdialysis showed a four-fold greater peak dopamine response to an alcohol challenge in h/mOPRM1-118GG than in h/mOPRM1-118AA mice. OPRM1 A118G variation is a genetic determinant of dopamine responses to alcohol, a mechanism by which it likely modulates alcohol reward.

Keywords: alcohol, dopamine, opioids, reward, polymorphism, positron emission tomography

## INTRODUCTION

Alcohol use is a major cause of disability worldwide [1](#R1), but available treatments have limited efficacy [2](#R2). Development of novel, mechanism-based pharmacotherapies will require an improved understanding of the neurobiology that underlies addictive properties of alcohol [3](#R3). Compared to other addictive drugs, alcohol has a complex pharmacology. Sedative, ataxic and anxiolytic alcohol effects are primarily mediated through GABA- and glutamate signaling. In contrast, rewarding properties of alcohol such as euphoria and psychomotor stimulation are thought to involve endogenous opioids and mesolimbic dopamine (DA). In response to alcohol, µ-opioid receptor (*OPRM1*) activation in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) suppresses the activity of inhibitory GABA-ergic interneurons, resulting in disinhibition of DA-neurons and DA release from their terminals in the ventral striatum [4](#R4). Accordingly, OPRM1 blockade is a treatment for alcohol dependence [3](#R3).

Humans vary substantially in their alcohol responses, and this variability is related to genetic susceptibility for alcohol use disorders, which accounts for more than half the disease risk in this condition [5](#R5)^,^[6](#R6). Striatal DA-release is a common element of drug reward [7](#R7), and alcohol-induced DA release has been shown both in rodents [8](#R8)^,^[9](#R9) and humans [10](#R10). There is, however, marked individual variation in alcohol-induced behavioral responses thought to be related to DA activation, such as psychomotor stimulation. Functional variation in opioid genes may contribute to this variation by modulating alcohol-induced DA release. A functional *OPRM1* 118G variant [11](#R11) confers enhanced subjective alcohol responses [12](#R12), and a functionally equivalent rhesus macaque 77G variant [13](#R13) confers enhanced alcohol-induced psychomotor stimulation [14](#R14). Despite this, it remains controversial whether the *OPRM1* A118G polymorphism is a risk factor for alcohol use disorders [15](#R15).

Here, we asked whether *OPRM1* A118G modulates striatal DA release in response to alcohol. We first carried out a human positron emission tomography (PET) study using displacement of the DA-D_2_ ligand [^11^C]-raclopride, an indirect measure of endogenous DA release [16](#R16), and found that 118G carriers had a markedly more vigorous striatal DA response to alcohol compared to subjects homozygous for the major 118A allele. In parallel, we isolated the influence of *OPRM1* A118G variation by generating humanized mice, carrying the human exon 1 of the *OPRM1* gene, either as the major 118A allele, or with the 118G SNP introduced through site directed mutagenesis. Direct microdialysis measures of the response to a rewarding dose of alcohol showed a four-fold higher peak DA response to the alcohol challenge.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

Additional details for all methods are provided in [Supplementary Information](#SD1).

### PET-study

Subjects were healthy male social drinkers, consuming less than 20 standard drinks per week. They were non-smokers who had never smoked, or had quit at least a year prior to enrolling in the study. Because the ventral striatum shrinks an average of 3.6 to 3.7% per decade [17](#R17), the age range was restricted to 21–45 years in order to reduce age-related variability. Furthermore, because rhesus macaque *OPRM1* genotype predominantly modulates psychomotor responses to alcohol in males [14](#R14), and because recent human data indicate only borderline detectable striatal DA release in response to alcohol in females [18](#R18), the study population was restricted to males. Subject demographics are provided in [table S1](#SD1). Allele frequencies of *OPRM1* 118G in Caucasian populations are around 15% [19](#R19), and 118G homozygotes are rare. Subjects were recruited into two approximately equally sized groups: 1) individuals homozygous for the major 118A allele (118AA genotype; n=16) of the OPRM1 polymorphism; 2) individuals carrying one or two copies of the variant 118G allele (118AG or 118GG genotype; because no subject with the rare 118GG genotype was in fact found, this group is hereafter called 118AG; n=12) of the *OPRM1* polymorphism. Genotyping was on DNA from whole blood. SNP rs1799971 (A/G) was genotyped using the assay-on-demand (assay ID: C_8950074_1) from Applied Biosystems (Foster City, CA, USA). The alleles were discriminated by post-PCR plate read on ABI PRISM® 7900HT Sequence Detector System (Applied Biosystems).

Because blood alcohol concentrations (BACs) after oral alcohol administration are highly variable [20](#R20), we used a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model, and intravenous (i.v.) alcohol infusion to achieve highly standardized brain alcohol exposure [21](#R21) successfully used in prior studies [20](#R20)^,^[22](#R22)^–^[27](#R27). Subjects underwent three infusion sessions, on separate days, separated by approximately 1 week.

A baseline session was carried out outside the PET scanner to ensure that subjects tolerated the alcohol infusion without experiencing nausea or marked sedation, and allow them to habituate to the procedure. All subjects received i.v. alcohol that raised BACs from 0 to 80 mg/dl within 15 min. This is a peak BAC commonly achieved by social drinkers, although the infusion procedure produced this level at a faster rate than is typically achieved with oral alcohol use. The exposure profile was chosen based on prior findings showing that it robustly activates the ventral striatum in a manner that can be detected by functional brain imaging [27](#R27). Subjective response to alcohol was measured using a modified Drug Effects Questionnaire [DEQ; [28](#R28)] obtained before the beginning of the infusion and every 7.5 min minutes during the infusion. Breath alcohol concentrations (BrAC) were monitored every 7.5 min during the infusion, and every 30 min thereafter. Blood samples for BACs were collected before starting the infusion, and then at 15, 30 and 45 min.

In the second and third sessions, subjects received infusions of alcohol or placebo in counter-balanced order while undergoing a PET scan with [^11^C]-raclopride (GE Advance Tomograph, Waukesha, WI). An 8-min transmission scan was first acquired with 2 rotating rod sources for the purpose of attenuation correction. Next, a 10 mCi dose of [^15^O]-water was administered over 1 min through an IV line to assess regional perfusion and co-register the PET image with a brain MRI obtained from the subject during a separate visit. Following this, the infusion (6% v/v ethanol or normal saline) was started. At 15 min after the start of the infusion, a 12 mCi dose of [^11^C]-raclopride was administered over 1 min. Dynamic PET scans began with the [^11^C]-raclopride injection and continued for 90 min. A schematic of the PET sessions is shown in [Figure 1D](#F1).

PET Analysis was based on a Region of Interest (ROI) methodology. A structural MR scan was co-registered with the subject’s PET scans. ROIs were drawn in the primary areas of post-synaptic dopamine release, namely the anterior ventral striatum, posterior ventral striatum, caudate and putamen ([figure S1](#SD1)). The anterior ventral striatum was defined as striatum anterior and inferior to the anterior commisure. The posterior ventral striatum was defined as striatum posterior and inferior to the anterior commisure. The PET data from the placebo session were used as a measure of baseline raclopride binding potential, using the simplified reference tissue model [29](#R29). Percent change in binding potential (%ΔBP) following alcohol was calculated as (BP_placebo_ - BP_alcohol_)/( BP_placebo_). Reduction in raclopride binding is attributed to competition with dopamine endogenously released by the alcohol challenge, and the percent change in binding potential has been shown to be proportional to the magnitude of dopamine release [16](#R16).

PET data were examined for homogeneity of variance and analyzed using general linear models (GLM, Statistica 6.0, StatSoft, Tulsa, OK). For the %ΔBP, a repeated measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, with genotype and testing sequence as between subjects variables, region of interest as the within subjects variable, and drinking frequency (measured by the Lifetime Drinking History questionnaire [30](#R30)) as the covariate. Subjective responses to alcohol were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with genotype and testing sequence as between subjects variables and time point as the within subjects variable. *Post hoc* comparisons were conducted using Newman-Kuel tests. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta[2](#R2) from Statistica, and Cohen’s f calculated using GPower 3.1.0 (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany).

### Humanized mouse lines

At the *OPRM1* locus, linkage disequilibrium (LD) exists between *OPRM1* A118G and other markers [31](#R31). We therefore generated two humanized mouse lines, where the *OPRM1* exon 1 was replaced by the corresponding human sequence. One of the lines, h/mOPRM-118AA, is homozygous for the major human 118A allele. For the other (h/mOPRM1–118GG), site directed mutagenesis was used to introduce a G in position 118. Thus, the lines are genetically identical, with the exception of the A → G substitution ([Figure 2A–C](#F2)). The lines are on a C57BL/6 background, where robust voluntary alcohol consumption is observed [32](#R32).

### Receptor binding studies

Membrane binding was assessed in preparations from CHO-K1 cells transfected with OPRM1 receptor cDNA and incubated with 2nM [^3^H]-D-Ala^2^, N-MePhe^4^, Gly-ol]-enkephalin (DAMGO; 37Ci/mmol; NEN, Boston, MA) and nonradioactive naloxone, morphine, and β-endorphin (β-End) ranging from 0.01nM to 1000 nM. The mixture was incubated at room temperature, filtered through Whatman GF/B filters (FP-100, Brandel), washed with ice-cold Tris-HCl, and assayed for radioactivity by liquid scintillation. Data were analyzed by nonlinear analysis using the Prism computer program (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Autoradiographic binding was studied on 20 µm coronal cryosections as described [34](#R34), using 5 nM [^3^H]DAMGO (48.9 Ci/mmol, NEN). Non-specific binding was determined from adjacent sections in the presence of the radioligand plus 10 µM naloxone. Radiolabeled, dried tissue sections and [^3^H]microscales (Amersham) were apposed to tritium-sensitive screens (Fujifilm imaging plates), and images were obtained using a Fujifilm BAS 5000 PhosphorImager.

Agonist-induced GTP[γ -^35^S] activity was evaluated as described [35](#R35) on 20 µm coronal sections, by incubating GTP[γ-^35^S] (0.04 nM) with 3 µM DAMGO in assay buffer at 25°C for 2 hr. Agonist studies were also performed in the presence of the antagonist naloxone to verify the receptor specificity of G-protein activation. Basal activity was assessed in each experiment with GDP in the absence of agonist, and nonspecific binding was evaluated in the presence of 10 µM unlabeled GTP[γS] without GDP. Slides were rinsed, dried and exposed to tritium-sensitive screens (Fujifilm imaging plates).

Images were analysed using MCID Image Analysis Software (Imaging Research Inc., UK). Regions of interest were defined by anatomical landmarks [36](#R36). Agonist-stimulated activity in brain sections was calculated by subtracting the optical density in basal sections (incubated with GDP alone) from that of agonist-stimulated sections; results were expressed as percentage of basal activity.

### Electrophysiology

Enzymatically dissociated trigeminal ganglion neurons were used. Ca2+ currents were recorded using whole-cell patch-clamping as described [37](#R37). Currents were acquired with the Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA), filtered, and cell membrane capacitance and pipette series resistance were electronically compensated. The concentration-response relationships were determined by sequential application of the respective agonist in increasing concentrations. Two to three different concentrations were used with each cell in order to minimize desensitization. The results were pooled and the concentration-response curves were fit to the Hill equation. Data and statistical analyses were performed with Igor Pro (Lake Oswego, OR) and Prism 4.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) software packages, respectively.

### Microdialysis

Under isofluorane anaesthesia, mice were implanted with microdialysis probes as previously described [38](#R38), aimed at the nucleus accumbens (NAc), and secured to the skull using a skull screw and cement. Histologically verified placement within the NAc for each subject is shown in [Figure 3A](#F3). During a 12h postsurgical recovery period, probes were slowly perfused with artificial cerebrospinal fluid. The perfusion flow rate was then increased to 0.6µL/min for 2h to reach an interstitial equilibrium before sample collection. Dialysate fractions were subsequently collected, during the dark cycle, at 10min intervals during a 60min baseline period and, after an injection of EtOH (2g/kg, ip), during a 120min post-injection period. Samples were analyzed for DA and 5-HT using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrochemical detection (HPLC-ECD).

## RESULTS

### Human PET-study

BACs at the end of the alcohol infusion during the PET session were, on average, within 1 mg/dl of the target level, confirming the high level of accuracy and precision of the method. There were no differences between BACs achieved in the AA and AG groups (ANOVA: F[1, 24]=0.287, p = 0.597). Baseline binding potential (BP) for [^11^C]-raclopride, as measured on the saline infusion session, also did not differ between genotypes (AA: 3535.8±107.8; AG: 3722.7±124.5 nCi/cc, mean±SEM; F[1,26]=1.3, p=0.27), making pre-existing differences in D2 receptor densities unlikely.

Across the four striatal subregions of interest (anterior ventral striatum, posterior ventral striatum, caudate and Putamen, [figure S1](#SD1)), the change in BP (ΔBP) attributable to the alcohol challenge [(BP_placebo_ - BP_alcohol_)/(BP_placebo_ )] differed markedly as a function of genotype, with AG subjects consistently showing signs of greater DA-release than AA individuals (main genotype effect: F[1,22]=9.3, p=0.006). Genotype accounted for 29.7% of the variance in response, measured as partial eta[2](#R2), translating into an effect size of Cohen's f=0.65, i.e. between “moderate” – “large”. There was also a significant genotype x region interaction (F[3,66]=3.8, p=0.01); accordingly, *post-hoc* tests showed that the main genotype effect was predominantly driven by significant differences in the anterior and posterior ventral striatum, regions most closely associated with drug reward ([Figure 1A–C](#F1)). The highest response to alcohol was observed in the anterior ventral striatum of the AG group, where ΔBP was approximately 9%. This is more than half of the response seen with amphetamine [16](#R16), a drug that potently enhances synaptic DA by acting on the DA transporter. This relative potency is in agreement with direct measures obtained by brain microdialysis in experimental animals [9](#R9)^,^[16](#R16). In contrast, AA subjects did not show an alcohol-induced displacement of the radioligand in any region. Inclusion of the BAC at 45 min as a covariate in the statistical analysis of the %ΔBP data did not influence the main effect of genotype (F[1,19]=9.7, p=0.0057). The BAC at 45 min *per se* showed a trend for a significant effect on the %ΔBP (F[1,19]=3.86, p=0.064).

Similar to other imaging genetic studies [39](#R39)^,^[40](#R40), subjective ratings showed higher variability than the biological measure. The only effect detected was a genotype x time interaction, with AG subjects reporting lower levels of intoxication than AA subjects at the later time points, suggestive of faster acute tolerance development (see [Supplementary Materials and Figure S2 E](#SD1)).

### Characterization of humanized receptors and mouse lines

When expressed in CHO cells, both humanized receptors showed binding of the classical OPRM1 selective ligand [^3^H]-DAMGO, displaced by the endogenous OPRM1 agonist β-End with expected low nanomolar IC_50_, and with no difference between genotypes (F[4,46]=1.3, p=0.3; [Fig. 2D](#F2)). Signaling, assessed as modulation of Ca^2+^ currents in acutely isolated superior trigeminal ganglion neurons, was also normal in response to both DAMGO and β-End, once again without any genotype differences (DAMGO: F[4,12]= 1.8, p=0.2; β-End: F[4,12]=1.2, p=0.4; [Fig 2E–F](#F2)). Finally, [^3^H]-DAMGO binding on mouse brain sections did not show genotype differences in receptor densities across a number of brain regions examined, including ventral and dorsal striatum and the ventral tegmental area (VTA; [Figure S5](#SD1)).

### DA responses to alcohol in humanized mouse lines

Microdialysis probes placement in ventral striatum / Nucleus Accumbens is shown in [Figure 3A](#F3). Baseline DA levels did not differ between genotypes (F[1,22]= 6.9 × 10^−6^, n.s.) and were 2.42±0.4 nM for h/mOPRM1–118AA and 2.43±0.6 nM for h/mOPRM1–118GG mice, respectively (n=12/group). Alcohol injection (2g/kg, a dose that robustly induces conditioned place preference in C57BL/6 mice) increased dialysate DA in both lines (main effect: F[6,132]=12.4; p<0.0001), However, response differed markedly between genotypes (main genotype effect: F[1,22]=7.2; p<0.05; genotype x time interaction: F[6,132]= 12.4; p<0.005; [Figure 3B](#F3)). Peak DA increase was nearly 4-fold greater in GG mice than in AA animals. The genotype effect was specific for DA release induced by alcohol; although alcohol also significantly increased 5-HT (main effect: F[6,132]=15.6; p<0.0001), this was not affected by genotype (main genotype effect: F[1,22]=3.6; n.s; genotype x time interaction: F[6,132]=0.92; n.s.; [Figure 3C](#F3)).

## DISCUSSION

Taken together, our data strongly support a causal role of the *OPRM1* 118G allele to confer a more vigorous DA response to alcohol in the ventral striatum. These findings may be related to observations of differential subjective alcohol effects as a function of *OPRM1* A118G genotype in humans [12](#R12), and of markedly increased psychomotor responses to alcohol in rhesus males carrying the functionally equivalent 77G variant [14](#R14). Interestingly, we also found that subjective feelings of intoxication over time differed as a function of genotype. Carriers of the 118G allele showed signs of rapid acute tolerance, similar to that previously described in men at genetic risk for alcoholism [41](#R41). The use of a reverse-translational approach allowed us to effectively isolate the influence of the human 118G variant from that of other polymorphisms with which it might be in LD.

Excessive activation of brain reward systems by alcohol in *OPRM1* 118G carriers might be expected to confer susceptibility for alcohol use disorders. Although we in fact found evidence in support of this notion in an ethnically homogenous Caucasian sample [42](#R42), others have not. One study found that other polymorphisms within the same haplotype block, but not A118G, were associated with diagnoses of substance dependence [31](#R31), while a meta-analysis of all available data for A118G was negative [43](#R43). This apparent discrepancy closely parallels recent findings with the 5-HT transporter gene promoter length polymorphism (5-HTTLPR). A robust role of 5-HTTLPR variation has been found using an imaging based endophenotype that is closely related to negative affect [39](#R39)^,^[44](#R44), and these findings are paralleled by experimental data in a non-human primate model [45](#R45)^,^[46](#R46). Nevertheless, findings from 5-HTTLPR association studies in depression have not held up on meta-analysis [47](#R47)^,^[48](#R48). The reasons for these discrepancies are presently unknown, much debated, and critical to resolve. One important possibility is that current diagnostic categories pool genetically heterogeneous phenocopies, as has specifically been proposed in depression [49](#R49). If so, the use of biological endophenotypes, such as in the present study, can potentially aid the dissection of these populations into genetically and pathophysiologically more homogenous categories.

Irrespectively of its possible role as a susceptibility factor, excessive reward-related DA-response to alcohol in carriers of *OPRM1* 118G might be of pharmacogenetic importance in treatment of excessive alcohol use. Indeed, two studies have found that the presence of the 118G allele was associated with a therapeutic response to the opioid antagonist naltrexone [50](#R50)^,^[51](#R51). Although one study failed to find such an effect [52](#R52), recent non-human primate data provide strong evidence for this notion under closely controlled experimental conditions [53](#R53). Our data are consistent with a selective role of endogenous opioids for alcohol reward in *OPRM1* 118G carriers, and provide a possible biological mechanism for the preferential naltrexone response in this population. However, the dissociation between objective measures of alcohol-induced DA-release and subjective reports of alcohol effects may argue against a simplistic view of alcohol-induced DA-release as an immediate mediator of drug reward. Instead, these data may be more compatible with a view of ventral striatal DA-activation as a signal predictive of alcohol reward, and perhaps involved in learning processes important for addiction [54](#R54). Of note, the level of alcohol-induce DA-activation in our study was overall lower than previously reported [10](#R10). A difference between the two studies is that alcohol administration was oral in the prior report, while we used intravenous administration. This may indicate that striatal DA response to alcohol is only in part driven by direct pharmacological actions of alcohol, while an additional component of the activation is evoked by smell or taste cues.

Our data demonstrate that the 118G allele, which encodes an amino acid substitution in the N-terminal extracellular loop of the receptor, confers a higher striatal DA-activation in response to alcohol regardless of whether it occurs in the context of other markers within its haplotype block or not. However, the molecular mechanism that mediates functional consequences of the 118G variant remains controversial. Initially described as a gain-of-function mutation due to increased affinity for β-End [11](#R11), *OPRM1* 118G has since been proposed to instead confer decreased expression in several systems, including human post-mortem brain tissue [55](#R55), and a mouse model where a mutation was introduced in a position corresponding to the human A118G marker [56](#R56). The latter model differs from that used in our study, in that it introduced the N → D substitution in the context of a murine N-terminal receptor protein sequence. In contrast, we generated mice expressing humanized receptors throughout this region by replacing all of exon 1, the main region of divergence between the two species ([Figure 2A](#F2)). Using this model, our present findings show that a key functional phenotype associated with the 118G variant, alcohol-induced striatal DA-release, is replicated across species in the absence of effects on receptor affinity, binding density, or signaling. A remaining possibility is altered oligomerization, a mechanism that is critical for opioid receptor desensitization, endocytosis, and re-insertion [57](#R57). *OPRM1* A118G modifies a glycosylation site in the N-terminal extracellular loop of the receptor, and could thereby influence receptor dimerization and trafficking.

Functionally equivalent *OPRM1* variants have evolved independently in two primate lineages, the rhesus macaque [13](#R13) and man [11](#R11). In both species, minor allele carriers are more sensitive to disruption of social attachment bonds [58](#R58)^,^[59](#R59). In rhesus, this is accompanied by bold - exploratory traits, while lower conscientiousness has been observed in human 118G carriers [13](#R13)^,^[58](#R58)^,^[60](#R60). These traits may have been advantageous in the evolutionary history of the respective species, and recent studies suggest that the 118G allele has been under positive selection [61](#R61). In contrast, the ability of these variants to modulate DA responses to alcohol is likely a coincidental evolutionary byproduct.

Identifying genetic sources of individual variation in complex behavioral phenotypes has proven challenging. Our findings support the notion that use of biological intermediate phenotypes and translational approaches can contribute important data to facilitate this difficult endeavor.

DETAILED MATERIALS AND METHODS

PET STUDY

Subjects

Subjects were community-recruited healthy male volunteers in good health, as determined by medical history, physical exam, electrocardiogram and laboratory tests, including blood chemistry, liver function tests, complete blood counts and urinalysis. They were social drinkers consuming an average of less than 20 alcoholic drinks per week, having normal liver enzymes and not seeking treatment for alcohol-related problems. Subjects were non-smokers who had never smoked or quit at least a year prior to enrolling in the study.

Subjects were recruited into two groups: 1) individuals homozygous for the major 118A allele (118AA genotype) of the OPRM1 polymorphism; 2) carriers of the variant 118G allele (118AG or 118GG genotype were both eligible, but no 118GG subjects were found, and the group is hereafter referred to as 118AG). Subject demographics are provided in table S1.

Subjects had 1) no current or prior history of any significant disease, including cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, hepatic, renal, endocrine, or reproductive disorders, or a positive hepatitis or HIV test at screening; 2) no use of any prescription or over-the-counter medications within two weeks prior to enrolling in the study; 3) no current Axis-I psychiatric illness, based on a structured clinical interview [SCID; 1]; 4) no current or prior history of any alcohol or drug dependence, or a positive result on urine screen for illicit drugs or breath alcohol test. A family history of substance use disorders was not exclusionary. Subjects were ineligible if they were non-drinkers (alcohol-naive individuals or current abstainers), or if they reported a history of &ldquo;facial flushing&rdquo; response to alcohol. Subjects who were also excluded if they were unable to undergo MRI scans of the brain due to head injury, claustrophobia or anxiety when confined to small spaces such as the magnet bore, or due to the presence of metallic implants in their bodies.

All the procedures employed were approved by the NIH Combined NeuroScience Institutional Review Board and the NIH Radiation Safety Committee.

Genotyping

Genomic DNAs were isolated from human whole blood using Maxwell&reg; 16 DNA purification kit (Promega). The SNP of rs1799971 (A/G) was genotyped using the assay-on-demand (assay ID: C_8950074_1) from Applied Biosystems (Foster City, CA, USA). The assay contained a pair of primers, a pair of fluorescent quenched probes (one for allele A; one for allele G) designed by Applied Biosystems with no sequence information. Primers and probes were mixed with TaqMan&reg; Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). 4.5 &mu;l of genomic DNA (2.5 ng/&mu;l) was transferred in triplicate to a 384-well plate, each well of which contained 5.5 &micro;L PCR mixtures. The PCR reaction was performed following a protocol provided by ABI. The allele was discriminated by post-PCR plate read on ABI PRISM&reg; 7900HT Sequence Detector System (Applied Biosystems). Data were processed using SDS 2.1 software (Applied Biosystems).

Study procedures

Experimental Design: Subjects underwent three infusion sessions on three separate days, separated by approximately 1 week. The first study session (baseline session) took place in the NIH Clinical Center Day Hospital, where subjects received an alcohol infusion to ensure that they tolerated the alcohol infusion without experiencing nausea or marked sedation and allow them to habituate to the procedures, prior to undergoing the infusions in the PET scanner during the second and third sessions. During each of the study sessions, subjects arrived at the NIH Clinical Center Day Hospital at approximately 7:00AM, following an overnight fast, and after abstaining from alcohol for at least 48 hours. After a negative breathalyzer test and urine drug screen was obtained, subjects received a light (~300 Kcal) breakfast. Two indwelling intravenous catheters were inserted, one into the ante-cubital vein of each arm using sterile technique. One catheter was used for the ethanol infusion and the other was used for blood sampling for measurement of blood alcohol concentration (BAC).

Baseline Session: In the first session, subjects received an infusion of alcohol to a target breath alcohol concentration (BrAC) of 80 mg/dl. Alcohol was infused as a 6%v/v solution in saline. The infusion rates were based on a physiologically-based pharmacokinetic model for alcohol 2, consisting of an exponentially increasing infusion rate from the start of the infusion until the target breath alcohol concentration (BrAC) of 80 mg/dl was reached at 15 min, followed by an exponentially decreasing infusion rate to maintain (or &ldquo;clamp&rdquo;) the BrAC at the target level. This infusion-rate profile was computed using individualized estimates of the model parameters, which are based on the participant&rsquo;s height, weight, age and sex. This method has been used successfully in several studies of the pharmacokinetics and pharmacological effects of alcohol in humans 3-9.

Serial breathalyzer measurements were obtained every 3-5 min from the start of the infusion using the Alcotest 7410 handheld breathalyzer (Drager Safety Inc., CO), to ensure that the BrACs were within 10 mg/dl of the target and to enable minor adjustments to the infusion rates to overcome errors in parameter estimation and experimental variability 2.  Subjective response to alcohol was measured using a modified Drug Effects Questionnaire [DEQ; 10] which was obtained before the beginning of the infusion and every 7.5 min minutes during the infusion. Blood samples (6 ml) were collected at four time-points: before the start of the infusion, and at 15, 30 and 45 min after the start of the infusion for measurement of blood alcohol concentrations (BAC) using gas chromatography with field-ionization detection (GC-FID). After the infusion was completed, BrAC measurements were taken every 30 min.  Participants were sent home in a taxi cab when their BrAC fell below 20 mg/dl.

PET scan sessions: In the second and third sessions, subjects received infusions of alcohol or placebo in counter-balanced order while undergoing a PET scan with [11C]-raclopride.  During these sessions, subjects were transported to the PET Center after placement of the IV catheters. Subjects were placed in the PET scanner (GE Advance Tomograph, Waukesha, WI), with the brain in the field of view. A thermoplastic head mold was used for each subject to gently immobilize the head while in the scanner and to ensure similar head positioning across scans. First, an 8-min transmission scan was acquired with 2 rotating rod sources for the purpose of attenuation correction.  Next, a 10 mCi dose of 15O-water was administered over 1 min through an IV line to assess regional perfusion and co-register the PET image with a brain MRI obtained from the subject during a separate visit.

Following this, the infusion (6% v/v ethanol or normal saline) was started. At 15 min after the start of the infusion, a 12 mCi dose of [11C]-raclopride was administered over 1 min. Dynamic PET scans began with the [11C]-raclopride injection and continued for 90 min.  Scans were acquired in 3D mode with septa removed producing a reconstructed resolution of 6 mm in all directions.

Subjective response to alcohol was measured using the DEQ, and blood samples (6 ml) were obtained at 0, 15, and 45 min after the start of the alcohol/placebo infusion. After the scan was completed, subjects were transported back to the Day Hospital unit where BrAC measurements were taken every 30 min. Subjects were sent home in a taxi cab when their BrAC fell below 20 mg/dl.

Data Analysis

PET Analysis: PET Analysis was based on a Region of Interest (ROI) methodology. A structural MR scan performed on each subject was co-registered with the subject&rsquo;s PET scans. ROIs were drawn in the primary areas of post-synaptic dopamine release, namely the anterior ventral striatum, posterior ventral striatum, caudate and putamen (figure S1). The anterior ventral striatum was defined as striatum anterior and inferior to the anterior commisure. The posterior ventral striatum was defined as striatum posterior and inferior to the anterior commisure. The&nbsp;anterior ventral stiatum ROI corresponds to the nucleus Accumbens while the posterior ventral&nbsp;striatum falls within the ventral Putamen. Rectangular ROIs (24 mm2) were used in the ventral&nbsp;striatum and circular ROIs were used in the dorsal striatum (6 mm2 in caudate n. and 24 mm2&nbsp;in Putamen). &nbsp;The size of the ROIs were selected so that approximately the same volume of striatal&nbsp;tissue could be sampled from each subject over all the slices in which striatum could be clearly&nbsp;identified. The ROIs were placed on T-1 MR images that had been registered with PET BP&nbsp;images, allowing us to visually confirm that every ROI fell within the boundaries&nbsp;of the striatum and within regions of high raclopride binding. ROI placements&nbsp;were done by an&nbsp;investigator (DWH) experienced in&nbsp;these procedures and who was&nbsp;unaware of each subject's&nbsp;genotype&nbsp;or treatment status.

The PET data from the placebo session was used as a measure of baseline raclopride binding potential, using the simplified reference tissue model 11. Percent change in binding potential (%&Delta;BP) following alcohol was calculated as (BPplacebo - BPalcohol)/( BPplacebo). Reduction in raclopride binding is attributed to competition with dopamine endogenously released by the alcohol challenge, and the percent change in binding potential has been shown to be proportional to the magnitude of dopamine release 12.

Statistical Analysis: Data were examined for homogeneity of variance and analyzed using general linear models (GLM, Statistica 6.0, StatSoft, Tulsa, OK). For the %&Delta;BP, a repeated  measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, with genotype and testing sequence (order) as between subjects variables, region of interest as the within subjects variable, and drinking frequency (days of consumption per month, as measured by the Lifetime Drinking History questionnaire 13) as covariate. The drinking measure was included to control for the possibility that individual differences in alcohol consumption confounded any genotypic effects on DA-levels. There was no significant contribution of order (main effect: F[1,24]=0.02, p=0.90), and only a trend level contribution from drinking frequency (main effect: F[1,24]=4.1, p=0.06), but their inclusion reduced the residual variance of the model, and they were therefore retained according to the pre-specified data analysis plan. Sensitivity analyses were carried out by dropping one or both of these factors from the model, but both the main effect of genotype as well as the genotype x region interaction remained significant in each case. Subjective responses to alcohol were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with genotype and testing sequence as between subjects variables and time point as the within subjects variable. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Newman-Keul tests. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta2 from Statistica, and Cohen&rsquo;s f calculated using GPower 3.1.0 (Franz Faul, Universit&auml;t Kiel, Germany).

Subjective responses to alcohol

Repeated measures ANOVA on the response to the question &ldquo;How intoxicated do you feel right now?&rdquo; on the DEQ obtained during the PET session showed a significant genotype X time interaction (ANOVA: F[7, 168]=2.2, p = 0.03). As figure S3 illustrates, subjects carrying the G allele appeared to show lower responses following the end of the ethanol infusion. This more rapid recovery toward baseline may be suggestive of greater acute tolerance development in the AG subjects, although the limited number of data points during the descending limb of the BAC-time curve precludes a more complete tolerance evaluation.

GENERATION OF HUMANIZED MOUSE LINES

To generate the OPRM1-118A line (C57BL/6-Oprm1tm1.1Arte), we replaced the coding part of exon 1 from the murine Oprm1 gene and additional 400 bp of intron 1 by corresponding human sequences followed by an FRT flanked selection marker to allow in vivo deletion of the selection marker. To generate the OPRM1-118G  line (C57BL/6-Oprm1tm2.1Arte) the human OPRM1 gene was modified by site directed mutagenesis resulting in the exchange of amino acid 40 of the ORMP1 protein from N to D.

Vector Construction

For the generation of both lines, mouse genomic fragments for the long arm and short arm were subcloned from a genomic C57BL/6 RP23 BAC library by ET cloning and recloned into a proprietary Artemis basic targeting vector harboring an FRT flanked Neo cassette for positive selection using homologous recombination in E.coli 14. To generate the OPRM1-118A line, a 783 bp fragment was amplified from the human OPRM1 gene with oligos OPRM1_15 (GGCTGGGGCGCCTGGAACCCGAACACTCTTGAGTGCTCTCAGTTACAGCCTACCGAGTCCGCAGCAAGCATTCAGAACCATGGACAGCAGCGCTGCCCCCACG and OPRM1_16 (GAGTTTTCGCTAGCTTTCTCTCCCATTATTCACAACTTACTC), containing the ATG for the ORF of exon 1 and additional 400 bp of intronic sequence  and  cloned by replacing the 756 bp NheI/ NarI fragment of the corresponding mouse genomic region. To generate the OPRM1-118G  line (Oprm1tm1199Arte), the 783 bp fragment that was amplified from the human OPRM1 gene was modified by site directed mutagenesis to change the Nucleotide 118 in the ORF from an A to a G with oligos OPRM1_29 (GTCCCACTTAGATGGCGACCTGTCCGACCCATGC) and OPRM1_30 (GCATGGGTCGGACAGGTCGCCATCTAAGTGGGAC).  This effectively changed the amino acid 40 of the ORMP1 protein from N to D. The confirmed sequence of the final targeting vector is available on request.

Mouse breeding and general health assessment

Mouse breeding was subsequently established at the NIAAA facility. Mice heterozygous for the respective human allele were bred to homozygosity to establish the h/mOPRM1-118AA and h/mOPRM1-118GG lines. For this, a separate PCR assay was established that simultaneously detects the presence of mouse and human sequence, respectively, allowing hetero- or homozygosity for the respective sequence to be determined (see Fig. 2C main paper). DNA was isolated from mouse tails using REDExtract-N-AmpTM tissue PCR kit (Sigma, St Lous, MO) according to the manufacturer&rsquo;s instructions, and PCR reactions were set up using 4 &mu;l of the extracts. The sense primers were Mouse-Oprm1(5&rsquo;-AGGGAACATCAGCGACTGCTC-3&rsquo;) and human-Oprm1 (5&rsquo;-TGCCCCCACGAACGCCAGCAATTG-3&rsquo;). The antisense primer, OPRM1-1236, targeted a sequence common to mouse and human (5&rsquo;GAAGTGGTGGGACTGTAGTC-3&rsquo;). A 585bp C57BL/6 genomic sequence that maps to chromosome 11 was used as PCR control. The primers for the control were 5&rsquo;-GAGACTCTGGCTACTCATCC-3&rsquo; and 5&rsquo;-CCTTCAGCAAGAGCTGGGGAC-3&rsquo;. Amplifications were performed on a DNA Engine Tetrad 2 Peltier Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) with one cycle at 95&deg;C followed by 30 cycles of 95&deg;C/30 sec, 60&deg;C/30 sec, 72&deg;C/1 min, and a final 5 minute extension at 72&deg;C. PCR products were separated on 1% agarose gels and visualized with ethidium bromide.

Mice heterozygous for the respective human allele were mated to generate lines of the respective genotype used in the experiments described. Determination of the respective h/mOPRM1-118 genotype was carried out using the PCR method described for the human PET study above. First generation h/mOPRM1-118AA and GG mice were assessed with regard to overall health and behavior using a testing battery according to 15. Animals were assessed for general appearance (body weight and physical appearance), behavior in novel environment (animal is placed in an empty cage for 3 min and assessed for behaviors: wild running, excessive grooming, freezing, and body posture), approach/avoidance (reaction to cotton-tip swab introduced into the empty cage), reflexes (the cage is moved rapidly from side-to-side and response observed; righting reflex; eye blink; whisker twitch), grip strength (hanging from wire lid), and general locomotor activity. A score between 0 (absent) and 2 (excessive) was given for each behavior assessed (except body weight, grip strength, and locomotor behavior for which absolute measures are available) and the score for each assessment group added. Both male and female offspring were tested (n=6-8 per group; age 12-14 weeks).

As Table S2 indicates, there were no major differences between male and female mice in the two lines, with the exception of some excessive running present in GG males and females upon introduction into the novel environment, and a modest but statistically significant increase in locomotion in h/mA118A males compared to h/m A118G males.

Male mice aged 13-16 weeks were used for the binding and signaling experiments, and male mice aged 8-16 weeks were used for the microdialysis experiments.

RECEPTOR BINDING STUDY

Transfected cDNAs

CHO-K1 cells were maintained in F-12K Medium (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) in a 37&nbsp;&deg;C incubator containing 5% CO2 water saturated atmosphere. Cells at 60-70% confluence were transfected with OPRM1 receptor cDNA (human, mouse, human-mouse-chimera and mutant cDNA) using LipofectAMINE reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were allowed to grow for an additional 24-36 h prior to use.

Membrane preparations

Membranes of CHO-K1 cells transfected with OPRM1 receptor were harvested when the cells grew on a 75cm2 flask until about 95% confluency. The media was discarded and the cells were rinsed with room temperature PBS. The PBS was discarded and 5mL of fresh PBS was added. The cells were detached using a single piece cell scraper. The cells and PBS were transferred to a 50mL falcon tube and centrifuged for 5 min at 1000 rpm at 4&deg;C. The  supernatant was discarded and the cell pellet was re-suspended in 5 mL of ice cold preparation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4). The cell pellet was homogenized using a sonicator for about 30 sec at intermediate settings (30%). An aliquot was taken for determination of total protein. The homogenate was centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm at 4C. The supernatant was transferred into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged for 20 min at 20000 rpm at 4C. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet re-suspended in 3 ml of assay buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 5 mM MgCl2,1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT pH 7.4, 1&mu;M bacitracin, 0.1% BSA).

Radioligand Binding Assay

Each binding incubation tube contained 150 &micro;g of membrane protein. The cell suspension (400-&micro;l) was incubated with 2nM concentrations of [3H] DAMGO (37&nbsp;Ci/mmol; NEN, Boston, MA) and nonradioactive naloxone, morphine, and beta-endorphin ranging from 0.01nM to 1000 nM in a final volume of 500 L in duplicate. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 90 min and then filtered through Whatman GF/B filter paper (FP-100, Brandel), washed three times with 4 mL of ice-cold 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4 using Brandel Harvesters. Radioactivity was assayed by liquid scintillation counting. Data were analyzed by nonlinear analysis using the Prism computer program (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

AUTORADIOGRAPHY STUDY

Tissues and Preparation

After decapitation, brains were rapidly removed and flash frozen in precooled isopentane on dry ice. Brains were kept frozen in sealed freezer bags at -70&ordm;C until they were sectioned. Frozen brains were placed in a Leica cryostat at -20&deg;C for approximately 1 h prior to sectioning.  20 &mu;m coronal sections were prepared and mounted on gelatin-subbed slides. Sections from selected regions were prepared from each brain. Mounted sections were stored at -70&deg;C until brain sections for all AA, GG and WT mice had been prepared.

GTP[&gamma;-35S] and [3H]DAMGO were obtained from New England Nuclear (Boston, MA). Unlabelled DAMGO, naloxone, GTP&gamma;S and GDP were purchased from Sigma Chemical (St. Louis, MO). All other reagents were of analytical grade and purchased from commercial sources.

Autoradiographic analysis

Determination of &micro;-opioid binding sites: The procedure as described by 16was used to label &micro;-opioid binding sites for autoradiographic analysis. Briefly, sections were incubated in 50 mM Tris buffer, pH 7.4, containing 5 nM [3H]DAMGO ([D-Ala2,N-Me-Phe4,Gly-ol5]enkephalin; specific activity 48.9 Ci/mmol, NEN) for 45 min at 25&deg;C. Non-specific binding was determined from adjacent sections in the presence of this radioligand plus 10 &micro;M naloxone. Following incubation, sections were transferred to ice-cold incubation buffer and given two 5-min washes followed by a brief rinse in ice-cold distilled water. Sections were then dried under a stream of cool dry air. Radiolabeled, dried tissue sections and [3H]microscales (Amersham) were apposed to tritium-sensitive screens (Fujifilm imaging plates) for seven days. The images were obtained by means of the Fujifilm BAS 5000 PhosphorImager.

Agonist-Stimulated GTP[&gamma; -35S] Binding Autoradiography: Slide mounted, 20 &mu;m coronal brain sections were incubated in assay buffer (50 mM Tris HCl/3 mM MgCl2/0.2 mM EGTA/100 mM NaCl, pH 7.7) at 25&deg;C for 10 min 17. Slides were then incubated with GDP in assay buffer at 25&deg;C for 15 min. Agonist-stimulated activity was determined by incubating GTP[&gamma;-35S] (0.04 nM) with 3 &micro;M DAMGO in assay buffer at 25&deg;C for 2 hr. Agonist studies were also performed in the presence of the antagonist naloxone to verify the receptor specificity of G-protein activation. Basal activity was assessed in each experiment with GDP in the absence of agonist, and nonspecific binding was evaluated in the presence of 10 &micro;M unlabeled GTP[&gamma;S]without GDP. Slides were then rinsed twice in ice-cold Tris buffer (50mM Tris HCl, pH 7.0 at 25&deg;C) and rinsed once briefly in deionized water.

Slides were dried overnight and exposed to tritium-sensitive screens (Fujifilm imaging plates). Phosphor imaging plates (Fujifilm for BAS-5000, Fujifilm Corp., Japan) were exposed for seven days to sections. Phosphor imager (Fujifilm Bio-Imaging Analyzer Systems, BAS-5000, Fujifilm Corp.) generated digital images were analysed using MCID Image Analysis Software (Imaging Research Inc., UK). Regions of interest were defined by anatomical landmarks as described in 18.

Agonist-stimulated activity in brain sections was calculated by subtracting the optical density in basal sections (incubated with GDP alone) from that of agonist-stimulated sections; results were expressed as percentage of basal activity.

ELECTROPHYSIOLOGY  STUDY

General Methods:

The experiments performed were approved by the Penn State College of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Single TG neurons were isolated from humanized AA and GG mice. Briefly, mice were initially anesthetized with CO2 and decapitated with a laboratory guillotine. Thereafter, the TG was removed and cleared of connective tissue in cold Hank&rsquo;s balanced salt solution. The ganglia were enzymatically dissociated in modified Earle&rsquo;s balanced salt solution containing 0.6 mg/ml collagenase (Roche Pharmaceuticals, Switzerland) and 0.4 mg/ml trypsin (Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ) for 40 min at 35&deg;C in a shaking water bath. The neurons were next dispersed by vigorous shaking and then centrifuged twice for 6 minutes at 130 X g. Finally, the cells were re-suspended in minimal essential medium (MEM), supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 1% glutamine and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (all from Invitrogen Corp., Carlsbad CA) and plated onto 35 mm poly-L-lysine coated dishes. The dissociated neurons were stored in a humidified incubator (5% CO2/95% air) at 37&ordm;C.

Electrophysiology and data analysis:

Ca2+ currents were recorded using the whole-cell patch-clamp technique. The patch pipettes were pulled from borosilicate glass (Garner Glass Co., Claremont, CA) and pulled on a P-97 micropipette puller (Sutter Instrument Co., Novato, CA). SPG whole-cell Ca2+ currents were acquired with the Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA), analog filtered at frequency 2 kHz (&ndash;3dB, 4-pole low-pass Bessel filter), digitized with custom-designed S5 software (Stephen R. Ikeda, NIH/NIAAA) equipped with an 18-bit AD converter board (Instrutech Corp. Elmont, NY). Cell membrane capacitance and pipette series resistance were electronically compensated (80-85%).

Solutions and drugs:

The external solution contained (mM): tetraethylammonium hydroxide (TEA-OH) 145, methanesulphonic acid 140, HEPES 10, glucose 15, CaCl2 2.5, TEACl 15 and TTX 0.0003, to pH 7.40 with TEA-OH. The pipette solution contained (mM): N-methyl-D-glucamine 110, TEA-OH 25, HCl 20, EGTA 11, HEPES 10, CsCl2 20, CaCl2 1, MgATP 4, Na2GTP 0.3 and Na-Creatine phosphate 7, to pH 7.20.

Stock solutions of [d-Ala2-N-Me-Phe4-Glycol5]-enkephalin (DAMGO) and &beta;-endorphin (&beta;-End) were prepared in H2O and diluted in the external solution to their final concentration prior to use.

DAMGO and &beta;-End concentration-response relationships:

The concentration-response relationships were determined by the sequential application of the &micro;-opioid receptor agonist in increasing concentrations. Two to three different concentrations were used with each cell in order to minimize desensitization. The results were then pooled and the concentration-response curves were fit to the Hill equation: I = IMAX/{1 + (IC50/[ligand])nH}, where I is the percentage inhibition, IMAX is the maximum inhibition of the Ca2+ current, IC50 is the half-inhibition concentration, [ligand] is the agonist concentration and nH is the Hill coefficient. Data and statistical analyses were performed with Igor Pro (Lake Oswego, OR) and Prism 4.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) software packages, respectively with P &lt; 0.05 considered statistically significant.

MICRODIALYSIS STUDY

Subjects

Male h/mOPRM1-118AA and h/mOPRM1-118GG mice (24-32g) were used for in vivo microdialysis.  All animals were group-housed (2-6 mice/cage, mixed genotypes) in a temperature-controlled vivarium (22&deg;C) with a 12h light/dark cycle (lights off at 06:00h AM) and were provided free access to food (PicoLab&reg; Mouse Diet 20) and water.  All procedures were conducted in strict adherence to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

In vivo Microdialysis

Experimentally na&iuml;ve male mice were each anesthetized with an isoflurane/ oxygen vapor mixture (1.5%) and mounted on a stereotaxic apparatus.  Microdialysis probes with 1mm active membrane length of cellulose and constructed as previously described 19 were aimed at the nucleus accumbens (NAc). The stereotaxic coordinates relative to Bregma were as follows: +1.5mm AP, &plusmn;0.8mm ML and -5.0mm DV from skull surface 18.  Probes were secured to the skull using a single skull screw anchor and a thin layer of Geristore&reg; resin ionomer cement (Den-Mat Corp., Santa Maria, CA, USA).  Following probe implantation each mouse was then transferred to a standard mouse cage and allowed to regain consciousness.

During a 12h postsurgical recovery period the microdialysis probes were perfused at 0.15&micro;l/min with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF; 149mM NaCl, 2.8mM KCl, 1.2mM CaCl2, 1.2mM MgCl2, 0.25mM Ascorbic acid and 5.4mM d-Glucose [pH7.2-7.4]).  After this period the perfusion flow rate was increased to 0.6&micro;L/min for 2h to reach an interstitial equilibrium before sample collection.  Dialysate fractions were subsequently collected at 10min intervals during a 60min baseline period and, after an injection of EtOH (2g/kg, ip), during a 120min post-injection period.  Samples were collected during the dark cycle, and were subsequently stored at -80&ordm;C until analysis for dopamine (DA) and serotonin (5-HT) content.  Proper placement of the active dialysis membrane within the NAc was verified histologically for each experimental subject.

DA and 5-HT Analysis

Dialysis samples were analyzed for DA and 5-HT using high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with electrochemical detection (HPLC-ECD) (model HTEC-500, from EiCOM Co., Kyoto, Japan).  Five microliter aliquots of dialysate were injected onto an analytical column (particle size 2 mm, PP-ODS 4.6 x 30 mm maintained at 25&ordm;C) and eluted using an isocratic mobile phase consisting of a 100mM NaH2PO4 buffer containing 134&micro;M 2Na-EDTA, 3.27mM SDS and 0.75% (v/v) methanol (pH 6.0) delivered at 400&micro;L/min.  Both monoamines were detected electrochemically using a graphite working electrode set at +400mV against a silver-silver chloride reference electrode.  Quantification was performed using external calibration curves that were constructed daily using freshly prepared standards.  All chemicals for the mobile phase and chromatographic standards were of the highest obtainable grade from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Statistical Analyses

Between-group differences in baseline dialysate DA and 5-HT concentrations were first compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA).  After confirmation of no group differences in baseline concentrations the data were converted to the percent change from the average baseline concentration obtained prior to pretreatment. Within- and between-groups analyses were performed by two-way ANOVA with repeated measures over time, with separate analyses performed for the DA and 5-HT data.
