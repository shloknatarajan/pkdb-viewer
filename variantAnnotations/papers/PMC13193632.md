## Abstract

**Background and Objectives:** 
Epilepsy, with its complex interplay of neurobiological, genetic, and pharmacological factors, continues to challenge neurologists worldwide—particularly in managing drug resistance. This study aimed to evaluate the frequency of the SCN1A and SCN2A gene polymorphisms among patients with epilepsy (PWE) from Eastern India to assess their relationship with clinical profiles, electroencephalography (EEG) findings, and response to antiseizure medication.

**Methods:** 
In this case–control genetic association study, a total of 110 PWE were assessed for demographic and clinical profiles, EEG and neuroimaging findings, and treatment response. SCN1A and SCN2A gene polymorphisms (five selected intronic single nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]—four in SCN1A [rs2298771, rs6730344, rs10167228, rs6732655] and one in SCN2A [rs17183814]) were assessed through polymerase chain reaction and restriction fragment length polymorphism.

**Results:** 
In the present study cohort, we observed that the generalized seizure type (73.6%) and early-onset seizures (mean 7.4 years in drug-resistant epilepsy [DRE]) were significantly more frequent in drug-resistant persons. The SCN2A rs17183814 AG genotype frequencies were 36/92 (39.1%) in DRE, 10/18 (55.6%) in responders, and 46/110 (41.8%) in controls. Statistical comparison showed no significant association between the AG genotype and drug resistance (DRE vs. responders: odds ratio [OR] 0.51, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.19–1.43, P = 0.303; DRE vs. controls: OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.51–1.57, P = 0.808).

**Conclusions:** 
On genetic analysis, the AG genotype of SCN2A rs17183814 was numerically higher among responders than DRE, but the difference was not significant. None of the SCN1A SNPs studied (rs2298771, rs6730344, rs10167228, rs6732655) showed significant associations with treatment response, consistent with previous Indian reports.

## Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common and disabling neurological disorders globally, affecting more than 50 million people across all age groups. In India, where the healthcare landscape is marked by both urban tertiary care centers and large underserved rural areas, the burden of epilepsy is profound—estimated at approximately 12 million individuals, or nearly one-sixth of the global epilepsy population.[[1](#R1)] Characterized by spontaneous and recurrent seizures resulting from abnormal synchronous neuronal activity, epilepsy encompasses a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, etiologies, and outcomes.

Despite the significant expansion in the arsenal of antiseizure medication (ASM) over the past few decades, approximately 30%–35% of individuals with epilepsy remain refractory to medical therapy.[[2](#R2)] This condition is termed drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) and is associated with increased morbidity, a diminished quality of life, neurocognitive decline, and elevated healthcare utilization. The pathogenesis of DRE is complex and multifactorial, encompassing pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, structural, and increasingly genetic determinants.

Among the molecular mechanisms contributing to ASM responsiveness, the role of voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) has attracted considerable attention. Variants in genes encoding the VGSC α-subunits—particularly *SCN1A* and *SCN2A*—have been implicated in both epilepsy susceptibility and variability in drug response.[[3](#R3)]

*SCN1A***,** located on chromosome 2q24.3, encodes the Nav1.1 α-subunit, which is preferentially expressed in inhibitory GABAergic interneurons. Dysfunction in *SCN1A*, particularly loss-of-function mutations, leads to disinhibition and hyperexcitability, as seen in genetic epilepsy syndromes such as Dravet syndrome and generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus.[[4](#R4)] Similarly, *SCN2A*, also located on 2q24.3, encodes Nav1.2, which is predominantly expressed in excitatory glutamatergic neurons during early development and plays a vital role in seizure susceptibility.

While pathogenic mutations in *SCN1A* and *SCN2A* have been implicated in severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, common intronic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in these genes have also been studied for their association with treatment response in more typical epilepsy populations.[[5](#R5)] Such SNPs may affect pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) splicing, transcription factor binding, mRNA stability, or even be in linkage disequilibrium with causative variants.

Several global studies have explored these associations, but findings have been inconsistent due to variations in ethnicity, allele frequency, sample size, epilepsy classification, and methodology.[[6](#R6),[7](#R7),[8](#R8)] The Indian population, with its distinct genetic diversity, represents a vital area for pharmacogenetic research. Yet, relatively few Indian studies have comprehensively examined the role of *SCN1A* and *SCN2A* polymorphisms in ASM responsiveness using well-characterized cohorts.[[9](#R9),[10](#R10),[11](#R11)]

This study was conceived to address this critical gap. We aimed to evaluate the frequency of five selected intronic SNPs—four in *SCN1A* (rs2298771, rs6730344, rs10167228, rs6732655) and one in *SCN2A* (rs17183814)—among patients with epilepsy (PWE) from Eastern India. We assessed their relationship with detailed clinical profiles, electroencephalography (EEG) findings, and response to ASM. The overarching goal was to identify potential pharmacogenetic biomarkers that could be used to stratify patients early in the disease course and optimize treatment outcomes through personalized medicine.

This study was therefore designed to study the genetic distribution by analyzing both *SCN1A* and *SCN2A* variants simultaneously in a clinically well-characterized epilepsy cohort with treatment outcome data.

## Methods

This was a case–control genetic association study conducted at the Department of Neurology, Bangur Institute of Neurosciences, Institute of Post Graduate Medical Education & Research (S.S.K.M. Hospital) (IPGMER), Kolkata, which serves as a tertiary referral center for neurological disorders in Eastern India. Patients were classified as per the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) 2021 criteria: drug-responsive epilepsy (seizure freedom ≥12 months after adequate ASM trials) versus DRE, defined as failure of adequate trials of two tolerated, appropriately chosen, and used ASM regimens.[[2](#R2)] The study was carried out over a 3-year period from January 2022 to January 2025. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee**,** and written informed consent was obtained from all study participants or their legal guardians in the case of minors.

### Sample collection

This study included consecutive PWE attending our epilepsy clinic during the stipulated study period, aged between 5–40 years. Both pediatric and adult patients within this age range were selected to find controls easily. A total of 110 PWE and 110 control subjects were included in this study. Inclusion criteria was based on diagnosis of epilepsy as per the 2017 ILAE classification (with ≥2 unprovoked seizures occurring >24 h apart), normal brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (to exclude structural causes of epilepsy), at least 12 months of follow-up under regular ASM, and availability of complete clinical, EEG, and treatment response records.

Age- and sex-matched healthy individuals of similar ethnicity and socio-economic background without any history of seizures or family history of epilepsy in first-degree relatives, or without any major psychiatric, neurological, or systemic disorders, were taken as the control population.

Patients with structural brain lesions (e.g., tumors, cortical dysplasia, mesial temporal sclerosis), with metabolic, infectious, or autoimmune etiologies, or co-existing intellectual disability, with hepatic or renal impairment affecting drug metabolism or neurodegenerative disorders, or with a history of significant ASM nonadherence (<80% documented compliance) were eventually excluded from the study.

Patients were classified into two groups based on the 2021 ILAE criteria: drug-responsive epilepsy, defined as seizure freedom for at least 12 months or for a period equal to three times the pre-treatment inter-seizure interval, whichever is longer, after adequate ASM trials, and DRE, defined as failure of adequate trials of two tolerated, appropriately chosen, and used ASM schedules (whether as monotherapies or in combination) to achieve sustained seizure freedom.[[2](#R2)]

A structured, pre-tested case record form was used to record the demographic and clinical profile (age at presentation, age at seizure onset, gender, educational level, socio-economic status, family history of epilepsy, etc.), seizure semiology (ILAE seizure type classification 2025: generalized seizure, focal seizure with preserved or impaired consciousness, focal to bilateral tonic-clonic, presence and description of auras, prodromal features, and postictal phenomena). All PWE underwent at least one routine interictal EEG (using the international 10–20 system); video-EEG monitoring was performed when clinically indicated. ASM history was elicited, including prescribed medications, dosage and duration, adverse effects, treatment adherence (pill counts, patient self-reports, follow-up documentation), and treatment response as per the above criteria.

For genetic analysis, 5 ml of venous blood was drawn from each subject using aseptic precautions and collected into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes. Samples were coded and stored at −20°C until further analysis. Genomic DNA was isolated using the phenol–chloroform isoamyl alcohol extraction method. The concentration and purity were evaluated using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0), and integrity was verified via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

Based on literature review and reported functional significance, five intronic SNPs were selected for analysis: *SCN1A*: rs2298771, rs6730344, rs10167228, rs6732655; *SCN2A*: rs17183814.

Each DNA sample was subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification using SNP-specific primers synthesized commercially. PCR was performed in a thermal cycler (Eppendorf Mastercycler) under optimized conditions (35 cycles, annealing temperatures 55°C–62°C depending on the primer). PCR products were then digested with restriction enzymes (PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism [RFLP])—a cost-effective, simple method suitable for moderate sample sizes—and fragments were separated on 4% agarose gel for genotype determination.

For RFLP, amplified products were digested with appropriate restriction enzymes [[Table 1](#aian_616_25-t001)]. Each reaction was incubated for 16–18 h at 37°C, and the digestion products were resolved on 4% agarose gel electrophoresis stained with ethidium bromide. Gels were visualized under a ultra-violet (UV) transilluminator and documented.

### Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using International Business Machines (IBM) owned Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics for Windows, version 24.0. Genotype frequencies were calculated for all SNPs and tested for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium using the Chi-square test. Comparative analysis between drug-resistant and drug-responsive groups was done using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Independent *t*-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the association of genotypes with drug resistance. A *P* value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

A total of 110 PWE were enrolled in the study and categorized into two groups based on treatment response: DRE, 92 patients (83.6%), and drug-responsive epilepsy, 18 patients (16.4%). Additionally, 110 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were included for genotypic comparisons.

### Demographic and baseline clinical characteristics

The demographic and baseline characteristics of patients are shown in [Table 2](#aian_616_25-t002) and [Figure 1](#aian_616_25-f001). The mean age of epilepsy patients was 19.04 ± 4.2 years. The male-to-female ratio was approximately 1.89:1, with 72 males and 38 females. The mean duration of epilepsy was 8.6 ± 3.7 years in DRE patients and 5.2 ± 2.4 years in drug-responsive patients, which was statistically significant (*P* = 0.01). A positive family history of epilepsy was documented in 26.4% of DRE patients compared to 11.1% of drug-responsive patients (*P* = 0.04). The mean age at seizure onset was 7.41 ± 3.2 years in the DRE group and 10.02 ± 2.9 years in the drug-responsive group. The difference was statistically significant (*P* = 0.008), suggesting that earlier onset may be a predictive factor for treatment resistance.

### Seizure semiology and treatment response

The seizure types were classified as per the ILAE 2025 classification. Generalized seizure was the most common, observed in 81 patients (73.6%), and among these, only 17 patients (21%) were drug-responsive. Focal seizure with impaired consciousness was the predominant seizure semiology in 10 patients (9.1%). Focal to bilateral tonic clonic seizures were found in 18 patients (19.6%), all drug resistant.

Generalized seizures were common overall; however, this was observed in 64/92 (69.6%) of DRE and 17/18 (94.4%) of responders. Because the responder group is small and group sizes are imbalanced (92 vs. 18), these percentage differences are unstable and should be interpreted with caution; no robust inference that generalized seizure predicts resistance can be drawn from these data. Focal seizure types were relatively more drug-responsive. Seizure frequency >2/month in 11.1% refers to historical pre-treatment frequency in responders; responders, as defined here, were seizure-free for ≥12 months at the time of assessment [[Table 2](#aian_616_25-t002)].

### ASM regimens and adverse effects

Monotherapy was effective in 15 out of 18 drug-responsive patients (83.3%). Polytherapy (≥2 ASMs) was required in 91.3% of DRE patients. Common ASMs used in our clinic were valproate, carbamazepine, levetiracetam, clobazam, lamotrigine, and lacosamide. Thirty-two patients (29.1%) reported side effects, which were more frequent in the polytherapy group. Common adverse effects were sedation, weight gain, tremor, and gastrointestinal symptoms.

### Genotypic analysis

The AG genotype frequencies of *SCN2A* rs17183814 (A>G) were 36/92 (39.1%) in DRE, 10/18 (55.6%) in drug-responsive participants, and 46/110 (41.8%) in controls. Statistical comparisons showed no significant association between the AG genotype and drug resistance: DRE vs. responders OR 0.51 (95% CI 0.19–1.43), *P* = 0.303; DRE vs. controls OR 0.89 (95% CI 0.51–1.57), *P* = 0.808. A three-group Chi-square test for AG vs. non-AG across DRE, responders, and controls gave *P* = 0.434. Therefore, the rs17183814 AG genotype was not significantly associated with ASM resistance in this cohort. All *SCN1A* SNPs examined did not show significant associations with treatment response (*P* > 0.05).

Additionally, the AA genotype was less common in DRE (28/92, 30.4%) compared to controls (34/110, 30.9%) and responders (6/18, 33.3%), while the GG genotype showed no significant variation across groups. Across all *SCN1A* SNPs, no statistically significant associations were found between genotype and drug response (*P* > 0.05). ORs suggested trends in both directions (e.g., protective and risk alleles), but none reached statistical significance.

### Gene-phenotype correlation

We conducted sub-analyses to explore phenotype–genotype associations. The rs17183814 AG genotype was observed more frequently in persons with generalised tonic-clonic seizure (GTCS), early age at onset (<8 years), behavioral prodrome, and interictal generalized spike discharges. These observations suggest possible clinical–genetic trends but did not reach statistical significance in this limited cohort. Although minor differences in genotype distribution were noted (AG 39.1% in DRE, 55.6% in responders, 41.8% in controls), the overall comparison was not significant (*P* = 0.303). Therefore, the rs17183814 AG genotype should be interpreted as an exploratory marker rather than a confirmed association with pharmacoresistant epilepsy. Larger studies are required to validate this potential genotype–phenotype relationship.

### Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and genotyping quality

All five SNPs were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (*P* > 0.05) in the control group. The genotyping error rate was 0% upon repeated testing of 10% of samples. Internal control samples confirmed consistent allele detection.

## Discussion

Epilepsy, with its complex interplay of neurobiological, genetic, and pharmacological factors, continues to challenge neurologists worldwide—particularly in managing drug resistance. In this genetic case–control study of Eastern Indian PWE, we explored the relationship between clinical features, treatment outcomes, and polymorphisms in the voltage-gated sodium channel genes *SCN1A* and *SCN2A*. Our findings reinforce prior insights while contributing regional evidence that supports the role of *SCN2A* in ASM responsiveness and provides updated data on *SCN1A* variants.

### High burden of drug resistance and clinical correlates

A striking 83.6% of patients in our cohort met criteria for DRE. This is notably higher than the global average (~30%) and exceeds the usual resistance rates (>36%) often observed in Indian clinic-based cohorts.[[8](#R8)] Several factors may account for this elevated burden. Our center is a tertiary referral institution, introducing referral bias toward more severe cases. Delayed presentation, prior suboptimal treatment, and irregular follow-up in underserved populations may contribute to refractoriness. In this study, inclusion of only patients with ≥1 year follow-up likely excluded early responders lost to follow-up.

Generalized seizures were the most common type overall. However, because the responder subgroup is small, the observed proportions (generalized seizure 69.6% in DRE vs. 94.4% in responders) are unstable; we therefore could not come to a conclusion from this dataset.

An earlier age at seizure onset (mean 7.4 years in DRE) was significantly associated with poor outcomes compared to responders (mean 10.0 years). This supports earlier findings linking early-onset epilepsy to developmental or genetic epileptic syndromes, many involving ion channel mutations.

### SCN2A rs17183814 polymorphism and drug resistance

*SCN2A* rs17183814: In our cohort, the AG genotype frequency was 36/92 (39.1%) in DRE, 10/18 (55.6%) in responders, and 46/110 (41.8%) in controls. Corrected statistical comparisons revealed no significant association between AG and drug resistance (DRE vs. responders OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.19–1.43, *P* = 0.303; DRE vs. controls OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.51–1.57, *P* = 0.808). Earlier versions of the manuscript contained inconsistent *P* values and ORs for rs17183814; those were transcription errors and have been corrected. Given the small number of responders and the imbalance between groups, these results are exploratory and require validation in larger, multicentric cohorts.

### SCN1A SNPs and nonsignificant associations

Despite being one of the most frequently implicated genes in epilepsy pharmacogenetics, none of the four *SCN1A* SNPs we studied—rs2298771, rs6730344, rs10167228, and rs6732655—showed a significant association with treatment response.

This aligns with Lakhan et al. (2009),[[10](#R10)] who found no statistically significant association between *SCN1A* rs2298771 and drug resistance, though they noted a higher frequency of the GG genotype in nonresponders. Our findings replicate their observations in a distinct Eastern Indian population.

Kumari et al. (2013)[[11](#R11)] evaluated a different *SCN1A* polymorphism—rs3812718 (IVS5-91G > A)—in 464 epilepsy patients and found that the AA genotype was associated with epilepsy susceptibility but not with treatment resistance. This distinction is important: susceptibility and drug responsiveness may be governed by different genetic mechanisms. Our study, while not including rs3812718, confirms that not all *SCN1A* intronic SNPs influence pharmacoresistance.

The absence of significant associations may be attributed to the noncoding location of these SNPs, their limited functional impact on splicing or protein translation, population differences in haplotype structures and linkage disequilibrium patterns, and a potential underpowered sample size to detect subtle associations.

### Integrating clinical and genetic data: toward precision epilepsy

Our study emphasizes the potential value of combining clinical indicators with genetic testing to predict treatment outcomes more effectively. Clinical features such as early onset, generalized seizure, EEG abnormalities, and behavioral prodromes are easily accessible markers. When combined with genotyping of *SCN2A* rs17183814, these may help clinicians to identify patients at higher risk of pharmacoresistance. For example, a child presenting with generalized seizure with Interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) and the AG genotype of *SCN2A* rs17183814 could be flagged for early treatment escalation, nonsodium ASM regimens, or pre-surgical evaluation. Conversely, the absence of these risk factors may guide the use of monotherapy, minimizing costs and adverse effects.

Incorporating such models into practice would require scalable and affordable genotyping platforms. Fortunately, the cost of PCR-based and SNP-focused assays is decreasing, making integration into clinical neurology plausible—particularly in resource-limited settings like India. Moving forward, larger, multicentric studies should validate these findings, explore gene–gene interactions, and build polygenic risk models incorporating *SCN1A*, *SCN2A*, and other candidate genes.

Our study confirms the clinical relevance of rs17183814, extends the findings to a new population, and is the first to assess five intronic SNPs in a rigorously phenotyped cohort from Eastern India. Unlike earlier studies, we included EEG data, polytherapy analysis, and seizure semiology correlation with genotypes.

### Limitations of the study

Additionally, the study has several limitations relevant to interpretation: (1) the sample is imbalanced (DRE n = 92; responders n = 18), which limits meaningful between-group comparisons and precluded reliable multivariable regression; (2) no correction for multiple testing (Bonferroni/False Discovery Rate [FDR] correction) was applied; (3) genotyping was performed using PCR–RFLP rather than sequencing; (4) treatment adherence was assessed by pill count and self-report, which may cause bias; (5) haplotype and gene–gene interactions were not analyzed; (6) the study is single-center, and findings require replication in larger, multicentric cohorts.

## Conclusions

In this case–control study from Eastern India, early age at seizure onset and generalized seizure type were more common among drug-resistant cases. No statistically significant association was found between *SCN2A* rs17183814 or any of the four *SCN1A* polymorphisms and ASM responsiveness. These findings indicate that the studied intronic sodium-channel variants are not reliable predictors of pharmacoresistance in this cohort. Larger multicentric studies are required to validate these observations and identify clinically meaningful pharmacogenetic markers.
