## Abstract

**Background and objective:** 
Tramadol is a widely used opioid, and pharmacogenetics is partially responsible for the variability in its response. The objective was to develop a tramadol population pharmacokinetic (popPK) model including pharmacogenetic information.

**Methods:** 
A single oral dose of 37.5 mg tramadol + 400 mg ibuprofen arginine was administered to 24 European healthy volunteers, and 18 blood samples were obtained from 0.25 h to 24 h after drug intake. Subjects were genotyped for the main pharmacogenes and a popPK model was built using NONMEM® (version 7.3). The role of CYP2D6, CYP2B6, and CYP3A4 phenotypes was analyzed. The final parameter estimates were compared with results obtained by noncompartmental analysis (NCA) in the same cohort. Simulations depending on CYP2D6 phenotype were performed in single dose and steady state conditions.

**Results:** 
A two-compartment model, with transit absorption into the depot compartment and first-order elimination, was the best fit to the data. In total, 8 volunteers were CYP2D6 intermediate metabolizers (IMs) and 14 were normal metabolizers (NMs), merged for the analysis with the two ultrarapid metabolizers (UMs), whereas no poor metabolizers (PMs) were present (European frequencies: 38.3%, 49.2%, 2.3%, and 6.5%, respectively). CYP2D6 phenotype affected clearance, which was 20% reduced in IMs compared with NMs + UMs. CYP2B6 (NMs = 11, IMs = 11, and PMs = 2) and CYP3A4 (NMs = 20, IMs = 3) phenotypes did not affect clearance. PopPK and NCA estimates were in close agreement. Simulations indicated a 20% and even 40% higher area under the curve after single dose and steady state conditions, respectively, in CYP2D6 IMs compared with NMs and UMs.

**Conclusions:** 
A popPK model including CYP2D6 phenotype well described the data. Further research with increased sample size is needed to analyze the clinical impact and effect of CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 phenotype on tramadol pharmacokinetics.

## Introduction

Tramadol is a synthetic minor opioid widely used in moderate-to-severe pain management [[1](#CR1)]. It acts as a nonselective agonist of the µ-opioid receptors and as an inhibitor of norepinephrine and serotonin reuptake, which lead to pain relief [[2](#CR2)]. It is metabolized by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6 isoform (CYP2D6) to its main active metabolite, *O*-desmethyl-tramadol [[3](#CR3)]. *O*-desmethyl-tramadol is the main metabolite responsible for the therapeutic effect of the drug, since it has higher affinity for the µ-opioid receptors compared with the parent compound [[4](#CR4)–[6](#CR6)]. However, tramadol also contributes to the therapeutic response, mainly through the inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake [[4](#CR4)–[6](#CR6)]. Tramadol is also metabolized to the inactive metabolite *N*-desmethyl-tramadol by other members of the CYP family, i.e., CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 [[3](#CR3), [4](#CR4), [6](#CR6)]. These compounds are further metabolized by different enzymes to other inactive products, and are renally excreted along with the parent drug (Fig. [1](#Fig1)) [[7](#CR7)].

Tramadol shows certain advantages over other opioids. In fact, a similar effectiveness with better safety profile than codeine was observed in other investigations [[8](#CR8)]. Furthermore, it constitutes a useful therapeutic alternative for those patients who experience adverse reactions (ADRs) when treated with nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs [[9](#CR9)]. However, one of its main drawbacks is the variability in response [[4](#CR4), [10](#CR10)], which can result in insufficient efficacy or the development of ADRs in certain patients.

Response is contingent upon tramadol and *O*-desmethyl-tramadol plasma concentration, which in turn depends on drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination [[6](#CR6)]. However, this relation is dose‐dependent and may vary considerably among patients [[6](#CR6)]. Consequently, understanding the variability in tramadol pharmacokinetics among individuals might be useful to explain and predict the associated variability in tramadol response.

Genetic variation in pharmacogenes, such as *CYP2D6*, is known to be partially responsible for this variability in tramadol pharmacokinetics [[6](#CR6), [10](#CR10), [11](#CR11)]. In 2012, the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) published a clinical guideline, updated in 2021, recommending that tramadol should be avoided in CYP2D6 ultrarapid and poor metabolizers (UMs and PMs, respectively) due to the risk of altered response [[12](#CR12)]. For CYP2D6 intermediate metabolizers (IMs), normal dosing and close monitoring were recommended [[12](#CR12)]. Although these recommendations are pertinent and should be applied in clinical practice, disposing of precise dose adjustments for each phenotype would be more beneficial. It would avoid limiting the therapeutic options for UMs and PMs and it would also reduce the time between drug prescription and finding the optimal dose in IMs. Moreover, response to tramadol is not solely conditioned by CYP2D6 phenotype. In fact, genetic variation in *CYP2B6* and *CYP3A4* might also alter tramadol pharmacokinetics and response, as observed in previous research [[6](#CR6)].

Thus, a better understanding on how genetic variation alters tramadol pharmacokinetics is highly desirable to reach a more precise and personalized drug prescription. Population pharmacokinetic (popPK) modeling constitutes a useful tool to understand the pharmacokinetic behaviour of a drug in a certain population and to identify the sources responsible for its variability [[13](#CR13)]. In addition, although previous tramadol popPK models are available in literature, none of them included *CYP2D6*, *CYP2B6*, and *CYP3A4* pharmacogenetic information [[14](#CR14)–[20](#CR20)]. Thus, the objective of this research was to develop a tramadol popPK model using data proceeding from healthy volunteers treated with this drug, and to analyze the impact of genetic variation on its pharmacokinetic variability.

## Materials and Methods

### Study Population

The study population was composed of 24 European healthy volunteers enrolled in a bioequivalence clinical trial with tramadol, conducted at the Clinical Trials Unit of the Hospital Universitario de La Princesa (UECHUP), Madrid, Spain (EUDRA‐CT: 2013‐000196‐32). An observational pharmacogenetic study with this population and the data from the noncompartmental analysis (NCA) data was already published [[21](#CR21)].

### Clinical Trial Design

The protocol and the informed consent form of the clinical trial and the pharmacogenetic study (code SFC-FG-2020-1, IRB/Code: 4176) followed all the regulatory requirements [[21](#CR21)–[23](#CR23)]. Volunteers were weighted and measured, and their body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Each volunteer self-reported their sex, age, and biogeographic origin.

The clinical trial was a phase I, open-label, randomized, replicated, and crossover bioequivalence clinical trial [[21](#CR21)]. For this research, only the pharmacokinetic data corresponding to the reference formulation, i.e., the coadministration of 400 mg Espidifen^®^ (ibuprofen arginine) granules for oral solution plus 37.5 mg of Adolonta^®^ (tramadol HCl) oral drops in solution, was considered. After intake of the reference formulation under fasting conditions, 18 blood samples for pharmacokinetic analysis were collected up to 24 h post-dose, in addition to a baseline sample obtained prior to drug intake (more detailed information on the clinical trial design and sample processing is available in the work of Saiz-Rodríguez et al. [[21](#CR21)]). The blinded measurement of tramadol plasma concentration was performed using a validated method of high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (further methodological details available in the Supplementary Material).

### Pharmacogenetics

Blood samples collected during the clinical trial were processed for DNA extraction using a Maxwell^®^ RSC Automated DNA extractor (Promega Biotech Iberica S.L, Madrid, Spain). Genotyping was performed by means of the customized array PriME-PGx Very Important Pharmacogene Open Array (VIPOA) on the basis of TaqMan^®^ probes, in a QuantStudio 12 k Flex real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) with an Open Array thermal block. A total of 180 genetic variants located in different pharmacogenes were analyzed (Supplementary Table [S1](#MOESM1)). Nucleotide variant annotation was performed complying with the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Reference Sequence (RefSeq) database [[24](#CR24)]. After literature revision, only the genes involved in tramadol metabolism were included in the exploratory analysis: *ABCB1*, *ABCC2*, *CYP2B6*, *CYP2D6*, *CYP3A4*, *CYP3A5*, *SLC22A1*, *UGT1A8*, and *UGT2B7* [[4](#CR4)] (Supplementary Table [S1](#MOESM1)).

For *CYP2D6*, *CYP2B6*, and *CYP3A4*, allele definition was conducted according to the CPIC and the Pharmacogene Variation Consortium (PharmVar) databases [[25](#CR25), [26](#CR26)]. Furthermore, the presence of *CYP2D6* gene duplications, deletions, and hybrid structures was investigated by performing a copy number variation (CNV) assay for both ends of the gene (5′ untranslated region and exon 9), as explained in Saiz-Rodríguez et al. [[21](#CR21)]. The pharmacogenetic inference of CYP2B6, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 phenotype was performed on the basis of their corresponding CPIC and Dutch Pharmacogenetic Working Group (DPWG) guidelines [[25](#CR25), [26](#CR26)]. The remaining genetic variants were analyzed individually.

### PopPK Analysis

The software used for popPK analysis included NONMEM^®^ (version 7.3), Pirana (version 24.9.2), PsN (version 5.0.0), Xpose (version 4.7.2), and R (version 4.4.2). The exploratory analysis and data preparation were performed in RStudio (version 2024.12.1+563) and IBM SPSS statistics (version 28.0.1.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

On the basis of the assumption of first order absorption, one-, two-, and three-compartment models with first-order elimination [[16](#CR16), [19](#CR19), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28)] were tested. After the selection of the number of compartments that best fit the data, the absorption model was retested. The following absorption models were considered: zero-order and first-order absorption, both with and without lag time; and transit absorption and inverse Gaussian function absorption, both into the depot and into the central compartment [[16](#CR16), [28](#CR28)–[30](#CR30)]. Additive, proportional, and combined strategies were tested to introduce the residual error in the model. Interindividual variability (IIV) was incorporated using exponential models. The existence of covariance between the IIVs was analyzed with the inclusion of full and partial OMEGA blocks. The estimation method used was first order conditional estimation method with interaction (FOCEi).

Models were sequentially and individually compared with the reference model in each step through the performance of log-likelihood ratio tests (LRT) between the differences in the objective function value (OFV). For model selection, significance was reached when the *p*-value was below the established significance level of *α* = 0.05. Furthermore, the Bayesian information criteria (BIC) of the models was calculated.

The impact of weight and pharmacogenetic phenotypes as covariates on the pharmacokinetics of tramadol was tested. Its analysis was performed by stepwise covariate modeling (SCM) [[31](#CR31)]. Significance was reached when the *p*-value was below the established significance level of *α* = 0.05 and *α* = 0.01 for the forward inclusion and the backward elimination step, respectively [[31](#CR31)]. The effect of weight on tramadol clearance (CL) and volume of the central compartment (Vc) was tested linearly and by allometric scaling, with fixed allometric exponents of 0.75 for CL and 1 for volume terms, respectively. The selection of the pharmacogenetic covariates to be analyzed was based on previous results in literature [[4](#CR4), [12](#CR12)], the level of evidence of its impact on protein activity [[26](#CR26), [32](#CR32)], the results of the exploratory analysis, and of the previous pharmacogenetic study performed with this cohort of volunteers [[21](#CR21)]. Up-to-date, genetic variation in most of the genes analyzed in this study, e.g., *SLC22A1*, *ABCB1*, or *UGT1A8*, remains poorly characterized. Their impact on protein activity is not well understood, the effects of carrying multiple variants simultaneously are unknown, no alleles have been defined, and phenotypic inference is unavailable. To ensure proper interpretation and avoid misleading conclusions, only genes with well-defined phenotypes, i.e., *CYP2B6*, *CYP2D6*, and *CYP3A4*, were evaluated as covariates for CL. For CYP2D6, normal metabolizers (NMs) and UMs were merged in a category (NMs + UMs (*n* = 14+2)) to be compared with IMs (*n* = 8), and for CYP2B6, the categories were NMs + IMs (*n* = 11+11) versus PMs (*n* = 2). For CYP3A4, only two phenotypes were available, i.e., NMs (*n* = 20) versus IMs (*n* = 3) (the phenotype for one volunteer was missing because of the presence of the SNV rs4986910, with unclear impact on enzymatic effect). The three covariates were incorporated into the model by means of the linear function for a bivariate categorical covariate, as specified in the SCM guideline from PsN^4^ [[31](#CR31)].

Model evaluation was conducted by analyzing the goodness-of-fit (GOF) plots, the visual predictive check (VPC) plots with 1000 simulations, and by performing a bootstrap analysis with 1000 runs. Moreover, the IIVs (represented as ETA) versus covariate and the normalized prediction distribution error (NPDE) plots were obtained. The empirical Bayesian estimates (EBEs) obtained, together with derived exposure metrics and pharmacokinetic parameters, such as the area under the concentration time curve (AUC), the maximum plasma concentration (*C*_max_), and the time to reach it (*t*_max_), or the half-life (*t*_1/2_), were compared with the estimates of the NCA, as explained in Cawello et al. [[21](#CR21), [33](#CR33)]. The 90% confidence intervals (CI) for the geometric means of the ratios popPK/NCA estimates were calculated. Furthermore, a simulation with *n* = 1000 model individuals (weight = 70 kg) with CYP2D6 NM+UM and IM phenotypes was performed, after a single 37.5 mg tramadol dose and in steady state conditions, with an 8 h dosing interval (specified in the dataset by II = 8 and SS = 1). The simulation included all IIVs but did not account for parameter uncertainty.

### NCA

The NCA of the exposure metrics and pharmacokinetic parameters was performed using the software WinNonLin Professional version 8.3 (Scientific Consulting, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) as described in the work of Saiz-Rodríguez et al. [[21](#CR21)] .

The median age of the participants was 24 years (interquartile ratio (IQR) 22–26.5 years), with a median height and weight of 1.72 m (IQR 1.67–1.77 m) and 70 kg (IQR 66.2–74.2 kg), respectively. The median BMI was 22.6 kg/m^2^ (IQR 21.8–25.5 kg/m^2^). The height and weight in women were significantly lower than in men (*p* < 0.001 for both comparisons) (Table [1](#Tab1)).

### PopPK Model

Assuming a first-order absorption [[16](#CR16), [19](#CR19), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28)], the two-compartment model was the one that best fit the data (change in the OFV (*Δ*OFV) = −105, change in the degrees of freedom (*Δ**df*) = 2), with no improvement observed when including an additional peripheral compartment, i.e., three-compartment model (*Δ*OFV = −105, *Δ**df* = 4), both with respect to the one-compartment model (Supplementary Table [S2](#MOESM1)). Concerning the error structures evaluated, the additive error component proved nonidentifiable, with estimates showing high uncertainty (RSE ~48%) or generating minimization and boundary problems. Fixing it to a negligible value (i.e., 0.01) or removing it had no impact on model fit or OFV but kept the model stable.

Once the number of compartments was selected, the absorption model was retested. Different absorption models were evaluated and compared in relation to the two-compartment model with first-order absorption without lag time, which was taken as the reference model for OFV comparison in this step. First-order absorption without lag time was better than zero-order absorption without lag time (*Δ*OFV = + 17, *Δ**df* = −1). For both kinds of absorption, the introduction of lag time (estimated in first-order absorption, fixed in zero-order absorption) improved the OFV significantly (*Δ*OFV = −479 and −265; *Δ**df* = 1 and 0, respectively) and the superiority of first-order absorption over zero-order absorption was maintained (Supplementary Table S2). However, the delay in absorption was better described by the mean transit time (MTT) and the mean input time (MIT) of the transit absorption and the inverse Gaussian function absorption models, respectively, than by the lag time. In these two absorption models, the improvement in the OFV was higher when the entry occurred into the depot compartment rather than into the central compartment (*Δ*OFV = −502 and = −501, *Δ**df* = 3, versus ΔOFV = −425 and −446, *Δ**df* = 1, respectively) (Supplementary Table [S2](#MOESM1)). The lowest OFV and BIC values were reached when a transit absorption into the depot compartment was included (ΔOFV = −502, Δ*df* = 3) (Supplementary Table S2). Therefore, a two-compartment model with transit absorption into the depot compartment was selected as structural model, with first-order elimination and including a proportional and an additive residual error fixed to 0.01 ng/mL (Fig. [2](#Fig2), Supplementary Figure [S1](#MOESM1)). The IIV was included following an exponential model on the CL, the Vc, the absorption constant rate (Ka), and the MTT, with covariance only between the IIV of CL and Vc (*Δ*OFV = −33, *Δ**df* = 0 compared with the two-compartment model with transit absorption into the depot compartment without covariance between IIVs) (Supplementary Table [S2](#MOESM1)). Correlations between IIVs were first introduced with a full block omega-structure, and the inspection of the correlations among the ETAs guided the development of a parsimonious omega-structure with covariance between CL and Vc.

The starting point for the SCM was the final structural model, which was used as reference model for OFV comparison. The allometric scaling of Vc and CL led to a significant improvement of the OFV (*Δ*OFV = −12, *Δ**df* = 0). However, the residual standard errors (RSEs) of some parameter estimates were extremely high (120% for Vc, 113% for Ka), arguing against this model. During the forward inclusion of the SCM, the linear effect of weight on the Vc and of the CYP2D6 phenotype on CL were included as covariates (Supplementary Fig. [S2](#MOESM1)). Both covariates were retained in the backward elimination step (improvement with respect to the selected structural model: ΔOFV= −18, *Δ**df* = 2). The linear effect of weight on CL was also included in the forward step, but was excluded in the backward elimination. None of the other pharmacogenetic covariates analyzed (i.e., CYP3A4 and CYP2B6) were observed to be significant, and therefore they were not included in the final model (Supplementary Fig. [S2](#MOESM1)). CYP2D6 IM phenotype was associated with a 19.8% reduction of tramadol CL, compared with being NM or UM. The inclusion of both covariates led to an improvement in the prediction of high concentrations in the GOF plots. In these plots (Fig. [3](#Fig3)A, B), individual or population predicted concentrations versus observed concentrations were low dispersed and adjusted to the identity line. Conditional weighted residuals (CWRES, Fig. [3](#Fig3)C, D) were homogeneously distributed around 0, with almost all values being inside the −2 to 2 range, and randomly distributed over time. The parameter estimates of the final model together with the results from the bootstrap analysis are summarized in Table [2](#Tab2). Due to the high number of samples available for each volunteer, the shrinkage of the IIV estimates was low (Table [2](#Tab2)).

### Model evaluation

The parameters estimated by this popPK model were in close agreement with the median bootstrap estimates, indicating high model accuracy (Table [2](#Tab2)). Furthermore, none of the 95% CI for the covariates included the value zero, thus proving the significance of their effects on the model (Table [2](#Tab2)). The stratified VPC and the NPDE plots demonstrated that the final model adequately predicted the data; only the distribution phase for CYP2D6 IMs was slightly underpredicted (Fig. [4](#Fig4), Supplementary Fig. [S3](#MOESM1)).

## Discussion

Interpreting tramadol metabolism is considered complex due to several factors, such as the existence of difference metabolizing pathways, the role of both tramadol and *O*-desmethyl-tramadol in drug effectiveness, and the genetic variation in the enzymes and transporters involved in its pharmacokinetics. In this context, popPK constitutes a valuable strategy for characterizing tramadol’s pharmacokinetic behaviour by accounting for IIV, including the impact of pharmacogenetic covariates.

In this research, a two-compartment model was selected as the best fit to the data, which is in accordance with other tramadol popPK models [[15](#CR15), [20](#CR20), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28)]. However, in other investigations, a one-compartment model was proposed [[14](#CR14), [16](#CR16), [19](#CR19), [30](#CR30)]. Differences in the number of compartments might be partially related to the fact that rich sampling data points were available, which facilitated the characterization of the distribution phase, compared with the data available in other research [[16](#CR16), [30](#CR30)]. The model of absorption that best described the data was a transit absorption model with entry into the depot compartment. Previous popPK models with tramadol showed different results concerning the absorption model [[14](#CR14)–[16](#CR16), [19](#CR19), [20](#CR20), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28), [30](#CR30)]. In most cases, a delay in absorption was included in the final model, by means of lag time or input time, which is concordant with our results [[14](#CR14), [16](#CR16), [19](#CR19), [28](#CR28)]. Specifically for oral drops, the lag time reported in the literature is 0.2 h, which is close to the 0.24 h of MTT observed in this work [[2](#CR2)]. In fact, similarly to the results observed in Brvar et al. [[28](#CR28)], inverse Gaussian function and transit absorption were almost identical in the present research. In addition, although the NMCT CI in the bootstrap analysis lacked precision, this did not compromise the validity of the absorption model selected. NMCT has no direct physiological meaning, and its value is less important than the overall fit of the absorption function, which depends on both NMCT and MTT. The selected model adequately captured the observed data, produced pharmacokinetic parameters consistent with NCA, and outperformed alternative absorption models, despite variability in early sampling times.

The estimated CL in this work (51.1 L/h) was inside the range reported in literature for oral tramadol (25–52 L/h) [[28](#CR28)], close to its superior limit probably due to the inclusion of healthy and young subjects, and it was higher than in other popPK models (25.1–34.7 L/h) [[19](#CR19), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28), [30](#CR30)]. Furthermore, tramadol was observed to be extensively distributed throughout the body (Vc and volume of the peripheral compartment of 126 L and 171 L, respectively), as it was expected due to its lipophilic nature. In fact, the reported volume of distribution (Vd) (297 L) was inside the range presented in previous investigations (260–324 L) [[2](#CR2), [16](#CR16), [28](#CR28), [34](#CR34)]. The *t*_1/2_ (4.5 h) and *t*_max_ (0.81 h) reported in this research were slightly inferior to the results presented in literature (5–6 h and 1.2–1.6 h, respectively) [[2](#CR2)]. Nonetheless, the geometric mean of the ratios popPK/NCA were found to be in close proximity to unity for all the exposure metrics and pharmacokinetic parameters, and the precision errors were below 10%. Therefore, it can be proposed that the deviations from the literature values are explained by the kind and design of the clinical trial rather than by model misspecification. Likewise, deviations do not seem to be caused by an interaction with the co-administered drug, i.e., ibuprofen, since no pharmacokinetic interaction between these two drugs has been described up to date, despite their common co-administration. In fact, the pharmacokinetics of tramadol remained within the bioequivalence range when combined with ibuprofen in previous research, and the metabolic routes of both drugs are different, which reduces the probability of metabolic interference [[35](#CR35), [36](#CR36)]. The IIV in CL (30.5%) and Vc (68.4%) were inside the expected ranges (8–43% and 15–104.5%, respectively) [[14](#CR14), [15](#CR15), [19](#CR19), [20](#CR20), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28), [30](#CR30)]. The IIV in Ka (108%) was over the reported range (42–70%) [[14](#CR14), [20](#CR20), [30](#CR30)], probably due to the great variability observed in the first sampling time-point among volunteers, which ranged from 0.85 to 153 ng/mL. Among the models tested, the selected model was the one providing the best fit to the data and the highest level of accuracy under the given conditions. However, a certain degree of mismatch between the model and the observed data was present. Although this mismatch may proceed from structural limitations, particularly in the absorption model, it should be noted that pharmacokinetic models are simplifications of complex biological systems, and the nature of the observed data may inherently limit the ability to achieve a perfect fit. Further concentration data collected before *C*_max_ would be required for a more comprehensive characterization of the absorption phase. However, the clinical implications of this mismatch are expected to be minimal, since the relation between pharmacokinetics and drug response is not primarily dependent on the absorption phase.

To our knowledge, the role of pharmacogenetics on tramadol pharmacokinetics has barely been studied in popPK models. In this research, the CL of tramadol was conditioned by CYP2D6 phenotype, being almost 20% lower in those individuals with reduced enzymatic activity. These differences were also reflected in other pharmacokinetic parameters, in the exposure metrics, and in the plasma concentration-time curve, which is concordant with previous knowledge [[12](#CR12)]. Notably, differences in CL were significant even though the most extreme phenotypes for this cytochrome were not present (i.e., there were no PMs, in which CL would be expected to be even lower, and UMs (*n* = 2) were merged with NMs). Moreover, the differences in the simulated exposure between IMs and NMs + UMs clearly increased in steady state conditions (40%) compared with the administration of a single dose (23%). Since the simulations accounted for IIV, between-subject variability is properly reflected. However, because parameter uncertainty was not included, the prediction intervals may underestimate overall uncertainty in exposure estimates and between-phenotype differences and should therefore be interpreted with caution. To our knowledge, the genotyping of *CYP2D6* was only performed in one tramadol popPK research with healthy Korean male subjects, where it was found to be a significant covariate [[19](#CR19)]. Furthermore, three tramadol popPK models addressed the role of CYP2D6 through mixture models [[16](#CR16), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28)], and only Salman et al. [[16](#CR16)] identified two populations (PMs and NMs). Lastly, one work analyzed the coadministration of tramadol with CYP2D6 inhibitors as a covariate [[20](#CR20)], and significance was not reached. Even though the results in the present research are consistent with the information on tramadol metabolism, the proposed model slightly underpredicted the observed concentrations during the distribution phase in IMs. Moreover, it should also be noted that the CYP2D6 phenotypic distribution in this cohort was unbalanced, as no PMs and only two UMs were available. As a consequence, NMs and UMs were grouped in the analysis to increase the statistical power and model stability. Therefore, these results have an exploratory character and further research with greater sample size and an adequate representation of each phenotype is required, ensuring that each phenotype is analyzed individually. In addition, due to the lack of data on efficacy and O-desmethyl-tramadol plasma concentration, further investigation assessing the clinical relevance of the observed differences is necessary.

CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 phenotypes were not retained in the present model. To our knowledge, no other tramadol popPK models in literature studied the role of genetic variation in these genes. Remarkably, in the previous pharmacogenetic study with this cohort and the NCA data, the CL in volunteers with a *CYP2B6* rs3745274 T/T genotype (i.e., PMs in this research) was not significantly different to CL in subjects with G/G or G/T genotypes (i.e., NMs and IMs, respectively) [[21](#CR21)], which is concordant with the results observed in this work. However, differences among these genotypes were observed in the AUC [[21](#CR21)]. The data transformation between CL and AUC might explain the discrepancy between the results observed for these two parameters [[21](#CR21)]. The lack of significance in CL in this research impeded its inclusion in the present model, and therefore, the analysis of its impact on drug exposure. On the contrary, no differences in tramadol pharmacokinetics were observed for the different CYP3A4 phenotypes. This lack of differences might be attributed to the reduced sample size available (there were no PMs, and only three IMs), but also to a less relevant role on tramadol metabolism. The impact of variation in the activity levels of CYP3A4 on tramadol pharmacokinetics and safety has been barely investigated, and contradictory results have been described [[6](#CR6), [20](#CR20)]. Thus, further research with increased sample size is required to address the impact of genetic variation in CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 on tramadol behavior.

Weight was included in the model as a covariate for Vc with a linear relationship, but not for CL. Conversely, previous tramadol popPK models proposed an allometric scaling of both parameters, discarded in the present model due to extreme high RSEs, which suggested model instability [[15](#CR15), [16](#CR16), [27](#CR27), [28](#CR28)]. Thus, the lack of relation between weight and CL in this research can be explained by the narrow weight range observed, which reduced the ability to inform weight–parameter relationships. To participate in the bioequivalence trial, the BMI of participants needed to be inside the 18.5–30 kg/m^2^ range. Therefore, weight differences among volunteers might not have been big enough to cause differences in CL. Moreover, CL might be conditioned by other factors, such as the hepatic or renal function, or genetic variation in pharmacogenes, which might play a more relevant role on this parameter than weight [[37](#CR37)]. Further, weight was responsible for differences in the Vc. Tramadol is a lipophilic drug; thus, it is highly distributed throughout the body, i.e., it shows an elevated Vd [[38](#CR38), [39](#CR39)]. It is known that the Vd is positively related to the body fat content, and the increase is higher in lipophilic drugs than in hydrophilic ones [[38](#CR38), [39](#CR39)]. Even inside the 18.5–30 kg/m^2^ BMI range, the percentage of body fat varies among healthy subjects, and this variation might be enough to alter tramadol distribution. These factors might explain why weight conditioned tramadol Vd but not its CL in this work. Since the differences in distribution did not cause variations in drug elimination in this population, it is not likely that they are clinically relevant. Moreover, further investigation could be of interest to elucidate whether the effect of body weight on tramadol Vd might impact other pharmacokinetic parameters or have clinical consequences in certain populations, such as in obese patients.

## Limitations

One of the main limitations of this research was the rather low sample size available, which led to a lack of enough representation of each phenotype and therefore to a diminished statistical power. This was especially important in the case of CYP2D6, since the lack of PMs and the presence of only two UMs might have conditioned the observed results. Furthermore, the lack of data concerning *O*-desmethyl-tramadol constituted an important limitation. Since this compound is the main responsible for the therapeutic effect, the clinical implications of the results presented here are limited. Additionally, disposing of data concerning the plasma concentrations of the parent compound and of both metabolites would have been of high interest. It would have facilitated a more thorough characterization of the role of each of the alternative routes in tramadol metabolism. Moreover, the poor structural and functional characterization of genetic variation in transporters prevented its inclusion as covariates. Lastly, disposing of data from a bioequivalence trial constituted a double-edged sword. On the one hand, the administration of a single dose and the inclusion of healthy subjects reduced population variability, did not allow an efficacy assessment, and impeded the inclusion of other covariates, such as illness status or administration route. Thus, these results may not generalize to patients, fed conditions, or chronic dosing, and further research is required for extrapolation. On the other hand, the confounding factors that alter pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and safety in clinical settings with patients, such as smoking, comorbidities, or concomitant treatment, were strictly controlled. Correct adherence to medication intake, uniform feeding conditions, and controlled and precise sampling were guaranteed. Moreover, since data proceeded from a bioequivalence trial, a high number of blood samples were available in each volunteer.

## Conclusions

Tramadol pharmacokinetics in European healthy volunteers is well described by a two-compartment model with transit absorption into the depot compartment. The phenotype of CYP2D6 alters drug CL, but further research with increased sample size and a balanced phenotype distribution is desirable. Moreover, further research analyzing the effect of CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 phenotype on tramadol pharmacokinetics is required.
